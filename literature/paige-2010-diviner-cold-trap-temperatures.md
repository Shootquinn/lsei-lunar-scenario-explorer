## Abstract

Orbital infrared radiometry of the lunar south polar region, mapped by the Diviner Lunar Radiometer Experiment on Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter during the approach to southern summer solstice in 2009, paired with a topographic thermal model that extends the observations in depth and in time. The observations establish that widespread surface and near-surface cryogenic regions exist and that they extend beyond the boundaries of persistent shadow. Measured mid-day bolometric brightness temperatures reach as low as 29 K, and the LCROSS impact site in Cabeus Crater is characterized with measured summer solstice daytime and nighttime surface values of 46.7 K and 38.7 K and a model-calculated annual average near 38 K at 2 cm depth. The paper reports modeled cold-trap areas for water ice, compares the near-surface hydrogen inventory implied by Lunar Prospector against the LCROSS site, and maps the thermal stability of water and of more and less volatile species against the recent history of the Moon's spin axis obliquity.

### Background and objective

The polar regions of the Moon are of interest because they can cryogenically trap water ice and other volatile species. Prior work on this question was largely modeling, and the objective here is to replace modeled polar temperatures with a measured surface-temperature map at a resolution fine enough to resolve individual crater floors, then to use a thermal model anchored to that map to extend the result to depths and to epochs the instrument cannot observe directly.

The immediate operational context is the Lunar Crater Observation and Sensing Satellite impact. Diviner mapped the south polar region just before the LCROSS impact, which allows the impact site to be placed within the measured thermal distribution of the region rather than treated in isolation. The companion questions the paper takes up are whether the polar cold traps could hold nearly pure water ice deposits of the kind radar has been used to argue for at Mercury, and what the distribution of temperatures implies for volatile species other than water.

### Methods and scope

Diviner has mapped infrared emission from the Moon since July 2009 using seven spectral channels spanning 7.55 to 400 micrometers at a spatial resolution of about 200 m (Paige et al., 2010, p. 479). The south polar maps reported here were acquired between 6 September and 3 October 2009 as the Moon approached southern summer solstice, during the LRO monthly mapping cycle just before the LCROSS impact. The mapped quantity is bolometric brightness temperature, meaning the wavelength-integrated radiance across all seven channels expressed as the temperature of an equivalent blackbody.

The interpretation of that quantity is qualified carefully in the source and the qualification matters for any downstream use. Where Diviner's surface footprint is filled by a uniform blackbody, the bolometric brightness temperature equals the surface temperature. Where the footprint contains sub-footprint-scale slopes, shadows, or rocks, the individual channel brightness temperatures vary with wavelength and the bolometric value cannot be read as a unique surface temperature. The authors show that within cold regions not in direct sunlight, simultaneously acquired brightness temperatures in channels 7 at 25 to 41 micrometers, 8 at 50 to 100 micrometers, and 9 at 100 to 400 micrometers agree well, and on that basis they treat bolometric brightness temperatures as reasonably accurate proxies for surface temperature in unilluminated regions only (Paige et al., 2010, p. 481).

The thermal model uses a triangular mesh at roughly 500 m scale built on south polar topography from the Kaguya LALT laser altimeter, with a spatially uniform set of thermal and reflectance parameters, and it accounts for direct and indirect solar and infrared radiation on the surface heat balance. Subsurface temperature gradients are estimated using heat flow parameters derived from the Apollo 15 and 17 experiments. Ice stability is computed as loss by sublimation and diffusive migration through the regolith.

### Key findings

Measured temperatures, meaning Diviner bolometric brightness temperatures at the surface, are the following. Each is a surface quantity and none of them applies at depth.

- The coldest regions are on the floors of larger impact craters that receive no direct sunlight. Topographic relief within those cold crater floors provides additional radiation shielding, producing intensely cold localized regions with measured mid-day bolometric brightness temperatures as low as 29 K (Paige et al., 2010, p. 481). This is the coldest measured value the paper prints.
- In the region surrounding the LCROSS impact site, Diviner-measured summer solstice daytime and nighttime surface bolometric brightness temperatures are 46.7 K and 38.7 K (Paige et al., 2010, p. 482).
- Shadowed portions of craters have measured daytime bolometric temperatures in the range 60 to 120 K, and this is the range over which the model and the data disagree (Paige et al., 2010, p. 481).
- Map epoch and viewing conditions for the measured maps are specific and should travel with any citation of them. Observations were acquired between 6 September and 3 October 2009 approaching southern summer solstice. Daytime values in Fig. 1A were acquired between 11.4 and 13.6 hours local time, and nighttime values in Fig. 1B between 21.41 and 1.66 hours local time. Instrument spatial resolution is about 200 m and the maps extend to 80 degrees south latitude at the outer circle.

Modeled temperatures and modeled areas are the following. The depth each one applies to is stated because the source states it and because the depths differ.

- The LCROSS impact site subsurface temperature of 38 K, which is the number the abstract leads with, is model-calculated and not measured. The body gives it as the model-calculated annual average temperature at 2 cm depth, close to 38 K (Paige et al., 2010, p. 482). The abstract phrases the same quantity as an estimate. The distinction between the 38 K modeled subsurface value and the 38.7 K measured nighttime surface value is easy to lose, because the two numbers are nearly equal and appear in the same paragraph.
- Model annual average temperatures at 2 cm depth are the quantity mapped in Fig. 1C and histogrammed in Fig. 2B.
- At the LCROSS impact site, temperatures at 2 m depth are estimated to be less than 6 K higher than annual average surface temperatures, using heat flow parameters from Apollo 15 and 17.
- Model skill is quantified rather than asserted. The only notable discrepancy is that in shadowed crater portions with measured daytime temperatures of 60 to 120 K, the model underestimates by roughly 15 K, which the authors attribute largely to directionally anisotropic infrared emission from rough sunlit crater walls that the model omits. The net effect on model-calculated annual average temperatures at 2 cm depth is less than 7 K in the warmest craters and close to zero in the coldest craters.
- Ice stability is mapped as the depth at which water ice would be lost at a rate of less than 1 kg per square meter per billion years, which the source states corresponds to a loss rate of 1 mm per billion years for a pure ice deposit. Fig. 1D maps that depth over the range 0.0 to 1.0 m, with white regions where water ice can currently be cold trapped on the surface, colored regions giving the upper surface of the lunar ice permafrost boundary, and gray regions where subsurface temperatures are too warm to permit cold-trapping of water ice within 1 m of the surface.
- Two cold-trap areas are given as estimates in note 17 rather than in the running text. The total area of surface water ice cold traps in the south polar region shown in Fig. 1D is 13,087 km2, and the total area of near-surface water ice cold traps to a depth of 1 m is 119,507 km2. Both are model-derived from Fig. 1D, both are stated as estimates, and neither carries an uncertainty band. The ratio between them is the paper's central structural point, since the near-surface trapping area is roughly an order of magnitude larger than the surface trapping area.
- Surface and near-surface water ice cold traps together comprise more than 66 percent of the surface area poleward of 85 degrees south latitude (Paige et al., 2010, p. 482).

The headline structural result is that cryogenic regions extend beyond persistent shadow. Surface cold traps for water ice are surrounded by much more extensive regions the authors call lunar permafrost, where water ice is stable in close proximity to the surface. These regions may receive direct solar radiation when lighting conditions are most favorable, yet maintain annual maximum temperatures at depth sufficiently cold to prevent appreciable water loss by sublimation. The authors state that because of their more hospitable surface thermal and illumination environments, lunar permafrost regions may be accessible locations for future in situ exploration of the Moon's cold traps. That is the paper's own framing of access conditions and not an inference added here.

The hydrogen inventory comparison sets a quantitative bound on the average deposit. Lunar Prospector Neutron Spectrometer results give an average hydrogen abundance in the near-surface regolith at the south pole of about 70 parts per million by weight, which translates to a water-equivalent average abundance of about 600 ppm by weight. If all of the hydrogen detected by that instrument were uniformly distributed within the cold traps mapped here, the average water-equivalent abundance would be only about 1000 ppm by weight, substantially less than the 1 to 10 percent water content inferred at the LCROSS impact site. The authors read this as showing that the LCROSS site must be enriched in water relative to the average south polar near-surface cold trap, consistent with enhanced hydrogen abundances observed in the Cabeus region by orbital neutron spectrometers.

Mobility and burial behavior are stated qualitatively rather than numerically. At the cold temperatures existing within most south polar craters, cold-trapped water molecules have negligible mobility, so water deposited on the surface will not effectively diffuse below the surface where it could be protected from loss processes such as photolysis and sputtering. In cryogenic regions, burial of frozen volatiles by impact gardening is likely to be the more effective process. Warmer permafrost regions at the margins of cold traps are described as somewhat more favorable environments for downward diffusion of water into the regolith, aided by the daytime gradient between warmer surface layers and colder subsurface layers.

Obliquity history supplies the paper's time axis. The Moon's spin pole is in a tidally damped Cassini State 2 configuration in which the time-averaged maximum angle between the spin axis and the normal to the ecliptic plane, written theta max, has decreased to a present value of 1.54 degrees as the Earth-Moon distance increased. The subsolar latitude on the Moon currently varies by approximately plus or minus 1.54 degrees over the Moon's 346-day draconic year. Model annual average near-surface temperatures were computed for theta max of 4, 8, 12, and 16 degrees in addition to the present-day case. Those runs show that portions of the south polar region cooled considerably as the orbital radius increased, first creating traps capable of holding water and then trapping compounds of higher volatility. When theta max was greater than about 10 degrees, the floors of large impact craters were not the coldest areas, because the walls of those relatively shallow craters did not shield their floors from direct solar radiation. The authors conclude that the Moon's earliest surviving near-surface cold traps are not on the floors of large impact craters but on the floors of intermediate-sized craters, which may therefore have had longer opportunities to accumulate water ice. The absolute time scale for this tidal evolution is called highly uncertain, with the depicted transition likely occurring over a period of more than 1 billion years.

Volatile species are treated by class, and the numbers behind that treatment are not printed anywhere in the text. The paper defines a volatility temperature as the temperature at which a pure solid exposed to vacuum at the surface would sublimate at a rate of 1 mm per billion years, and notes that non-water subsurface volatiles will be stable at higher temperatures than that owing to diffusive migration through the regolith. The running text states that large areas of the south polar region can cold-trap water and less volatile species such as mercury and sodium, and that colder surface and subsurface areas can additionally cold-trap super volatile species of higher volatility than water, naming sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, formaldehyde, ammonia, and methanol. All three of the first group were present in the LCROSS ejecta plume, and detection of a representative cross-section of the super volatile species in that plume is presented as strong evidence for impact delivery of volatiles to the Moon by primitive outer solar system bodies and their subsequent cold-trapping at the poles.

### Limitations

The instrument measures a surface radiometric quantity, and the source is explicit that this quantity is not always a surface temperature. Where the Diviner footprint contains sub-footprint-scale slopes, shadows, or rocks, the bolometric brightness temperature cannot be interpreted in terms of a unique surface temperature. The authors restrict the proxy interpretation to unilluminated regions, so measured values quoted for sunlit or partly sunlit terrain do not carry the same standing as those quoted for shadowed crater floors.

Every temperature below the surface in this paper is a model output, not a measurement. Diviner cannot see below the surface. The 38 K LCROSS subsurface figure, the annual average maps at 2 cm depth, the estimate that 2 m depth is less than 6 K warmer than the annual average surface, and both cold-trap areas are all model products conditioned on the model's assumptions.

The subsurface thermophysical properties the model depends on are stated to be poorly constrained. The authors write that the thermophysical properties of the Moon's cryogenic regolith are not currently well constrained and could differ substantially from those in warmer regions, particularly at depth. They expect cryogenic regions to extend to depths of at least tens of meters, and they say directly that estimating the volumetric extent of those regions purely from surface-temperature observations is highly uncertain. Any inference from this paper about the volume of cold material, as distinct from its surface area, inherits that uncertainty.

A known model bias is documented and is not corrected in the mapped products. In shadowed crater portions with measured daytime temperatures between 60 and 120 K, the model underestimates by roughly 15 K, attributed to anisotropic infrared emission from rough sunlit crater walls that the model does not represent. The authors bound the downstream effect at less than 7 K on annual average temperatures at 2 cm depth in the warmest craters and close to zero in the coldest, but the bias itself remains present in the daytime comparison.

The measured maps are a single seasonal snapshot rather than a seasonal survey. Data span 6 September to 3 October 2009 as the Moon approached southern summer solstice, which the authors describe as a snapshot extended in depth and in time by models. Seasonal extremes outside that window are modeled, not observed.

Cold-trap areas are stated without uncertainty. The values 13,087 km2 and 119,507 km2 are given to five significant figures in a note as estimates, with no error bar, no sensitivity to the assumed loss-rate threshold, and no statement of how they respond to the documented model bias. The precision of the printed digits should not be read as the accuracy of the estimate.

This paper measures temperature and models ice stability. It does not detect ice. Its cold-trap areas are areas where water ice would be thermally stable, which is a capacity for retention rather than an observed inventory, and the paper's own hydrogen comparison shows that filling that capacity uniformly with the hydrogen actually detected would yield only about 1000 ppm water-equivalent by weight.

The volatility temperatures underpinning the non-water volatile discussion are not reproducible from this document, since Fig. 2D plots them and no text, caption, axis label, or table prints them.
