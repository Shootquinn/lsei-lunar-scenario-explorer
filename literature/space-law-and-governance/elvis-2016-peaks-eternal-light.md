## Abstract

Examines whether the Outer Space Treaty's ban on national appropriation leaves room for de facto property claims on the Moon. Uses the Peaks of Eternal Light (PELs), near-permanently sunlit ridges and crater rims at the lunar poles occupying roughly one square kilometer of surface in total, as a worked example: a simple, near-term-feasible solar radio telescope placed on a PEL would require non-interference to function, and Article XII's precautionary language could be read to justify de facto exclusion of other parties, producing quasi-property status without any formal claim of sovereignty. The paper works through the treaty ambiguity, the ethical stakes (protection of scientific opportunity versus distributive justice), and proposes two non-binding conduct principles, proportionality and reasonable means, as a practical response. This is a policy, law, and ethics paper; the illumination geography of the PELs is reported as background evidence establishing scarcity, not as new remote-sensing science.

---

### Background and objective

The paper opens from a legal tension: Outer Space Treaty (OST) Article II bars national appropriation of the Moon by any means, but recent high-resolution lunar mapping (citing Crawford, 2015) shows lunar resources are highly inhomogeneous rather than uniformly distributed, so occupying one location can materially deprive others of a scarce resource. The authors identify the PELs as the sharpest available test case: a resource so geographically concentrated that a single state or company could occupy essentially all of it. The stated objective is to show, via a concrete and technologically near-term thought experiment, that occupation of a PEL could produce an unresolved property-like dispute under the existing treaty within years rather than decades, and to work out what ethical and regulatory response that implies.

### Methods and scope

The paper is a legal, ethical, and policy argument built around one technical thought experiment, not an empirical study. Its method is: (1) synthesize existing illumination-mapping literature (Bussey et al. 2005, 2010; Gläser et al. 2014; Noda et al. 2008) to establish how scarce PELs and permanently dark craters are; (2) construct a technically grounded thought experiment, a simple dipole solar radio telescope on a PEL, and argue for its near-term feasibility using contemporary lander programs (China's Chang'e 3/4, Japan's planned lander, Google Lunar X-Prize entrants, Moon Express, Shackleton Energy); (3) analyze the resulting situation against OST Articles II, IX, and XII; (4) run an ethical analysis weighing four candidate duties (extending human presence, extending scientific knowledge, planetary protection, distributive justice); (5) propose two conduct principles (proportionality, reasonable means) as a non-binding practical response, explicitly not a new treaty or legal instrument.

### Key findings

Quantitative illumination content (drawn from cited prior studies, not generated by this paper):

- PELs occupy about 1 square kilometer of lunar surface in total (abstract), described elsewhere in the text as roughly 1/100 of a billionth of the lunar area (p. 3).
- The Moon's spin-axis tilt to the ecliptic is 1.54 degrees, versus 23.5 degrees for Earth; this small tilt is what makes near-permanent polar illumination geometrically possible (p. 3).
- Permanently dark polar craters, by contrast, occupy thousands of square kilometers but still only about 0.1% of the total lunar surface (p. 2, citing Noda et al. 2008, Figure 1).
- North pole (Figure 2, Bussey et al. 2005): true "eternal light" regions exist, mapped within about 30 km (1-1.5 degrees) of the pole. Narrow ridge and crater-rim strips are illuminated at least 75% of the time; red-coded areas are 90-99% illuminated and white-coded areas are 100% illuminated.
- South pole (Figure 3, Bussey et al. 2010): no permanently (100%) illuminated region exists at the 500 m measurement scale used by Bussey et al. (2005). High-illumination regions (sunlit over 70% of the time) are each only a few hundred square meters, described as comparable in width to a narrow strip along a FIFA regulation soccer pitch (100-110 m by roughly 64-75 m). Four peaks at the south pole are identified as illuminated more than 80% of the time (Figure 3 caption).
- Gläser et al. (2014) found that elevating the viewpoint above the surface improves illumination fraction: even a 2 m rise makes a measurable difference, and a 10 m rise yields a 200 m by 500 m south-pole region with greater than 90% illumination.
- The proposed radio telescope would target wavelengths longer than about 10 m (below approximately 30 MHz), a band the terrestrial atmosphere is opaque to; a 100 m antenna at that wavelength gives directional resolution of about 6 degrees (about six times the Sun's angular diameter as seen from the Moon), which the authors argue would need to run along the length of one of the elongated PELs.

Legal and ethical findings: OST Article XII's openness/reciprocity language conflicts with an experiment's need for non-disturbance, so a precautionary claim could function as de facto appropriation without invoking sovereignty (Article I) directly (pp. 5-9). Article IX allows a state to request consultation over interference but the authors note no existing mechanism resolves such a dispute (p. 9). Of four candidate ethical grounds considered, the authors judge that a duty to extend human presence (weak, non-action-guiding) and planetary protection (already covered by existing law, not PEL-specific) carry less weight than protection of scientific opportunity and distributive justice, which they identify as the two ethical considerations that should shape any PEL-specific regulatory response (pp. 10-13). The paper's proposed practical response is two non-binding conduct principles: proportionality (impact commensurate with stated scientific objective) and reasonable means (minimizing exclusion of others and lasting environmental impact), intended as informal norms rather than treaty law (pp. 16-17). The authors also discuss licensing with renewal requirements and compensation mechanisms (financial payment, debt relief, or transferable access claims) as possible justice-oriented responses, without endorsing a specific scheme (p. 13).

### Limitations

The paper is explicitly a thought experiment and policy argument, not a technical or legal-determinative analysis; the authors state the OST's indeterminacy on this question could be resolved differently depending on background jurisprudential commitments (pp. 6-7). All illumination figures are drawn from secondary sources (Bussey et al. 2005, 2010; Gläser et al. 2014; Noda et al. 2008) rather than original measurement, and the south-pole figures are bounded by the 500 m resolution of the Bussey et al. (2005) analysis the authors cite. The proposed conduct principles (proportionality, reasonable means) are offered as non-binding practice guides, and the authors state they carry no legal force and that "reasonable" remains a contestable, context-dependent term (p. 17). The paper does not model or propose an enforcement mechanism for its own recommendations, and states that a durable international agreement on this point currently seems unlikely (p. 18).
