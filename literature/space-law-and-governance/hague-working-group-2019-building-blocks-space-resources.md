# Building Blocks for an International Framework on Space Resource Activities (The Hague Working Group, 2019)

## Citation

The Hague International Space Resources Governance Working Group. (2019). *Building blocks for the development of an international framework on space resource activities* (Revised text following the meeting of April 2019).

Publisher URL: none in source

## Abstract

Twenty-provision non-binding policy instrument drafted by a multi-stakeholder working group hosted at Leiden University. Sets out recommended elements — objective, definitions, scope, principles, priority rights, resource rights, safety zones, environmental/heritage protection, benefit-sharing, registration, liability, dispute settlement, and review — for a possible future international framework governing the search, recovery, and extraction of abiotic space resources. Presented as a groundwork document for international discussion, not as a proposed treaty text, and framed around the principle of incremental, adaptive governance.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The document is issued by The Hague International Space Resources Governance Working Group, described in the text's own footnote as an initiative promoting international cooperation and multi-stakeholder dialogue on space resource activities.

The introduction states the Building Blocks are intended "to lay the groundwork for international discussions on the potential development of an international framework, without prejudice to its form and structure," and that pending adoption of any such framework, "States, international organizations and non-governmental entities are encouraged to use the Building Blocks."

The introduction also notes a companion commentary on the Building Blocks was to be made available after a subsequent meeting referenced as occurring in November 2019, which places this revised text as the output of the Working Group's April 2019 meeting. The introduction closes by stating that the Working Group "hopes that its activities will complement other efforts at the national, regional and global level to address space resource activities."

The document does not print a separate publication date, DOI, volume/issue number, or page range beyond its own 7-page pagination; it identifies itself only by title and by the "revised" status following the April 2019 meeting, per the introduction and the running footer on each page.

Building Block 1 (Objective) states that the international framework "should create an enabling environment for space resource activities that takes into account all interests and benefits all countries and humankind" (1.1), and that to achieve this it should (1.2):

- identify and define the relationship of space resource activities to existing international space law, including the UN outer space treaties (a);
- propose recommendations for States to apply or develop domestic frameworks (b);
- propose recommendations for international organizations to apply or develop internal frameworks (c);
- promote identification of best practices by States, international organizations, and non-governmental entities (d).

### Methods and scope

The document is not an empirical study; it is a normative, provision-by-provision policy text. Each numbered Building Block states what "the international framework should" provide, encourage, or permit, using recommendatory rather than mandatory language throughout.

The introduction explains the Working Group's methodology as one of adaptive governance: it "considered it neither necessary nor feasible to attempt to comprehensively address space resource activities in the building blocks," proposing instead that such activities "be incrementally addressed at the appropriate time on the basis of contemporary technology and practices."

Scope is set explicitly in Building Block 3: the framework should address States and international organizations, and could provide for regulation of the conduct of States, international organizations, and non-governmental entities (3.1); and it should address space resource activities "within the solar system" (3.2).

Building Block 2 supplies the operative definitions on which the rest of the text relies:

- **2.1 Space resource** — "an extractable and/or recoverable abiotic resource in situ in outer space." A footnote clarifies this includes mineral and volatile materials, including water, but excludes satellite orbits, radio spectrum, and energy from the sun except when collected from unique and scarce locations.
- **2.2 Utilization of space resources** — "the recovery of space resources and the extraction of raw mineral or volatile materials therefrom." A footnote excludes secondary utilization: use of already-derived raw materials, and marketing/distribution of space resources.
- **2.3 Space resource activity** — an activity conducted in outer space to search for, recover, and extract raw mineral or volatile materials from space resources, including construction and operation of associated extraction, recovery, processing, and transportation systems.
- **2.4 Space object** — an object launched into outer space from Earth, including component parts and its launch vehicle and parts thereof.
- **2.5 Space-made product** — a product made in outer space wholly or partially from space resources. A footnote excludes raw mineral and volatile materials, including water, irrespective of form.
- **2.6 Operator** — a governmental, international, or non-governmental entity conducting space resource activities.

### Key findings

The Building Blocks proceed through 20 numbered provisions:

**1. Objective** — an enabling environment for space resource activities "that takes into account all interests and benefits all countries and humankind" (1.1), achieved by (1.2):
  a) identifying and defining the relationship of space resource activities to existing international space law, including the UN outer space treaties;
  b) proposing recommendations for States on domestic frameworks;
  c) proposing recommendations for international organizations on internal frameworks;
  d) promoting identification of best practices by States, international organizations, and non-governmental entities.

**2. Definition of key terms** — the six definitions (2.1–2.6) covering space resource, utilization of space resources, space resource activity, space object, space-made product, and operator, summarized under Methods and scope above, each with clarifying footnotes on inclusions/exclusions.

**3. Scope** (3.1–3.2) — the framework should address States and international organizations, could regulate the conduct of non-governmental entities, and should address space resource activities "within the solar system."

**4. Principles** — consistency with international law (4.1). Design aims (4.2 a–k): adhere to adaptive governance; promote compatibility/predictability of domestic and internal frameworks; contribute to sustainable development; prevent disputes; promote orderly and safe utilization; promote sustainable, rational, efficient and economic use of resources; promote sustainable technology; provide legal certainty for operators; take particular account of the needs of developing countries, of science, and of pioneer operators' contributions. Substantive commitments (4.3 a–e): space resources used exclusively for peaceful purposes; activities carried out for the benefit and interests of all countries and humankind regardless of development level; Article IX OST consultations where harmful interference may result; no harmful interference with other space activities, including other space resource activities; international cooperation conducted in accordance with international law.

**5. International responsibility** (a–c) — States bear international responsibility for national space resource activities, governmental or non-governmental, and for their conformity with the framework; non-governmental activities require prior authorization and continuing State supervision; where an international organization conducts activities, responsibility is borne jointly by the organization and its participating States.

**6. Jurisdiction and control over space-made products** — States exercise jurisdiction and control over space-made products used in space resource activities for which they are responsible.

**7. Access to space resources** — the priority-rights mechanism. The framework "should enable the attribution of priority rights to an operator to search and/or recover space resources for a maximum period of time and a maximum area upon registration in an international registry," with international recognition of such rights. Attribution, duration, and area are to be determined "on the basis of the specific circumstances of a proposed space resource activity" — case-by-case rather than by fixed formula. As printed, this is a time- and area-limited, registration-based exclusivity to search and/or recover, not a title to the resource or the area itself.

**8. Utilization of space resources** (8.1–8.2) — resource rights over raw mineral and volatile materials extracted from space resources, and products derived from them, "can lawfully be acquired," with mutual recognition of such rights between States; utilization must nonetheless be carried out in accordance with the non-appropriation principle of Article II OST. The two sub-provisions together separate acquirable rights in extracted/removed material from the prohibition on appropriating the celestial body or area itself.

**9. Due regard** — States and international organizations responsible for space resource activities shall give due regard to the corresponding interests of all countries and humankind.

**10. Avoidance and mitigation of harmful impacts** — "taking into account the current state of technology," responsible States/organizations should adopt measures aimed at avoiding and mitigating (a–i):
  a) risks to the safety of persons, the environment, or property;
  b) damage to persons, the environment, or property;
  c) adverse changes in the environment of the Earth, per planetary protection policies;
  d) harmful contamination of celestial bodies, per planetary protection policies;
  e) harmful contamination of outer space;
  f) harmful effects of the creation of space debris;
  g) harmful interference with other on-going space activities, including other space resource activities;
  h) changes to designated and internationally endorsed outer space natural or cultural heritage sites;
  i) adverse changes to designated and internationally endorsed outer space sites of scientific interest.

**11. Technical standards, prior review, and safety zones** — a prior review is required before proceeding with a space resource activity, to ascertain that it is carried out safely (11.1). The framework should encourage (11.2 a–c): procedures to avoid harmful impacts; a conformity-assessment methodology for equipment, procedures, and processes; and operator-driven standardization. The safety-zone mechanism (11.3): taking into account Article II OST non-appropriation, States/organizations may establish "a safety zone, or other area-based safety measure," around an area identified for a space resource activity, as necessary to assure safety and avoid harmful interference; such a measure "shall not impede the free access, in accordance with international law," of another operator's personnel, vehicles, and equipment; access may be restricted for a limited period given timely public notice stating reasons. International consultations are required in case of overlapping safety zones or conflicts with recognized freedom of access (11.4).

**12. Monitoring and redressing harmful impacts** (12.1–12.2) — responsible States/organizations shall ensure monitoring of harmful impacts; where a harmful impact occurs, or is reasonably expected to occur, they shall implement response measures and consider whether the activity should be adjusted or terminated — termed "adaptive management" in the text.

**13. Sharing of benefits** — benefit-sharing through promotion of participation in space resource activities by all countries, particularly developing countries (13.1), with an illustrative, non-exhaustive list of benefits (a–g):
  a) development of space science and technology and of its applications;
  b) development of relevant and appropriate capabilities in interested States;
  c) cooperation and contribution in education and training;
  d) access to and exchange of information;
  e) incentivization of joint ventures;
  f) exchange of expertise and technology among States on a mutually acceptable basis;
  g) establishment of an international fund.
  The framework "should not require compulsory monetary benefit-sharing" (13.2); operators "should be encouraged" — not required — "to provide for benefit-sharing" (13.3).

**14. Registration and sharing of information** — States/organizations should (a–f):
  a) register priority rights of an operator to search and/or recover space resources;
  b) give advance notification of space resource activities, including any area-based safety measure, through an international database;
  c) register space objects per the Registration Convention (REG), UNGA Resolution 1721 B (XVI), or Article XI OST, taking into account UNGA Resolution 62/101;
  d) notify frequency assignments for recording in the ITU Master International Frequency Register;
  e) provide, with regard to Article XI OST and operators' legitimate interests, information and best practices on authorization and supervision through an international database, including purposes/locations/orbital parameters/duration; nature, conduct, and locations of activities and associated logistics; results of activities; discovered phenomena endangering life or health, or indicating life; and harmful impacts with planned or implemented redress measures;
  f) notify termination of activities through the international database, with a statement on the condition of the area, including presence of space objects or space-made products.

**15. Assistance in distress** — applicability of Article V OST and the Rescue Agreement (ARRA) to persons involved in space resource activities.

**16. Liability** (16.1–16.2) — applicability of Articles VI and VII OST and the Liability Convention (LIAB) to damage resulting from space resource activities; the framework should encourage operator initiatives, individual or collective, to provide compensation for such damage.

**17. Visits** — applicability of Article XII OST, taking into account operators' legitimate interests.

**18. Institutional arrangements** — the framework should provide for (a–c):
  a) a publicly available international registry for registering operators' priority rights to search and/or recover space resources;
  b) an international database, in addition to the registry, publicly listing advance notifications of activities (including safety measures), information and best practices, and lists of designated heritage and scientific-interest sites;
  c) an international body or bodies responsible for considering/promoting best practices; listing heritage and scientific-interest sites; monitoring and reviewing implementation; and governing the registry, database, and other implementation mechanisms.

**19. Settlement of disputes** — the framework should encourage recourse by States, international organizations, and operators to amicable dispute resolution, for example by developing consultation procedures or promoting use of the 2011 Permanent Court of Arbitration Optional Rules for Arbitration of Disputes Relating to Outer Space Activities.

**20. Monitoring and review** — mechanisms should be developed for monitoring implementation, for example on the basis of State/organization reports, and for review and further development of the framework, consistent with the adaptive-governance principle.

### Limitations

As a non-binding recommendatory instrument, the document itself states no empirical basis, enforcement mechanism, or legal force; each provision is phrased as what a future framework "should" contain, not as adopted law.

The document explicitly declines comprehensiveness, stating the Working Group considered it "neither necessary nor feasible to attempt to comprehensively address space resource activities" at this stage — the Building Blocks are presented as a starting point for discussion rather than a complete regime.

No explicit publication date, ISBN, or document/version number appears on the pages extracted. The "revised... following the meeting of April 2019" status is drawn from the source filename and corroborated only indirectly by the introduction's reference to a companion commentary "available at: […TBC after meeting November 2019]," a reference left unresolved in the text itself, which places the document's composition sometime between the two meetings referenced.

The Working Group's full membership, mandate, and institutional affiliation beyond the footnoted Leiden University web address (identifying it as the "Hague Space Resources Governance Working Group") are not detailed in the body text; no author names or contributor list is printed.

Several key terms remain undefined despite the definitions section, including the substantive criteria by which "maximum period of time," "maximum area," or the "specific circumstances" governing priority-rights attribution (Building Block 7) are to be assessed — the document leaves these to future elaboration.
