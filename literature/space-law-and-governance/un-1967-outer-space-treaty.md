# Treaty on Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer
Space, Including the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies (1967 Outer Space Treaty)

## Citation

United Nations General Assembly. (1966). *Resolution 2222 (XXI). Treaty on Principles Governing
the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space, including the Moon and Other
Celestial Bodies.* Adopted 19 December 1966, 1499th plenary meeting. Annexed treaty text done in
triplicate at London, Moscow, and Washington, 27 January 1967. Cited in the text as Official
Records of the General Assembly, Twenty-first Session, agenda items 30, 89 and 91, document
A/6431.

Publisher URL: none in source

## Abstract

Seventeen-article multilateral treaty, annexed to UN General Assembly resolution 2222 (XXI) and
adopted 19 December 1966, stating foundational principles for state conduct in outer space.
Declares exploration and use the "province of all mankind," free of national appropriation (Arts.
I-II); subjects space activity to international law and the UN Charter (Art. III); bars orbital
and celestial-body weapons of mass destruction and reserves celestial bodies for peaceful purposes
(Art. IV); sets astronaut-rescue and mutual-assistance duties (Art. V); assigns state
responsibility and an authorization/supervision requirement for national activities, including
those of non-governmental entities (Art. VI); establishes launching-state liability (Art. VII) and
registry-based jurisdiction and control (Art. VIII); requires due regard, contamination avoidance,
and consultation (Art. IX); and sets cooperative-observation, information-sharing, and
facility-access rules (Arts. X-XII), followed by final clauses on scope, signature, ratification,
entry into force, amendment, withdrawal, and authentic texts (Arts. XIII-XVII).

## Summary

### Background and objective

The captured text is a UN General Assembly resolution together with its annexed treaty, as
published on a UNOOSA (United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs) web page. Resolution 2222
(XXI) was adopted at the 1499th plenary meeting on 19 December 1966. Its preamble records that the
Assembly had considered the 1966 report of the Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space
(COPUOS), including the fifth session of its Legal Subcommittee held at Geneva (12 July-4 August)
and New York (12-16 September); noted progress from subsequent consultations among member States;
and reaffirmed the importance of international cooperation and of developing "the rule of law" in
this new area of human endeavour. Operative paragraphs 1-3 commend the annexed Treaty, request the
Depositary Governments to open it for signature and ratification "at the earliest possible date,"
and express hope for its widest possible adherence. Operative paragraph 4 directs COPUOS to
continue elaborating separate agreements on liability for launching-caused damage and on
assistance to and return of astronauts and space vehicles, to begin studying the definition and
utilization of outer space (including implications for space communications), and to report
progress to the Assembly's twenty-second session. A closing note (Note 1) cites Official Records
of the General Assembly, Twenty-first Session, agenda items 30, 89 and 91, document A/6431.

The annexed Treaty's own preamble recites, in sequence: the "great prospects" opened by humanity's
entry into outer space; the common interest of all mankind in peaceful exploration and use; the
view that exploration and use should benefit all peoples irrespective of economic or scientific
development; a desire for broad international cooperation in both scientific and legal aspects of
the field; the view that such cooperation strengthens mutual understanding and friendly relations
between States and peoples; a recollection of resolution 1962 (XVIII), the "Declaration of Legal
Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space," adopted
unanimously on 13 December 1963; a recollection of resolution 1884 (XVIII), calling on States to
refrain from placing in orbit or installing on celestial bodies any objects carrying nuclear
weapons or other weapons of mass destruction, adopted unanimously on 17 October 1963; and an
invocation of resolution 110 (II) of 3 November 1947, condemning war-provoking propaganda, as
applicable to outer space. The preamble states the Parties' conviction that the Treaty "will
further the purposes and principles of the Charter of the United Nations."

### Methods and scope

As a legal instrument, the document carries no research methodology; its content is the resolution
text and the 17-article annexed Treaty, setting binding principles for state conduct in the
exploration and use of outer space, including the moon and other celestial bodies. The capture is
a plain-text rendering of a UNOOSA web page rather than a scan of the primary UN document, and it
carries non-treaty page furniture around the resolution and treaty text -- a search box, "About
Us," "Our Work," "Space4SDGs," event and document navigation labels, a "Links" block, and a Vienna
contact/postal footer with news items dated into 2026 -- disregarded here as site chrome. The
Treaty itself covers, article by article, the freedom and non-appropriation principles (Arts.
I-II); applicable law and the peaceful-purposes/demilitarization regime (Arts. III-IV); astronaut
status, rescue, and hazard notification (Art. V); state responsibility, authorization, and
liability (Arts. VI-VII); jurisdiction, registry, and return of space objects (Art. VIII);
environmental and interference safeguards (Art. IX); cooperative observation, information-sharing,
and facility access (Arts. X-XII); and final clauses on application, signature, ratification,
entry into force, amendment, withdrawal, and authentic texts (Arts. XIII-XVII).

### Key findings

- **Article I** provides that exploration and use of outer space, including the moon and other
  celestial bodies, "shall be carried out for the benefit and in the interests of all countries,"
  irrespective of their degree of economic or scientific development, and "shall be the province of
  all mankind." It further provides that outer space "shall be free for exploration and use by all
  States without discrimination of any kind, on a basis of equality and in accordance with
  international law," with free access to all areas of celestial bodies, and states there shall be
  freedom of scientific investigation, which States shall facilitate and encourage through
  international cooperation.
- **Article II**, in full, provides that outer space, including the moon and other celestial
  bodies, "is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or
  occupation, or by any other means." The article names three specific channels of appropriation
  (claim of sovereignty, use, occupation) and closes the list with the catch-all "or by any other
  means"; it states no exception and draws no distinction between governmental and non-governmental
  actors.
- **Article III** requires States Parties to carry on outer space activities "in accordance with
  international law, including the Charter of the United Nations," in the interest of maintaining
  international peace and security and of promoting international cooperation and understanding.
- **Article IV** sets two obligations. First, States Parties undertake not to place in earth orbit
  any object carrying nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction, not to install such
  weapons on celestial bodies, and not to station them in outer space "in any other manner."
  Second, the moon and other celestial bodies are reserved "exclusively for peaceful purposes":
  military bases, installations, fortifications, weapons testing, and military manoeuvres on
  celestial bodies are forbidden, though use of military personnel for scientific or other peaceful
  purposes, and use of equipment or facilities necessary for peaceful exploration, are not
  prohibited.
- **Article V** requires States Parties to regard astronauts as "envoys of mankind in outer space,"
  rendering them all possible assistance in the event of accident, distress, or emergency landing on
  another State Party's territory or the high seas, with prompt and safe return to the State of
  registry of their vehicle; requires astronauts of one State Party to assist those of other States
  Parties during outer space activities; and requires States Parties to immediately inform other
  States Parties, or the UN Secretary-General, of phenomena discovered in outer space that could
  endanger astronaut life or health.
- **Article VI** assigns States Parties "international responsibility for national activities in
  outer space," whether carried on by governmental agencies or non-governmental entities, and for
  ensuring such activities conform to the Treaty's provisions. Activities of non-governmental
  entities in outer space "require authorization and continuing supervision by the appropriate
  State Party." Where activities are carried on by an international organization, responsibility
  for compliance with the Treaty is borne jointly by the organization and by the States Parties
  participating in it.
- **Article VII** makes each State Party that launches or procures the launching of an object into
  outer space, and each State Party from whose territory or facility an object is launched,
  "internationally liable for damage" caused by the object or its component parts to another State
  Party or to that State's natural or juridical persons, whether the damage occurs on Earth, in air,
  or in outer space.
- **Article VIII** provides that a State Party on whose registry a launched object is carried
  retains jurisdiction and control over the object, and over any personnel aboard it, while in
  outer space or on a celestial body. Ownership of objects launched into outer space -- including
  objects landed or constructed on a celestial body -- and of their component parts is unaffected
  by their presence in space, on a celestial body, or by return to Earth. Objects or component parts
  found beyond the limits of the registering State Party are to be returned to it, which shall,
  upon request, furnish identifying data prior to return.
- **Article IX** directs States Parties to be guided by cooperation and mutual assistance and to
  conduct activities "with due regard to the corresponding interests of all other States Parties";
  requires States to pursue study and exploration "so as to avoid ... harmful contamination and
  also adverse changes in the environment of the Earth resulting from the introduction of
  extraterrestrial matter," adopting measures where necessary; requires a State Party that believes
  its own planned activity would cause "potentially harmful interference" with others' peaceful use
  of outer space to hold prior international consultations; and allows a State Party with the same
  concern about another State's planned activity to request consultation on it.
- **Article X** requires States Parties, to promote international cooperation, to consider on a
  basis of equality other States Parties' requests for an opportunity to observe the flight of space
  objects, with the nature and conditions of observation determined by agreement between the States
  concerned.
- **Article XI** requires States Parties conducting outer space activities to inform the UN
  Secretary-General, the public, and the international scientific community, "to the greatest
  extent feasible and practicable," of the nature, conduct, locations, and results of such
  activities, with the Secretary-General expected to disseminate the information promptly.
- **Article XII** requires that stations, installations, equipment, and space vehicles on the moon
  and other celestial bodies be open to representatives of other States Parties "on a basis of
  reciprocity," subject to reasonable advance notice so that consultations may be held and
  precautions taken for safety and against interference with normal operations.
- **Article XIII** applies the Treaty's provisions to States Parties' activities whether carried on
  by a single State, jointly with other States, or within the framework of international
  intergovernmental organizations, and directs that practical questions arising from such
  organizations' activities be resolved between States Parties and either the organization itself
  or one or more of its member States that are Parties to the Treaty.
- **Article XIV** sets the final-clause mechanics in six paragraphs: the Treaty is open to all
  States for signature, with accession available at any time to a State that has not signed before
  entry into force (para. 1); it is subject to ratification, with instruments of ratification and
  accession deposited with the Governments of the United Kingdom, the Union of Soviet Socialist
  Republics, and the United States, "hereby designated the Depositary Governments" (para. 2); it
  enters into force upon deposit of ratification instruments by five Governments including all
  three Depositaries (para. 3); for States ratifying or acceding afterward, the Treaty takes effect
  on the date of their own deposit (para. 4); the Depositary Governments are to promptly inform
  signatory and acceding States of signature dates, deposit dates, the entry-into-force date, and
  other notices (para. 5); and the Treaty is to be registered under Article 102 of the UN Charter
  (para. 6). The captured text does not itself state a calendar date on which entry into force
  occurred.
- **Article XV** allows any State Party to propose amendments to the Treaty; amendments enter into
  force for each accepting State Party upon acceptance by a majority of States Parties, and
  thereafter for each remaining State Party on the date of its own acceptance.
- **Article XVI** allows a State Party to give notice of withdrawal by written notification to the
  Depositary Governments, but only "one year after its entry into force" for that Party, with the
  withdrawal taking effect one year from the Depositary Governments' receipt of the notification.
- **Article XVII** provides that the Treaty's English, Russian, French, Spanish, and Chinese texts
  are equally authentic and are to be deposited in the archives of the Depositary Governments, with
  duly certified copies transmitted by them to the Governments of signatory and acceding States. The
  annex closes with "IN WITNESS WHEREOF the undersigned, duly authorized, have signed this Treaty,"
  followed by a "DONE" clause stating the Treaty was done in triplicate at the cities of London,
  Moscow, and Washington on "the twenty-seventh day of January, one thousand nine hundred and
  sixty-seven" (27 January 1967). Note 1, appended to the resolution text, cites Official Records of
  the General Assembly, Twenty-first Session, agenda items 30, 89 and 91, document A/6431.

### Limitations

The source is a plain-text capture of a UNOOSA web page rather than a scan or PDF of the primary UN
document, and no URL was captured with the text, so provenance cannot be verified beyond the
page's own content. Surrounding site furniture -- a search box; "About Us," "Our Work,"
"Space4SDGs," and event/document navigation labels; a "Links" block; a Vienna postal address and
phone/fax numbers; and a "Copyright (c)2026 UNOOSA, All Rights Reserved" footer, alongside news
items dated into 2026 -- confirms this is a live site render rather than an archival document
image; it is excluded from the summary above as non-treaty content. The resolution text states an
adoption date (19 December 1966), and the annexed Treaty's closing "DONE" clause states 27 January
1967 as a date and set of places (London, Moscow, Washington), but the text does not itself state a
date on which the Treaty opened for signature or entered into force: operative paragraph 2 of the
resolution only requests the Depositary Governments to open it for signature "at the earliest
possible date," and Article XIV(3) describes the entry-into-force mechanism (ratification by five
Governments including all three Depositaries) without a calendar date. No such dates are asserted
here beyond what the text prints. Consequently the source does not permit reporting which or how
many States have signed, ratified, or acceded, nor confirmation, from this document alone, of
whether or when the Treaty entered into force. Note 1's citation to document A/6431 is reproduced
as printed but was not independently checked. Resolution paragraph 4 references two companion
agreements then still under elaboration by COPUOS -- on launching-damage liability and on
astronaut assistance/return -- neither of which is included in this capture.
