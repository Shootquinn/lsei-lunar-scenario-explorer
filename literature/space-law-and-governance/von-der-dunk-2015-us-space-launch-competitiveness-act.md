# The US Space Launch Competitiveness Act of 2015 (von der Dunk commentary)

## Citation

von der Dunk, F. (2015, November 30). The US Space Launch Competitiveness Act of 2015. *Jurist
Academic Commentary*. [Archived as Space, Cyber, and Telecommunications Law Program Faculty
Publications, Paper 98, University of Nebraska College of Law].
https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/spacelaw/98

Publisher URL: https://www.jurist.org/commentary/2015/11/frans-vonderdunk-space-launch/

## Abstract

Short practitioner/academic commentary, published the week the US Commercial Space Launch
Competitiveness Act (H.R. 2262) was signed, walking through its four titles and concentrating on
Title IV (space resource exploitation). Sets out the statutory text on resource ownership (51 USC
§51303) and the disputed question of whether it is compatible with the non-appropriation principle
of Article II of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, then argues, on four grounds, that resource ownership
and commercial exploitation are not barred by that principle.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The piece was occasioned by President Obama's signing of H.R. 2262 on November 25, 2015, and was
published five days later as a guest commentary in Jurist (edited by Elizabeth Dennis), with a
parallel copy deposited in the University of Nebraska College of Law's Space, Cyber, and
Telecommunications Law faculty repository. The author, identified in the piece as Professor of Space
Law at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln, sets out to explain the Act's structure to a legal/policy
audience and to assess its most contested title against international space law.

### Methods and scope

The piece is a doctrinal legal commentary, not an empirical or data-driven study. It proceeds by
textual reading of the statute (all four titles, with detailed attention to Chapter 513 of 51 USC,
i.e. Title IV, sections 51301–51303) set against the 1967 Outer Space Treaty (Articles I, II, and
VI), the 1979 Moon Agreement, and the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea as later modified in
1994, with a reference to International Institute of Space Law (IISL) declarations on
non-appropriation.

### Key findings

- **Structure of the Act.** Title I (SPACE) amends the Commercial Space Launch Act: it revises the
  methodology for the third-party liability cap, extends third-party liability insurance and the
  mandatory cross-waiver of liability to spaceflight participants, bars licensees from flying
  "government astronauts" on informed consent alone, and directs government authorities to develop
  authorization/supervision approaches for new categories of private space activity and orbital
  traffic management, alongside provisions on ISS utilization, state-government involvement, and
  future government launch systems. Title II amends the Land Remote Sensing Policy Act, requiring
  the Secretary of Commerce to review existing remote-sensing licensing practice and assess whether
  the statutory regime needs updating. Title III renames the Office of Space Commercialization as
  the Office of Space Commerce and revises its stated purposes.
- **Title IV as the focal point.** The author calls Title IV, the Space Resource Exploration and
  Utilization Act, "the most innovative and disputed part of the Act" internationally. It has three
  parts: a short title, a saving clause affirming (from the US perspective) that the Act does not
  violate Article II of the Outer Space Treaty, and a substantive middle part of three sections.
- **Definitions and ownership grant.** Section 51301 defines "space resource" and "asteroid
  resource" (the latter a subset of the former). Section 51303 secures for US citizens, including
  commercial operators, entitlement to harvested space resources — the author notes the statute
  avoids the words "ownership" or "property" — conditioned on "applicable law, including the
  international obligations of the United States." He reads this as limiting the entitlement's reach
  to US jurisdiction (US territory and courts), since the US cannot bind other states or their
  nationals, and as invoking Article II of the Outer Space Treaty's bar on national appropriation
  "by means of use or occupation, or by any other means," which, per IISL declarations, is
  understood to also foreclose private appropriation.
- **The unresolved question.** Whether a ban on appropriating "space estate" also bars ownership of,
  or commercial exploitation of, resources extracted from it is described as unaddressed by the
  Outer Space Treaty's text and not conclusively settled in international law.
- **Arguments attributed to opponents of resource ownership.** The commentary summarizes three lines
  of reasoning used by those who equate the appropriation ban with a ban on resource exploitation:
  that celestial bodies as "province of all mankind" could only be commercially exploited under a
  developed international regime akin to "common heritage of mankind"; that Article II's broad
  wording bars any unilateral national authorization of such activity; and that the Treaty's
  "exploration and use" freedom does not extend to commercial use.
- **Author's position and supporting arguments.** The author states the "better view" is that the
  appropriation prohibition does not bar commercial exploitation, on four grounds: (1) outer space
  activity is governed by a baseline freedom limitable only by international consensus, with the
  Treaty's only operative constraint being Article VI's requirement of state authorization and
  continuing supervision of non-state actors — once met, private activity is lawful absent other
  prohibitions, paralleling the high seas and Antarctica; (2) "province of all mankind" is not
  equivalent to "common heritage of mankind" — the 1979 Moon Agreement attempted to impose a
  common-heritage-style regime (mandatory international administration, technology transfer, and
  benefit-sharing) on celestial bodies and failed because no major spacefaring state ratified it,
  which the author reads as evidence against treating "province of all mankind" as requiring a prior
  international regime, likening it instead to the high seas regime where appropriation is barred
  but resource use (e.g., fishing) is not; (3) the only body of international law where "common
  heritage of mankind" was actually operationalized, the deep seabed regime under the 1982 UN
  Convention on the Law of the Sea, required substantial modification in 1994 before major
  industrialized states would ratify it, and now permits commercial seabed exploitation under
  national licenses within a comparatively light international regime, offered as an analogy for
  what could exist in outer space under the Outer Space Treaty; (4) "use" has historically been read
  to include commercial use, citing commercial satellite communications since the early 1960s as
  precedent.
- **Section 51302 and the international dimension.** This section directs the US President to ensure
  authorization and continuing-supervision mechanisms are actually in place before commercial
  resource-harvesting activity begins, and to pursue development of an international regime for such
  activity, since the US cannot regulate other states or their operators. The author characterizes
  this as, in effect, an invitation to the international community to build a parallel international
  regime along similar lines.

### Limitations

The source is a four-page commentary rather than a peer-reviewed research article: it presents no
original data, no systematic comparative-law methodology, and no citation apparatus beyond inline
references to the statute, the Outer Space Treaty, the Moon Agreement, UNCLOS, and IISL
declarations. The author explicitly frames the resource-ownership question as legally unsettled and
presents his "better view" as one side of an ongoing debate rather than a settled conclusion. The
analysis is limited to the US statute and the existing multilateral treaty framework as of late
2015; it does not address subsequent state practice, other countries' space resource legislation, or
case law, none of which yet existed at the time of writing.

### Topic mapping

FA4. Source list entry 3. Sub-Q 3. Covers Titles I–III of H.R. 2262 (launch/reentry licensing and
liability provisions, remote-sensing licensing review, renaming and repurposing of the Office of
Space Commerce) at a summary level, and Title IV (Space Resource Exploration and Utilization Act,
51 USC Chapter 513, §§51301–51303) in detail, including the definitions of "space resource" and
"asteroid resource," the resource-ownership provision and its jurisdictional limits, and the
presidential duties regarding domestic authorization/supervision and international
regime-building. Also addresses Outer Space Treaty Articles I, II, and VI as applied to private
space resource extraction, and draws comparisons to the 1979 Moon Agreement's common-heritage
regime and the 1982/1994 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea deep-seabed regime.
