# U.S. Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act (Pub. L. 114-90, 2015)

## Citation

U.S. Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act, Pub. L. No. 114-90, 129 Stat. 704 (2015) (H.R.
2262, 114th Cong.).

Publisher URL: none in source

## Abstract

Public law, four titles, amending Title 51 of the United States Code (and related NASA
authorization provisions) to alter the regulatory and liability framework for U.S. commercial
space activity. Title I extends the commercial launch third-party liability indemnification
regime and the "learning period" restricting new crew/spaceflight-participant safety regulation,
both to dates in the 2023-2025 range, creates a new "government astronaut" category threaded
through Chapter 509's licensing definitions, and extends International Space Station operations
authorization through at least September 30, 2024. Title II adds annual and one-time reporting
requirements for commercial remote sensing licensing. Title III renames and expands the
functions of the Office of Space Commerce. Title IV creates a new Chapter 513 of Title 51
establishing that U.S. citizens engaged in commercial recovery of asteroid or space resources
are entitled to possess, own, transport, use, and sell resources obtained, while disclaiming any
assertion of U.S. sovereignty over celestial bodies. Enacted November 25, 2015.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The Act states its own purpose in the enacting clause: to facilitate a pro-growth environment
for the developing commercial space industry by encouraging private sector investment and
creating more stable and predictable regulatory conditions, and for other purposes. It
originated as H.R. 2262 in the 114th Congress, was reported by the House Committee on Science,
Space, and Technology (H. Rept. 114-119), passed the House on May 21, 2015, passed the Senate in
amended form on November 10, 2015, was concurred in by the House on November 16, 2015, and was
approved (signed) on November 25, 2015. Except where otherwise stated, amendments and repeals in
the Act are expressed as amendments to Title 51 of the United States Code. Title I is separately
short-titled the "Spurring Private Aerospace Competitiveness and Entrepreneurship Act of 2015,"
or "SPACE Act of 2015." Title IV is separately short-titled the "Space Resource Exploration and
Utilization Act of 2015."

### Methods and scope

The Act is organized into four titles amending existing statutory chapters of Title 51, U.S.
Code (and, for the ISS provisions, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration
Authorization Act of 2010, 42 U.S.C.). Title I, Spurring Private Aerospace Competitiveness and
Entrepreneurship (Secs. 101-117), amends Chapter 509 (commercial space launch licensing and
liability), Chapter 701 (renamed from Space Shuttle to Space Launch System use policy), and
related NASA-authorization provisions governing the ISS. Title II, Commercial Remote Sensing
(Secs. 201-202), amends Chapter 601, adding a new reporting section and directing a statutory-
update study. Title III, Office of Space Commerce (Secs. 301-302), amends Chapter 507, renaming
the office and enumerating its functions. Title IV, Space Resource Exploration and Utilization
(Secs. 401-403), adds a new Chapter 513 to Subtitle V of Title 51 and states a disclaimer of
extraterritorial sovereignty.

Each section generally pairs a "sense of Congress" or findings clause with amendatory language
(striking and inserting text in the U.S. Code) and, frequently, a directive for an executive-
branch report, study, or determination naming the responsible official, the parties consulted,
the report's recipients (most often the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and
Transportation and the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology), and a deadline,
ranging from 90 days to 1 year after enactment or a fixed calendar date through 2024. A number
of amendments are purely technical or conforming updates to tables of contents or tables of
chapters, noted below but not itemized individually.

### Key findings

**Title I, Secs. 102-103, launch liability and indemnification extensions.** Sec. 102 records
the sense of Congress that the "maximum probable loss" (MPL) methodology under 50914 should be
updated using a validated risk-profile approach; within 180 days the Secretary of
Transportation, consulting industry and insurers, must evaluate that methodology and submit any
update plan to the Senate Commerce and House Science committees, with a Comptroller General
independent assessment due 270 days after submission. It also amends 50915(f) to extend the
launch-liability risk-sharing (indemnification) regime, previously expiring December 31, 2016,
to September 30, 2025. Sec. 103 amends 50914(a)(4) and 50915(a) to add "space flight
participants" to the categories covered by third-party claims and by the Federal Government's
excess-claims payment provision; both additions cease to be effective September 30, 2025,
matching the Sec. 102 date.

**Title I, Secs. 104-105, experimental permits and a licensing-streamlining report.** Sec. 104
amends 50906 to extend experimental-permit eligibility to reusable launch vehicles on suborbital
trajectories, not solely suborbital rockets; removes a requirement that testing occur "prior to
obtaining a license"; and clarifies that issuance of a license does not invalidate an existing
permit. Sec. 105 requires the Secretary of Transportation, within 120 days, to report to the
Senate Commerce and House Science committees on approaches to streamline launch/reentry vehicle
licensing and permitting, covering efficiency, cost, inconsistency, duplication, and existing
infrastructure.

**Title I, Secs. 106-107, federal jurisdiction and cross waivers.** Sec. 106 adds 50914(g),
making federal courts the exclusive jurisdiction for third-party or space-flight-participant
claims for death, injury, or property loss from a licensed launch or reentry activity. Sec. 107
rewrites 50914(b)(1) to require every license to contain a reciprocal cross-waiver of claims
among "applicable parties" — contractors, subcontractors, and customers of the licensee, their
contractors/subcontractors, and space flight participants — with the space-flight-participant
clause again sunsetting September 30, 2025.

**Title I, Sec. 108, space authority.** Within 120 days, the OSTP Director, consulting State,
Transportation, NASA, other agency heads, and industry, must assess non-governmental commercial
space activities, identify appropriate authorization/supervision authorities, recommend an
approach prioritizing safety and treaty compliance, and report to the Senate Commerce and House
Science committees. The section does not apply to ISS National Laboratory activities under
section 504 of the NASA Authorization Act of 2010 (42 U.S.C. 18354).

**Title I, Secs. 109-110, orbital traffic management and situational-awareness data.** Sec. 109
records the sense of Congress that an improved space-traffic-management framework may be needed;
within 90 days NASA, consulting DOT, the FCC, Commerce, and DOD, must contract an independent
engineering organization to study alternate frameworks covering current regulations, agency
authorities, treaty compliance, federal assets, smallsat risk, and private information-sharing,
reporting to Congress within 1 year; a rule of construction preserves DOD's national-security
authority. Sec. 110 requires DOT, concurring with DOD, within 120 days, to study the feasibility
of releasing safety-related space situational awareness data and report to the Senate Commerce
and House Science committees.

**Title I, Sec. 111, consensus standards and the "learning period."** Restructures 50905(c):
directs the Secretary to keep working with industry, including the Commercial Space
Transportation Advisory Committee (COMSTAC), on voluntary consensus safety standards, and
clarifies the Secretary may discuss regulatory approaches with industry pre-rulemaking without
that being read as authorizing regulation beyond the section. Sets a reporting sequence: interim
consensus-standards reports not later than December 31, 2016 and every 30 months through
December 31, 2021; a key-metrics readiness report within 270 days of enactment; further
transition-plan reports by March 31, 2018 and March 31, 2022; and an independent readiness
assessment by December 31, 2022. The operative "learning period" provides that, beginning
October 1, 2023, the Secretary may propose regulations under the subsection without regard to
subparagraphs (C) and (D) of paragraph (2), taking the evolving standards from the prior reports
into account.

**Title I, Sec. 112, government astronauts.** Defines a new category, "government astronaut"
(50902(4)): an individual designated by NASA under new section 20113(n), carried within a
licensed launch or reentry vehicle in the course of employment, who is either a U.S. government
employee (including uniformed services) performing a federal function, or an "international
partner astronaut" designated under Article 11 of the ISS Intergovernmental Agreement (signed
Washington, January 29, 1998; TIAS 12927). Ties the new NASA designation authority to the
Commercial Crew Development Program authorized under section 402 of the NASA Authorization Act
of 2010 (124 Stat. 2820; Pub. L. 111-267). Threads "government astronaut" through Chapter 509's
definitions of launch, launch services, reenter/reentry, reentry services, and third party,
redefines "space flight participant" as an individual who is not crew or a government astronaut,
and amends the license-application, monitoring, suspension, and government-for-government
nonapplication provisions (50904, 50905, 50907, 50908, 50919) accordingly.

**Title I, Sec. 113, streamlining commercial launch requirements.** Records the sense of
Congress that eliminating duplicative federal requirements aids the commercial sector. Directs
the Secretary of Transportation, consulting DOD and NASA, to identify and resolve outmoded or
duplicative launch/reentry licensing requirements, and to report to the Senate Commerce
Committee, House Science Committee, and congressional defense committees within 180 days of
enactment and annually thereafter until no such duplication remains, describing the application
process, current interagency coordination, and legislative recommendations.

**Title I, Sec. 114, ISS operation and utilization.** Amends the NASA Authorization Act of 2010
to extend ISS operations authorization from "through at least 2020" to "through at least 2024"
(section 501, 42 U.S.C. 18351), and the parallel dates in section 503 (42 U.S.C. 18353,
maintenance of the U.S. segment) and section 504(d) (42 U.S.C. 18354(d), research capacity
allocation) from September 30, 2020 to September 30, 2024. Rewrites 51 U.S.C. 70907, retitled
"Maintaining use through at least 2024," directing the NASA Administrator to keep the ISS viable
and productive as a national laboratory for exploration, partnership, and all NASA mission
directorates' research use through that date.

**Title I, Secs. 115-116, state launch facilities and space support vehicles.** Sec. 115 records
the sense of Congress that state-owned launch facilities support commercial growth but carry
liability risk, and directs a Comptroller General report, within 1 year, to the Senate Commerce
and House Science committees on including state and municipal property within the existing 50914
indemnification regime. Sec. 116 directs a parallel 1-year Comptroller General report on the use
of space support vehicle services in the commercial industry, covering launch-provider reliance,
statutory/regulatory/market barriers, and recommendations.

**Title I, Sec. 117, Space Launch System update.** Renames Chapter 701 from "Space Shuttle" to
"Space Launch System" throughout. Rewrites 70102 to permit SLS use for missions extending human
presence beyond low-Earth orbit that substantially benefit from its unique capabilities, other
missions substantially benefiting from those capabilities, space-available federal/educational
payloads consistent with NASA's beyond-LEO exploration mission, and "compelling circumstances"
as determined by the Administrator, with written notification to the Senate Commerce and House
Science committees within 30 days of such a determination. Authorizes agreements with foreign
entities for SLS-based international science/technology payloads and adds a definitions section
(70104) cross-referencing section 302 of the NASA Authorization Act of 2010 (42 U.S.C. 18322).

**Title II, Sec. 201, annual remote-sensing licensing reports.** Adds 51 U.S.C. 60126, requiring
the Secretary to report to the Senate Commerce and House Science committees not later than 180
days after enactment, and annually thereafter, on implementation of section 60121 — lists of
applications received, licensed, denied (with reasons), requiring additional information, or
exceeding the 120-day deadline under 60121(c) (with days overdue and an explanation) — plus
notifications under section 60122 and actions taken under 60123(a)(4)-(6). The report may
include classified annexes; the reporting requirement sunsets effective September 30, 2020.

**Title II, Sec. 202, statutory update report.** Within 1 year, the Secretary of Commerce,
consulting other agency heads and NOAA's Advisory Committee on Commercial Remote Sensing, must
report to the Senate Commerce and House Science committees on statutory updates needed to
license private remote sensing systems, balancing national security against U.S. private-sector
leadership and reflecting the current state of the art.

**Title III, Secs. 301-302, Office of Space Commerce.** Sec. 301 renames Chapter 507 and its
defining provisions from the "Office of Space Commercialization" to the "Office of Space
Commerce" (chapter heading, table of chapters, sections 50701 and 50702(a)). Sec. 302 rewrites
50702(c) to enumerate the office's functions: fostering economic growth and technological
advancement of the U.S. space commerce industry; coordinating space commerce policy within
Commerce; representing Commerce in policy development and foreign-country negotiations;
promoting U.S. geospatial technologies related to space commerce; and supporting federal Space-
Based Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PNT) policy work, including the National Coordination
Office for Space-Based PNT.

**Title IV, Sec. 402, Chapter 513 definitions and the commercial-recovery framework.** Adds
Chapter 513 to Subtitle V of Title 51. New section 51301 defines "asteroid resource" as a space
resource found on or within a single asteroid; "space resource" as an abiotic resource in situ
in outer space, including water and minerals; and "United States citizen" by cross-reference to
section 50902. New section 51302 directs the President, acting through appropriate federal
agencies, to facilitate commercial exploration for and recovery of space resources by U.S.
citizens, discourage government barriers to that industry, and promote U.S. citizens' right to
engage in it free from harmful interference, consistent with U.S. international obligations and
subject to federal authorization and continuing supervision; a presidential report to Congress
specifying the necessary authorities and agency-responsibility recommendations is due within 180
days of enactment.

**Title IV, Sec. 402 (Sec. 51303) and Sec. 403, resource rights and the sovereignty
disclaimer.** New section 51303 ("Asteroid resource and space resource rights") provides that a
U.S. citizen engaged in commercial recovery of an asteroid resource or space resource under the
chapter "shall be entitled to any asteroid resource or space resource obtained," including to
possess, own, transport, use, and sell it, in accordance with applicable law, including U.S.
international obligations. Sec. 403 ("Disclaimer of Extraterritorial Sovereignty") separately
states it is the sense of Congress that enactment of the Act does not assert U.S. sovereignty,
sovereign or exclusive rights, jurisdiction, or ownership over any celestial body.

### Limitations

- As a statute, the document contains no empirical data, methodology, or evaluative findings of
  its own; this summary reports only what the text provides, not the Act's effects,
  implementation status, or subsequent interpretation.
- Numerous provisions direct future executive-branch studies, reports, and determinations (for
  example Secs. 102, 105, 108, 109, 110, 113, 115, 116, 202, and 51302(b)) whose contents and
  outcomes are not part of this document and are not addressed here.
- Purely technical or conforming amendments (table-of-contents and table-of-chapters updates,
  terminology substitutions such as "Commercialization" to "Commerce" or "space shuttle" to
  "space launch system") are summarized only briefly above rather than itemized line by line.
- The legislative-history block identifies House Report No. 114-119 and floor-action dates in
  the Congressional Record but does not include committee-report substance, floor-debate
  content, or subsequent agency implementing regulations, none of which are available from this
  source.
- Several substantive terms used repeatedly in the amendatory text (for example "applicable
  law," "harmful interference," and the scope of "international obligations of the United
  States" invoked in Secs. 51302 and 51303) are not further defined within the Act itself.
