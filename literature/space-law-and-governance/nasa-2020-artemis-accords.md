# The Artemis Accords (2020)

## Citation

*The Artemis Accords: Principles for cooperation in the civil exploration and use of the Moon, Mars, comets, and asteroids for peaceful purposes.* (2020, October 13). Multilateral political commitment signed at the 71st International Astronautical Congress by Australia, Canada, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, the United Arab Emirates, the United Kingdom, and the United States; original text maintained by the Government of the United States of America.

Publisher URL: none in source

## Abstract

Founding text of the Artemis Accords, a set of thirteen sections of principles, guidelines, and best practices for civil exploration and use of the Moon, Mars, comets, and asteroids, adopted October 13, 2020. Framed in the preamble and Section 1 as a political commitment rather than a treaty, intended to operationalize obligations under the Outer Space Treaty and related instruments. Covers peaceful purposes, transparency, interoperability, emergency assistance, registration of space objects, release of scientific data, preservation of outer space heritage, space resources, deconfliction of space activities (including "safety zones"), orbital debris, and final/administrative provisions. Signed on the stated date by eight states: Australia, Canada, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, the United Arab Emirates, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The preamble states that the Signatories act recognizing their "mutual interest in the exploration and use of outer space for peaceful purposes," noting the value of existing bilateral space cooperation agreements, and citing the more-than-50-year interval since the Apollo 11 Moon landing and the more-than-20-year continuous human presence aboard the International Space Station. It describes the Artemis program as intending to "land the first woman and next man on the surface of the Moon" and, with international and commercial partners, to establish sustainable human exploration of the solar system, building on the legacy of Apollo. The preamble affirms compliance with four prior instruments by name and date: the Outer Space Treaty (opened for signature January 27, 1967), the Rescue and Return Agreement (April 22, 1968), the Liability Convention (March 29, 1972), and the Registration Convention (January 14, 1975), as well as the value of coordination through the UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS). The stated objective is to "implement the provisions of the Outer Space Treaty and other relevant international instruments" and thereby "establish a political understanding regarding mutually beneficial practices" for future exploration and use of outer space, with a stated focus on activities supporting the Artemis Program.

Section 1 (Purpose and Scope) restates this objective: to establish "a common vision via a practical set of principles, guidelines, and best practices" to enhance governance of civil exploration and use of outer space, intended to increase safety of operations, reduce uncertainty, and promote sustainable and beneficial use of space. The text states directly that "the Accords represent a political commitment to the principles described herein, many of which provide for operational implementation of important obligations contained in the Outer Space Treaty and other instruments."

### Methods and scope

The document is organized as a preamble followed by thirteen numbered sections, several of which are further subdivided into numbered paragraphs and, in Section 11, lettered sub-points:

1. Purpose and Scope
2. Implementation
3. Peaceful Purposes
4. Transparency
5. Interoperability
6. Emergency Assistance
7. Registration of Space Objects
8. Release of Scientific Data
9. Preserving Outer Space Heritage
10. Space Resources
11. Deconfliction of Space Activities
12. Orbital Debris
13. Final Provisions

The text is followed by signature pages for each original Signatory. As printed, the Accords apply to "civil space activities conducted by the civil space agencies of each Signatory," on the Moon, Mars, comets, and asteroids — including their surfaces and subsurfaces — as well as in orbit of the Moon or Mars, at Earth-Moon Lagrangian points, and in transit between these bodies and locations. Section 2 specifies that Signatories intend to implement the Accords' principles through their own activities via measures such as mission planning and contractual mechanisms with entities acting on their behalf, and that cooperative activities may be carried out through Memoranda of Understanding, Implementing Arrangements under existing Government-to-Government Agreements, Agency-to-Agency arrangements, or other instruments referencing the Accords. Section 2 also specifies that such instruments are expected to address liability, intellectual property, and transfer of goods and technical data; that cooperative activities should follow each Signatory's applicable legal obligations; and that each Signatory commits to ensuring that entities acting on its behalf comply with the Accords' principles.

Section 13.2 records that the Government of the United States of America maintains the original text and transmits a copy to the UN Secretary-General "with a view to its circulation to all the members of the Organization as an official document of the United Nations," while stating that this copy "is not eligible for registration under Article 102 of the Charter of the United Nations." Section 13.3 provides that, after October 13, 2020, any State seeking to become a Signatory may submit its signature to the U.S. Government for addition to the text, and records that the Accords were "Adopted on October 13, 2020, in the English language."

### Key findings

**Section 3 (Peaceful Purposes):** Signatories affirm that cooperative activities under the Accords should be "exclusively for peaceful purposes and in accordance with relevant international law."

**Section 4 (Transparency):** Signatories commit to transparency in broadly disseminating information on their national space policies and exploration plans, consistent with their own national rules and regulations, and to sharing resulting scientific information with the public and international scientific community on a good-faith basis, consistent with Article XI of the Outer Space Treaty.

**Section 5 (Interoperability):** Signatories recognize that interoperable and common exploration infrastructure and standards — including fuel storage and delivery systems, landing structures, communications systems, and power systems — will enhance exploration, scientific discovery, and commercial utilization. They commit to use reasonable efforts to apply current interoperability standards, to establish new standards where none exist or are inadequate, and to follow such standards.

**Section 6 (Emergency Assistance):** Signatories commit to reasonable efforts to render necessary assistance to personnel in outer space who are in distress, and acknowledge their obligations under the Rescue and Return Agreement.

**Section 7 (Registration of Space Objects):** For cooperative activities under the Accords, Signatories commit to determining which of them should register a relevant space object under the Registration Convention, and to consult with any non-Party to that Convention involved in an activity to determine an appropriate means of registration.

**Section 8 (Release of Scientific Data):** Signatories retain the right to communicate information about their own activities, but intend to coordinate in advance on releasing information relating to other Signatories' activities to protect proprietary or export-controlled information. They commit to open sharing of scientific data, intending to make results from cooperative activities available to the public and international scientific community "in a timely manner." The commitment to open data sharing is stated not to apply to private-sector operations unless conducted on behalf of a Signatory.

**Section 9 (Preserving Outer Space Heritage):** Signatories intend to preserve "outer space heritage," defined to comprise "historically significant human or robotic landing sites, artifacts, spacecraft, and other evidence of activity on celestial bodies," in accordance with mutually developed standards and practices, and intend to apply their experience to multilateral efforts to further develop international heritage-preservation practices and rules.

**Section 10 (Space Resources):** Signatories note that utilizing space resources can benefit humankind by supporting safe and sustainable operations. They state that extraction and utilization of space resources — including recovery from the surface or subsurface of the Moon, Mars, comets, or asteroids — should comply with the Outer Space Treaty and support safe and sustainable activities. The text states directly that "the extraction of space resources does not inherently constitute national appropriation under Article II of the Outer Space Treaty," and that related contracts and legal instruments should be consistent with that Treaty. Signatories commit to informing the UN Secretary-General, the public, and the international scientific community of their space resource extraction activities in accordance with the Outer Space Treaty, and to contributing their experience to multilateral efforts on resource extraction and utilization rules, including ongoing work at COPUOS.

**Section 11 (Deconfliction of Space Activities):** The longest section, with eleven numbered paragraphs. Signatories reaffirm their Outer Space Treaty commitments on due regard and harmful interference, and affirm that exploration and use should give due consideration to the 2019 UN Guidelines for the Long-term Sustainability of Outer Space Activities adopted by COPUOS, adapted to operations beyond low-Earth orbit. Consistent with Article IX of the Outer Space Treaty, an authorizing Signatory commits to respect the principle of due regard, and a Signatory with reason to believe it may suffer or has suffered harmful interference may request consultations with the authorizing Signatory or any other Outer Space Treaty Party. Signatories commit to seek to refrain from intentional actions causing harmful interference, and to provide each other necessary information on the location and nature of activities where harmful interference or a safety hazard is believed possible. Signatories intend to contribute their experience to multilateral development of practices, criteria, and rules on safety zones and harmful interference.

The section defines a "safety zone" as the area in which notification and coordination will occur "to avoid harmful interference," sized to the area in which nominal operations or an anomalous event could reasonably cause harmful interference. Stated principles for safety zones include: (a) size and scope should reflect the nature and environment of the operations; (b) size and scope should be determined reasonably, using commonly accepted scientific and engineering principles; (c) safety zones are expected to change over time with the status of the operation and are "ultimately temporary, ending when the relevant operation ceases"; and (d) Signatories should promptly notify each other and the UN Secretary-General of a safety zone's establishment, alteration, or end, consistent with Article XI of the Outer Space Treaty. A Signatory maintaining a safety zone commits, on request, to provide the basis for the area in accordance with applicable national rules. Establishment, maintenance, or ending of a safety zone should protect public and private personnel, equipment, and operations from harmful interference, with relevant information made available to the public as practicable, subject to protections for proprietary and export-controlled information. Signatories commit to respect reasonable safety zones, including prior notification and coordination before operating within one, and to use safety zones in a manner that encourages scientific discovery, technology demonstration, and safe and efficient resource extraction, while respecting "the principle of free access to all areas of celestial bodies and all other provisions of the Outer Space Treaty," adjusting their use of safety zones over time through mutual experience and consultation.

**Section 12 (Orbital Debris):** Signatories commit to planning for orbital debris mitigation, including safe, timely, and efficient passivation and disposal of spacecraft at end of mission, as part of mission planning; for cooperative missions, plans should specify which Signatory holds primary responsibility for end-of-mission planning and implementation. Signatories commit to limiting, to the extent practicable, generation of new long-lived harmful debris from normal operations, break-up in operational or post-mission phases, and accidents or conjunctions, through measures such as safe flight profiles, operational configurations, and post-mission disposal of space structures.

**Section 13 (Final Provisions):** Signatories commit to periodic consultation, building on existing consultative mechanisms where applicable, to review implementation and exchange views on future cooperation. The U.S. Government's depository role and the non-registration statement under UN Charter Article 102 are set out here (see Methods and scope, above), as is the open accession mechanism for States joining after October 13, 2020.

### Limitations

As printed, the Accords describe themselves as a "political commitment" rather than a treaty, and the text's own final provisions record that the U.S.-transmitted copy sent to the UN Secretary-General "is not eligible for registration under Article 102 of the Charter of the United Nations." The principles are stated to apply to "civil space activities conducted by the civil space agencies of each Signatory," and several provisions (e.g., Section 8.3 on release of scientific data) explicitly exclude private-sector operations unless conducted on behalf of a Signatory. The text does not itself specify numerical or geographic parameters for safety zones, leaving size and scope to be "determined in a reasonable manner" by the operating Signatory; nor does it establish a binding dispute-resolution mechanism beyond consultation and requests for consultation under Section 11.3. Obligations recur as commitments to "intend," "commit to use reasonable efforts," or "should," rather than as uniformly mandatory terms. The document states it was adopted "in the English language," with the U.S. Government maintaining the original text.
