## Abstract

Diviner Lunar Radiometer observations and thermophysical modeling of lunar collapse pits and the caves they may host. The Mare Tranquillitatis and Mare Ingenii pits show elevated nighttime thermal emission about 100 K warmer than the surrounding surface. Modeling shows regolith-covered pit floors near the equator can reach peak daytime temperatures above 420 K, while shadowed regions beyond the pit opening hold a nearly constant 290 K, behaving like a blackbody cavity in radiative equilibrium. A cave extending from such a pit would maintain this near-constant 290 K along its length. Diviner readily detects pit thermal signatures but is insensitive to caves, which would raise the overlying surface nighttime temperature by only about 0.1 K.

### Background and objective

- Collapse pits form when the ceiling of a subsurface void (a lava tube or cave) collapses. There are 16 confirmed lunar collapse pit features potentially stemming from lava tube networks, with diameters of 15 to 150 m, spanning latitudes about -36 to 45 degrees, mostly in the maria; several have verified overhangs.
- Evidence for intact caves is limited but suggestive: GRAIL gravity data and SELENE Lunar Radar Sounder data indicate the Marius Hills pit could be part of a lava tube system extending tens of kilometers.
- The objective is to characterize the thermal (and illumination) environment inside pits and potential caves, both to interpret Diviner observations and to assess the sites for exploration and habitation.

### Methods and scope

- Diviner has continuously measured lunar surface temperature for over 11 years; the study uses its nighttime thermal-emission measurements of prominent pits (Mare Tranquillitatis, Mare Ingenii).
- Computational thermophysical models connect the observed pit thermal signatures to the temperature environment on pit floors and inside hypothetical caves, treating a deep cave far from the opening as a blackbody cavity in radiative equilibrium.

### Key findings

- The studied pits emit about 100 K more thermal radiation at night than the surrounding surface, and models keep pit floors above 230 K through the night.
- Near the equator, regolith-covered pit floors can reach peak daytime temperatures above 420 K, while regions in permanent shadow beyond the pit opening hold a nearly constant 290 K.
- A cave stemming from such a pit would behave like a blackbody cavity at about 290 K (roughly 17 degrees C), with temperature varying by less than about 1 degree C over an entire lunar day and remaining nearly invariable far from the opening.
- This makes lunar caves a temperate, stable, and comparatively safe thermal environment relative to the extreme swings on the open surface, favorable for long-term exploration and habitation.
- Thermal infrared instruments like Diviner can detect a pit's thermal signature but cannot confirm a connected cave, since a cave would raise the overlying surface nighttime temperature by only about 0.1 K, below practical detectability.

### Limitations

- The existence of intact caves connected to the pits cannot be confirmed by these remote thermal observations; the cave environment is modeled from pit data and radiative-cavity assumptions, not measured directly.
- Detailed results center on a small number of prominent pits (chiefly the Mare Tranquillitatis pit), and cave geometry and extent remain unknown.
- The 290 K cave temperature follows from radiative-equilibrium blackbody-cavity modeling; real caves may depart from that idealization depending on geometry, regolith properties, and connection to the surface.

### Topic mapping

- The Key Points and Abstract state the 100 K nighttime pit excess, the greater-than-230 K modeled floor, the 290 K cavity temperature, and the 0.1 K cave-detectability limit. The introduction reviews lunar collapse pits (16 confirmed, 15 to 150 m, maria) and the GRAIL and LRS evidence for the Marius Hills lava tube. The models-and-observations sections develop the Diviner measurements and the thermophysical cave model, and the discussion frames the habitation and exploration implications of the stable cave thermal environment.
