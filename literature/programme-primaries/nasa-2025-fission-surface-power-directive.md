## Abstract

A three-page memorandum from NASA's Acting Administrator directing the agency to accelerate Fission Surface Power (FSP) development for lunar and Mars applications. States the policy rationale (competitiveness with China/Russia lunar reactor plans, national security, lunar-night survival, and high-power ISRU needs), summarizes prior FSP investment and the FY2026 Mars Technology program budget, assigns specific 30-day and 60-day organizational actions to ESDMD and STMD, and specifies mandatory technical and contractual requirements for a forthcoming FSP Request for Proposal (RFP), including a minimum 100 kWe power output, a heavy-class lander mass allocation of up to 15 metric tons, launch readiness by Q1 FY30, and a closed Brayton cycle power conversion system. Closes with a set of longer-range policy directives on program governance, interface standardization, and dual-use coordination.

---

### Purpose and policy context (p. 1)

- States that fission surface power is framed as "an essential and sustainable segment of the lunar and Mars power architectures for future human space exploration missions" (p. 1).
- Ties the FSP project to commercial microreactor technology referenced in the White House's Executive Order 14299, "Deploying Advanced Nuclear Reactor Technologies for National Security," dated May 23, 2025 (p. 1).
- States the agency must "move quickly" to advance the technology to support a future lunar economy, high-power Mars energy generation, and national security in space (p. 1).

### Background cited in the directive (p. 1)

- Since March 2024, China and Russia have announced, on at least three occasions, a joint effort to place a reactor on the Moon by the mid-2030s. The memo states that the first country to do so "could potentially declare a keep-out zone" that would inhibit a planned Artemis presence if the U.S. is not there first (p. 1).
- FSP is stated to directly address the top two shortfalls in NASA's 2024 Civil Space Shortfall Ranking document: shortfall #1, surviving and operating through the lunar night, and shortfall #2, providing high-power energy generation for the Moon and Mars surfaces (p. 1).
- Industry feedback to NASA indicates surface power needs of at least 100 kWe for long-term human operations, including in-situ resource utilization (p. 1).
- Since 2000, NASA has invested over $200M in FSP technologies spanning 1 kWe to 200 kWe, with, per the memo, "no significant advancement towards flight system readiness" (p. 1).
- The most recent completed effort, FSP Phase 1 (2023), consisted of three $5M industry study contracts focused on a 40 kWe concept; cost estimates, schedules, and initial concept designs were delivered under that effort (p. 1).
- The President's Budget Request (PBR) for FY2026 includes $350M in FY26 for a new Mars Technology program intended to accelerate development of high-priority Mars technologies, including FSP; this funding ramps to $500M starting in FY27 (p. 1).
- The memo notes that additional funding is expected to become available as NASA transitions to commercial services for Artemis IV and beyond (p. 1).

### Directed organizational actions (p. 2)

- Within 30 days of the directive, the Associate Administrator (AA) for the Exploration Systems Development Mission Directorate (ESDMD) shall designate a Fission Surface Power Program Executive (FSP PE), the empowered and accountable official for end-to-end execution of the FSP effort, covering procurement, technology integration, interagency coordination, and performance oversight. The FSP PE is authorized to report and provide updates with maximum transparency directly to the Administrator (p. 2).
- Within 60 days of the directive, ESDMD shall issue a new procurement Request for Proposal (RFP) to industry (p. 2).
- The Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) shall immediately cease any new FSP technology-maturation efforts that do not support the RFP, and shall align available FY25 funding to support the RFP's initial award amount (p. 2).
- ESDMD, through the FSP PE, shall lead the program with support from the Office of General Counsel and the Office of Procurement (p. 2).
- The FSP project is capped at 15 full-time engineer equivalents (FTEs) on a yearly basis and a maximum of 10% overhead cost to manage. The project is directed to adopt a "minimum viable structure" (MVS) for its management team, composed of essential civil-servant roles supported by flexible, targeted contractor expertise, prioritizing agility, reduced duplication, and milestone-driven delivery (p. 2).
- The Partnerships Office is excused from participating in the management and execution of the Space Act Agreement (p. 2).

### Required RFP features (p. 2)

- Award to two providers within six months of RFP release, with an option to down-select to one provider at PDR (Preliminary Design Review) (p. 2).
- Flexibility to industry in the extent of demonstration capability, within these floors/ceilings:
  - Minimum 100 kWe power output (p. 2).
  - Assumed use of a heavy-class lander, with up to 15 metric tons of mass available (p. 2).
  - Readiness to launch by the first quarter of FY30 (p. 2).
- A closed Brayton cycle power conversion system, specified "to reduce risk and ensure extensibility to higher power systems" (p. 2).
- Use of NASA's funded Space Act Authority (SAA) to grant industry maximum flexibility in how to design and develop FSP flight systems (p. 2).
- Completion of an operational spaceflight system, with an option for continued sustainment support (p. 2).
- Flexibility for NASA to set contract value based on proposed industry capability, potential industry cost-sharing (for example, in exchange for industry owning and operating the reactor power once operational), and fund availability (p. 2).
- Milestone-based payments, with no less than 25% of total contract value paid after successful checkout and delivery of the FSP flight system (p. 2).

### Additional policy directives for competitiveness and leadership (p. 3)

- Empower the FSP Program Executive to make time-sensitive decisions within delegated authority, and adopt streamlined internal approval pathways prioritizing decision velocity for critical milestones (p. 3).
- Prioritize commercial partnerships proposing integrated lander-FSP architectures, with power systems serving as structural and thermal subsystems (p. 3).
- Define and publish a Standardized Modular Interface (SMI) for FSP systems to ensure compatibility across landers and habitats (p. 3).
- Encourage dual-use civil and defense operational architectures for deployed FSP systems, in coordination with interagency partners (p. 3).
- Mandate FSP-readiness in upcoming lunar cargo transportation services solicitations, including structural and thermal compatibility requirements (p. 3).
- Provide priority to power systems capable of supporting ISRU and in-situ manufacturing demonstrations by FY30 (p. 3).
- Assign named accountable project executives for all phases, to ensure continuity and leadership (p. 3).
- Establish key performance indicators (KPIs) and success metrics for each project milestone (p. 3).
- Require a stakeholder engagement plan aligning NASA, industry, and interagency participants, including technical interchange meetings (p. 3).
- Publish a detailed schedule with key decision points, demonstration readiness reviews, and flight-readiness timelines (p. 3).
- Include a governance and reporting framework specifying monthly and quarterly review cadences, escalation protocols, and performance tracking (p. 3).
