### LSIC General Updates

- **NASA 2026 Civil Space Shortfalls ranking list** (released May 2026): integrates over 400 responses from industry, government, and academic organizations to rank the space community's most pervasive technology shortfalls and guide NASA's space-technology investment. Details and future feedback opportunities at nasa.gov/civilspaceshortfalls.
- **Lunar Enabling Infrastructure Accelerator (LEIA) — NextSTEP-3 BAA Appendix A**: NASA Glenn Research Center (on behalf of STMD) plans to solicit proposals from U.S. institutions to mature/demonstrate capabilities in vertical solar arrays, ISRU oxygen-production systems, Stirling radioisotope generators, in-space manufacturing, and advanced nanomaterials production, aiming to advance these technologies to TRL 5–6. Timeline (tentative): Draft BAA on/about June 2026; Final BAA on/about August 2026; anticipated offer due date on/about September 2026. Notice ID 80GRC026R0008; SAM.gov listing referenced in the source.
- **NASA STMD/SSERVI Simulants Database**: newly hosted database providing information on 81 simulants (Moon, Mars, asteroids, comets, other regolith materials); open to community submissions of additional simulants or feedback.

### Capability Area Updates

**Crosscutting Capabilities (CC).** Spring Meeting breakout sessions covered Interoperability for Early Activities and Autonomous Needs for Exploration, addressing near-term interoperability gaps (especially for "Phase One Moon Base" objectives) and technical barriers to robust autonomous surface mobility. Next CC telecon (June 18, 1 PM) is focused on the lunar surface radiation environment — radiation characterization/data-collection plans, space-weather impacts, and how radiation testing of parts/materials informs hardware choices for early payloads.

**Excavation and Construction (E&C).** Spring Meeting breakouts addressed ESDMD Architecture-driven Data and Technology Gaps (Day 2) and a Moon Base Precursor and Enabling Activities session (Day 3) on developments/decision spaces needed for early Moon Base surface demonstrations, testing, and operations. In May, E&C held a joint meeting with ISRU on "Mapping Lunar Resources for ISRU and Lunar Infrastructure," featuring a Boeing update on lunar surface interests, an AI-interfacing knowledge-base introduction from Joseph Varga (Forge Orbital), and a ground-penetrating radar (GPR) discussion from Adrian Tang (JPL) on the SkyFall Mars drone approach and its applicability to lunar geotechnical data needs. Next E&C meeting: Wednesday, June 24, 2 PM ET.

**In Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU).** Spring Meeting participation included poster, lightning-talk, and tech-showcase presentations. Day 2 ESDMD gaps breakout ranked transportation/infrastructure/ISRU/mobility gaps, with the top two identified data gaps being in-situ measurement of water and volatiles. Day 3 ISRU-specific breakout discussed near-term lunar surface ISRU demonstration opportunities, their risk-reduction value for a large-scale ISRU plant, and a prioritization exercise assessing how participants' systems reduce risk for ISRU plant operations. May's joint ISRU/E&C meeting (see E&C above) featured the same Boeing/Forge Orbital/JPL content, describing the JPL instrument as designed for "MoonFall drones" mapping lunar geotechnical data (note: the Feature/E&C sections of this issue use "SkyFall" for the Mars-context radar and "MoonFall" for the lunar-mapping application — both terms appear in the source as given). June's ISRU monthly meeting will include LSIC updates, a talk on the Oasis-1 mission, and speaker Q&A.

**Surface Power (SP).** Spring Meeting breakouts: a Lunar Surface Power Systems session (Day 2) covering near-term technology demonstrations, paths to power-grid operationalization, and needed critical actions, with discussion of accelerated Moon Base pacing, standards-development alignment, and testing-facility availability; a Considerations for Lunar Power Systems Testing session (Day 3) further addressed facility-availability challenges, prioritizing (1) triaging highest-risk systems/subsystems/components, (2) reducing barriers to sharing test data, and (3) adopting a "fail-iterate-succeed" culture for Phase I technology demonstrations.
On **May 28**, SP hosted the **Lunar Surface Power Transmission Workshop**, drawing **145 participants**. Panels covered the lunar surface environment (abrasive regolith, radiation, thermal cycling, electrostatic charging as challenges to transmission infrastructure), lessons from terrestrial microgrids (interoperability, automation, resilience as early design priorities), lunar transmission architecture concepts (incremental grid build-out; AC vs. DC transmission debate), and power conditioning/distribution (power electronics, protection systems, radiation-hardened components, cryogenic energy storage). Early demonstrations/testing to reduce technical risk was a recurring workshop theme. A full workshop report is planned for future release to the SP community.
Upcoming: June 25 extended telecon on lunar surface battery developments — Brian Holler and Nick Uguccini (NASA Glenn Research Center) and Brian Elliott (TDA Research) on low-temperature battery/electronics performance (reducing heater requirements for night survival); Earl Isidro (NASA Johnson Space Center) presenting NASA's Surface Power Exchange Connector project.

### Member Spotlight: Astrobotic (LunaGrid)

By Brett Molina. Describes Astrobotic's mobile lunar power-outlet system, designed to transmit power from a lunar lander, Vertical Solar Array Technology (VSAT) power plant, or fission surface power reactor via a tethered rover with a wireless charger, to distributed Moon Base elements (habitat, science suite, lunar terrain vehicle). Underpins Astrobotic's planned **LunaGrid** service — an end-to-end power-delivery offering combining Astrobotic landers, VSAT, and the mobile power outlet, intended to support sustained, multiyear lunar operations. Key technical points:
- Tested against lunar-night duration (~14 Earth days) and abrasive regolith/dust degradation risk.
- A 500-meter (1,640 ft) ultralight cable and accompanying CubeRover have undergone environmental qualification testing at the engineering-model level: Thermal Vacuum (TVAC), Electromagnetic Interference (EMI), vibration, and shock.
- System components: CubeRover, Ultralight Cable, Cable Deployment System, and Universal Modular Interface Converter (UMIC).
- First version: an Astrobotic CubeRover autonomously deploys/unreels up to 500 m of ultralight cable from a company-built lander, transmitting high-voltage AC power.
- Quoted: Matt Zamborsky (senior project manager, Astrobotic) on cable/regolith interaction testing and on mobile outlets potentially opening permanently shadowed regions to powered operations; Nate Holland (associate chief engineer, Astrobotic) on lunar infrastructure as a stepping stone to destinations beyond the Moon.
