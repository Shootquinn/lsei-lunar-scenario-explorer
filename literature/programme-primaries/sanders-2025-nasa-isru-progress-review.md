# Progress Review of NASA Lunar ISRU Development, 2019 to 2025

## Citation

Sanders, G. (Jerry), & Kleinhenz, J. (2025, May 19). *Progress review of NASA lunar ISRU
development: 2019 to 2025* [Conference presentation]. Luxembourg Space Resources Week,
Luxembourg. Presenter: Gerald (Jerry) Sanders, ISRU System Capability Lead, NASA Space
Technology Mission Directorate (STMD); co-author Julie Kleinhenz, ISRU System Capability
Deputy, NASA STMD.

Publisher URL: none in source

## Abstract

A NASA STMD status briefing surveying lunar in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) technology
maturation from the 2019 start of the Artemis program through mid-2025. Covers resource
exploration instrumentation, oxygen/metal extraction from regolith (carbothermal reduction,
molten regolith electrolysis, hydrogen/CO reduction), and polar ice/water mining (excavation,
processing, capture/cleanup, electrolysis), reporting technology readiness level (TRL) status
and selected performance figures for each process as asserted in the briefing.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The deck is framed as a six-year progress check (2019-2025) against the earlier Constellation
program era (2006-2011), identified as the first focused U.S. effort on lunar ISRU. The stated
program goal is "scalable ISRU production/utilization capabilities," including sustainable
commodities on the lunar and Mars surface, pursued through market transparency (defining
customer needs for oxygen, water/hydrogen, bulk and refined regolith, metals, silicon/ceramics,
and other feedstocks), technology and system development, and mission insertion via flight
demonstrations and an eventual industry-led pilot plant. The talk's objective is to summarize
what has been demonstrated, at what maturity, since 2019, and what gaps remain.

### Methods and scope

The source is a 12-slide conference presentation, not a peer-reviewed study; it synthesizes
NASA-internal program results, NASA solicitations and public-private partnerships (Tipping
Points, Announcements of Collaborative Opportunities), Big Idea Challenges, and third-party
(commercial and academic) development work. Scope is lunar ISRU only, in three technical
threads: resource exploration instrumentation, oxygen/metal extraction from regolith, and
polar ice/water mining. Slide 11 is a bare "BACKUP" divider; slide 12 is a backup slide
expanding the exploration-instrument content of slide 4. No experimental methods are described
for the underlying work; the deck reports outcomes and TRL assessments as program status,
generally without citing primary sources (one exception: a 2024 Space Resources Roundtable
conference paper cited on slide 4).

### Key findings

**Oxygen/metal extraction from regolith**

- Carbothermal (CH4) reduction — described as the highest-TRL oxygen extraction technology.
  Breadboard tested in Hawaii in 2010 with mare-like material; a new prototype reactor was
  built and tested with highland simulants through a Tipping Point with Sierra Space; breadboard
  and prototype reactors were then tested under lunar environmental conditions using a laser
  heat source at Johnson Space Center (JSC), reaching **TRL 5**. Reported figures: single melts
  equivalent to a production rate of **140 kg O2/yr**; 5 consecutive melt operations performed
  under lunar vacuum/thermal conditions; oxygen extraction of **>20 g O2/kW-hr thermal**; O2
  yield of **>20% gm O2 per gm regolith**; **>99.7%** recovery of the carbon used in the melt.
  An integrated reactor/solar-concentrator system was slated for testing in 2025 (NASA and
  Sierra Space).
- Molten Regolith Electrolysis (MRE) — TRL "significantly improved" for oxygen and metal (Fe,
  Si, Al) extraction from highland regolith. Two firms cited: Lunar Resources tested an MRE
  iron reactor under lunar environmental conditions at Kennedy Space Center in a single
  melt/electrolysis operation, processing **25 kg** of lunar highland simulant over **36 hours**
  (24 hours of electrolysis), with measured O2 production matching theoretical levels. Blue
  Origin has MRE and purification reactors (Fe, Si, Al) plus solar-cell and aluminum-wire
  fabrication subsystems under development, with integrated lunar-environmental testing planned
  for **2026**. Slide 10 characterizes both carbothermal reduction and MRE as demonstrated to
  **TRL 5/6** for polar highland regolith.
- Hydrogen/CO reduction of mare regolith — advanced to **TRL 5** (also stated as TRL 5/6 on
  slide 10). Two breadboard systems (Lockheed Martin and JSC) were tested in Hawaii in 2008
  with mare-like material. The MMOST system (Pioneer Astronautics, now Redwire) combined size
  sorting/beneficiation, H2 reduction for oxygen, and separate CO reduction for metal
  extraction.
- Other oxygen/metal approaches — advancing to **TRL 4**: vacuum vapor pyrolysis (multiple
  companies, using cryo-distillation, electro/magnetic separation, or solid-oxide-transport
  removal of oxygen); and hydrochloric reduction followed by Molten Salt Electrolysis (MSE) of
  aluminum chloride, with all process steps demonstrated in the Lunar Forge Big Idea Challenge
  and stated as viable for both terrestrial and lunar reduction of highland anorthosite.
  Regolith excavation, transport, and preparation (size sorting/mineral separation) are stated
  as demonstrated to **TRL 4/5**.
- Funding context: these technologies are advancing via internal R&D and/or venture capital, PPP
  mechanisms (Tipping Points, ACOs), and private investment with and without NASA funding; DOE
  funding and terrestrial industry partnerships (clean iron/steel) are cited as growing.

**Polar ice/water mining**

- Loose-regolith excavation and delivery — **TRL 5** in a simulated mission. Arm/scoops and
  mobile excavators (bucket drum, bucket wheel, bucket ladder) were developed and demonstrated;
  the ISRU Pilot Excavator (IPEx), a bucket-drum excavator, moved **10 metric tons in 5 days**
  in a simulated mission.
- Hard icy-regolith excavation and delivery — **TRL 5** in simulated excavation/delivery via the
  Break the Ice Challenge: 15 teams operated for 15 days at their own sites, excavating
  **12,000 kg total (800 kg/day)** with each delivery over a 500 m traverse; 6 teams advanced to
  a 2-day timed competition at Alabama A&M.
- Icy-regolith processing (reactor) — the Lunar Auger Dryer ISRU (LADI) reached **TRL 4**:
  continuous processing with regolith plugs at each end, targeting **1.78 kg/hr** water
  extraction rate and **75%** water extraction efficiency. It was cancelled before reaching TRL
  5 due to budget cuts, though the deck states the technology/concept was validated.
  Contained subsurface reactor/coring — the Planetary Volatile Extractor (PVEx) reached
  **TRL 5/6**: a coring drill contains icy regolith, the inner core wall is heated
  (electrical or thermal fluid), and sublimated water/volatiles are captured in a cold trap.
  Initial design was a 1 m core with 2.5 cm inner diameter (ID); larger IDs were examined.
  Reported performance for a 5 cm ID, 0.5 m length core: 4-6 wt% water content yielding 43-56%
  water extraction, contingent on icy-regolith properties and core geometry.
- Water capture and cleanup (Paragon Space and NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC)/JSC) — cold
  trap/freeze distillation, the primary capture method, advanced to **TRL 3/4** with both active
  and passive cooling designs; data obtained up to **0.1 kg/hr** water vapor flow, described as
  sufficient to proceed to prototype/flight design. Cleanup adds absorption and membrane
  separation after freeze distillation to remove contaminants, demonstrated at mission-relevant
  rates, but the deck flags further work needed on material compatibility and mixed
  contaminants.
- Water electrolysis — Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolysis is stated to have high TRL
  provided the input water is sufficiently clean; multiple vendors/projects have matured
  cathode-fed, anode-fed, and non-flow PEM configurations to system breadboard/prototype level.
  Solid Oxide Electrolysis (SOE) of water and CO2 reached **TRL 5**: OxEon has demonstrated
  scale, performance, and operation under lunar and Mars environmental conditions at
  mission-relevant scale, with each stack producing up to **0.9 kg/hr O2 and 0.12 kg/hr H2**
  over "1000's of hours" of operation; individual or joint electrolysis of CO2 and H2O is
  described as enabling system-level trades. Alkaline electrolysis of dirty water is reported to
  have unresolved corrosion issues.
- Top-level status (deck's own summary): significant advancement has occurred; technologies are
  advancing via IR&D/venture capital; there is **no current NASA technology development work on
  icy-regolith processing**; and final processing-system designs depend on icy-regolith resource
  information "not currently available."

**Resource exploration instrumentation and missions**

- The deck asserts a "significant number" of instruments and rovers developed for lunar science
  are applicable to resource assessment, with some already manifested on flight missions, but
  states not all are aimed at understanding lunar resources and that new instruments should fill
  a specific gap rather than duplicate existing capability. It states no U.S. instruments are
  currently funded for icy-regolith sample acquisition and heating (i.e., subsurface volatile
  measurement).
- A matrix on slides 4 and 12 maps individual instruments (drills, pneumatic samplers,
  spectrometers, ground-penetrating radar, seismometers, neutron spectrometers, mass
  spectrometers, rovers, etc.) against named missions (including PRIME-1, VIPER, IM-2, IM-3,
  Chandrayaan-3, LUPEX (JAXA), SLIM (JAXA), and Luna 27), with an X marking which instrument
  flies on which mission. **The precise instrument-to-mission mapping in this matrix could not
  be reliably recovered**: the column headers are set as rotated/mirrored text that both the
  PDF text layer and a rendered-image reading could not resolve with confidence, so it is
  reported here only as a matrix's existence and its named entries, not as reconstructed
  cell-by-cell data.
- Slide 12 (backup) lists individual TRL values for named exploration payloads: PRIME-1's
  TRIDENT drill and MSolo mass spectrometer at **TRL 9** (consistent with flight use); the
  ESA-provided PROSPECT package (including the ProSPA instrument) at **TRL 7**; SPARTA (JPL,
  Science Mission Directorate) at **TRL 5**; Light WAVE — Light Water Analysis & Volatile
  Extraction (NASA JSC, Game Changing Development) at **TRL 5**; the Honeybee Robotics PVEx
  drill (SSEVI RESOURCE project) at **TRL 4/5**; and an item labeled MEERCAT (KSC) at **TRL 4**.
  Named rover/lander platforms without stated TRL figures include MoonRanger/CADEE (JPL),
  VIPER, LUPEX, MAPP (Lunar Outpost), and the Micro-Nova Hopper (Intuitive Machines).
- Cited risk: several resource-exploration-related missions have failed, been cancelled, or
  been delayed (unnamed on the slide beyond this general statement), which the deck says causes
  knowledge gaps and argues for redundancy — multiple missions targeting different locations to
  build confidence in the geological context of measurements. A 2024 Space Resources Roundtable
  paper (Patterson, O'Brien, Galien, Vaenciano, & Neal) is cited regarding lunar polar volatile
  reserve-potential assessment.

**2019-2025 timeline and background context**

- The talk's implicit baseline is the Constellation program (2006-2011), described as the first
  focused U.S. lunar ISRU effort, covering resource prospecting, excavation, transfer, and
  processing, and originating the three-tier oxygen-from-regolith risk framing (H2 reduction =
  low risk/low performance; carbothermal CH4 reduction = medium risk/medium performance; MRE =
  high risk/high performance). Constellation-era analog field tests: H2 reduction at Mauna Kea,
  HI in 2008 (bucket-drum hardware reported at 250 kg O2/yr; a fluidized H2 reduction reactor at
  660 kg O2/yr; rotating H2-reduction hardware at 17 kg O2/batch, per slide imagery); CH4
  reduction at Mauna Kea in 2010 (125 kg O2/yr, per slide imagery); and lunar polar water
  prospecting (RESOLVE, a NASA-CSA payload) at Mauna Kea in 2012. RESOLVE is stated to have led
  to Lunar Prospector (2011-18) and then VIPER (2019+).
- A Constellation-era "TRL increase in ETDP" table (Exploration Technology Development Program)
  gives start/end TRL and delta for numerous system- and subsystem-level items over the
  2008-2012 window, e.g., Lunar Volatile Characterization (RESOLVE) TRL 1 to 5 (delta 4); H2
  Reduction of Regolith TRL 2-3 to 5 (delta 2-3); CH4 Reduction of Regolith TRL 2-3 to 5 (delta
  2-3); Molten Oxide Reduction of Regolith TRL 2 to 3 (delta 1); and Solar Thermal Energy for
  Regolith Reduction TRL 2 to 5 (delta 3), among some two dozen other line items. This table
  documents Constellation-era progress specifically, not the 2019-2025 window that is the deck's
  nominal subject.
- For the 2019-2025 window itself, the deck does not present an equivalent dated table; instead
  it gives discrete, mostly undated milestones under "Results to Date" (above) plus two forward
  dates: an integrated carbothermal reactor/solar-concentrator test **planned for 2025**
  (Sierra Space) and integrated MRE lunar-environmental testing **planned for 2026**
  (Blue Origin). PRIME-1 (TRIDENT drill and MSolo), listed at TRL 9, is consistent with a flown
  payload but the deck does not narrate its mission timeline or outcome beyond the TRL entry.

**Stated gaps** (deck's own summary, condensed): no current NASA technology development work on
icy-regolith processing; final icy-regolith processing designs depend on resource information
(water/ice form, concentration, distribution in polar regolith) that is "currently unknown";
alkaline electrolysis of dirty water has unresolved corrosion issues; no U.S. instruments are
currently funded for icy-regolith sample acquisition and heating; water/volatile subsurface
instruments still need to be matured to flight. The deck's own risk framing: flight
demonstrations "can proceed with reasonable risk" for oxygen/metal extraction from regolith,
but lunar water/volatile extraction "is lacking sufficient resource knowledge to proceed
without significant risk."

### Limitations

This is a conference slide deck, not a peer-reviewed paper: claims and figures are
assertion-dense and, apart from one cited 2024 conference paper, uncited within the deck.
Numeric claims (production rates, TRL values, efficiencies) cannot be traced to underlying test
reports from this document alone and should be read as the program's stated position circa May
2025, not as independently verified results. Several figures appear only as photo captions on
slide 5 and are reported here as such. The instrument-to-mission matrix on slides 4 and 12 uses
rotated column-header text that could not be reliably parsed from either the PDF text layer or
a rendered image; its cell-level content (which instrument flies on which named mission) is not
reported beyond the instrument and mission names themselves. Slide 1 carries leftover template
placeholder text ("PRESENTATION TITLE," "SUB-TITLE," "Presenter's Name") alongside the actual
title, presenter, venue, and date, which appear unambiguously in the surrounding text; this is
treated as a template artifact and does not affect confidence in the citation elements above.
No abstract or paper text accompanies the deck.
