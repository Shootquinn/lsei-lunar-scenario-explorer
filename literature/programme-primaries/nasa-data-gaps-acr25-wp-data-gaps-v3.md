## Abstract

Defines what NASA means by an "architecture-driven data gap" and how such gaps are derived, documented, and evolved within the Moon to Mars architecture process. A data gap exists where missing or incomplete information hinders NASA's ability to meet its Moon to Mars Objectives — distinct from a technology gap (a capability shortfall). The paper is a framework/communications document, not a comprehensive gap catalog: it explains the six standard fields that describe each gap, the knowledge-decomposition process that produces them, the annual revision cycle, and the intent to signal partnership opportunities to industry, academia, and international partners. It illustrates the concept with one fully worked catalog entry (sub-meter south-pole imaging, DN-001) and a short representative list of preliminary high-priority, near-term gaps from Architecture Definition Document Revision C.

---

### Background and objective

NASA frames data as a driver of exploration innovation. The agency already holds much of the data needed for lunar and Martian missions, but available data sometimes cannot meet mission needs. Architecture-driven data gaps mark those shortfalls — missing information that impacts NASA's ability to perform architecture analyses, characterize or reduce mission risk, develop hardware, mature technology, advance science, or enable crew health and performance.

The gaps are documented in an appendix to NASA's Architecture Definition Document (ADD, ESDMD-001), which captures how NASA will accomplish its Moon to Mars Objectives. Revision C of the ADD carried the first list of architecture-driven data gaps. The stated objective of publishing them is to provide a "clear demand signal" so partners can align their data-gathering efforts with agency needs. The gaps are explicitly not procurement directives, do not assign a monetary value, and do not cover enhancement of already-acquired data (e.g., post-processing).

### Methods and scope

- **Knowledge decomposition.** Just as functional decomposition breaks objectives into required capabilities, "knowledge decomposition" breaks objectives into required information. NASA coordinates across its mission directorates (Science, Space Operations, Space Technology, Aeronautics Research) to identify information needed to plan utilization activities and meet exploration goals. Where necessary information is absent, a data gap is documented. Not every item in the knowledge decomposition becomes a data gap, but the decomposition informs gap development.
- **Data need vs. data gap vs. partner data.** A data need is any information (e.g., a specific measurement) valuable to Artemis / Moon-to-Mars activities. Data gaps are a subset of data needs — the unmet ones. Partner data is information partners can supply beyond what NASA holds; the paper cites CLPS (Commercial Lunar Payload Services) partners already gathering potentially valuable data during payload deliveries. NASA can acquire partner data through data-sharing agreements, data buys, or other methods.
- **Data gaps vs. technology gaps.** Both use the word "gap" (a difference between existing capability and current/expected need). Technology gaps describe development needs; data gaps describe information needs. The two can be linked (e.g., new data is needed to set performance targets for a technology), and NASA tracks those dependencies. Technology gaps are published separately (2024 Architecture-Driven Technology Gaps white paper).
- **Evolution.** The list is deliberately small and non-comprehensive — high-priority, near-term needs only. It is refined each annual strategic analysis cycle: NASA validates, updates, adds, or closes gaps as data is collected and new needs arise, and solicits partner feedback. Future ADD revisions publish updated lists.

Each gap is documented with a standard set of fields (ADD Table One):

| Component | Definition |
|---|---|
| Description | Defines the data needed |
| Objectives | Traceability to the Moon to Mars Objectives — how the data contributes to objective satisfaction |
| Segment | Traceability to the Moon to Mars Architecture — the timeframe in which NASA needs the data |
| Target Measurement Parameters | Threshold for data quality; a goal for data providers |
| Impacts and Benefits | Significance of the data, usually tied to enabling architecture analyses, characterizing risk, maturing technology, or advancing science |
| Current State of Data | Relevant available data and existing acquisition solutions, which may not fully satisfy the need |

### Key findings

The paper's substantive content is (a) one fully worked catalog entry and (b) a short representative list.

**Worked example — DN-001 (illustrative full catalog entry, from ADD p. 265):**
- **Title:** Sustained, site-specific sub-meter-scale imaging of lunar south-pole exploration zones and sites.
- **Data Type:** Orbit-to-surface imagery. **Objectives:** LPS-01 LM, SE-05 LM, LI-03 L. **Segment:** Human Lunar Return. **Need driver:** Surface Site Characterization.
- **Description:** Produce optical maps / image sets of the south-pole region to identify surface features, obstacles, and hazards across all Artemis landing regions, supporting landing site/ellipse design, element placement, and traverse planning. Multiple images of the same site should be captured under varying lighting and at higher resolution than currently available.
- **Target parameters:** Sustained high-spatial-resolution optical imaging, <0.5 m/pixel preferred; sufficient temporal resolution to capture varying lighting; images at least every 1 week (every 48 hours preferred) across an entire year.
- **Current state of data:** No additional orbital imagery is required to land and run a nominal south-pole mission. LRO/LROC Narrow Angle Camera (NAC) data over ~15 years is the primary planning dataset; highest-resolution panchromatic imagery of Artemis regions from LROC NAC is ~0.5 m/pixel. ShadowCam on the Korean KPLO images permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) as fine as 1.7 m/pixel but only in low-light areas. JAXA Kaguya scanned at ~8–10 m/pixel. ISRO Chandrayaan-2 OHRC images select south-pole areas at ~0.3 m/pixel.
- **Impacts:** Without added imagery, additional data is desirable to inform surface-element design (power systems, energy storage, winter-hibernation operating power). Lack of continued high-resolution data could cause element loss from winter power failure, unsafe landings, or mission-ending mobility hazards; over-compensating for uncertainty adds prohibitive mass, while underestimating threatens survival.

**Representative preliminary gaps (ADD Rev C, Table Two):**

| Listed ID (as printed) | Data gap | Data utility |
|---|---|---|
| L / 200-ND | Comprehensive, high-fidelity elevation-map coverage of lunar south-pole exploration zones and sites | Better characterization of landing sites; increased confidence in landing accuracy and mobility-system navigation |
| L / 700-ND | In situ measurements of the horizontal and vertical distribution, abundance, and physical makeup of shallow bulk water ice | Better identification of potential sites for in-situ resource utilization |
| L / 800-ND | Geotechnical properties of highland regolith at the lunar south pole | Higher certainty in the landing environment to inform lander design and site selection |
| L / 710-ND | In situ measurement of particle velocity during lunar plume–surface interaction (PSI) phenomena | Better modelling of lander–regolith interactions to mitigate risk of hardware damage |

**Key takeaways (as stated):** Architecture-driven data gaps arise when missing information affects NASA's ability to meet Moon to Mars Objectives; they mark areas where current/future NASA or partner missions can supply data. The Revision C list is an initial set of high-priority, near-term items — neither comprehensive nor prioritized — and future ADD revisions will update it.

### Limitations

- The paper is a framework/communications document. It defines the concept and process and gives illustrative entries; it is not the full data-gap catalog (that lives in the ADD appendix). No TRLs, quantities, or production targets are assigned here (those belong to technology gaps, published separately).
- The published list is explicitly described as non-comprehensive and non-prioritized, and subject to annual change.
- Figures One, Two, and Three are diagrams; their captions are captured but the graphical content is not reproduced here. The extracted identifiers in Table Two are partially garbled (see note above).

### Topic mapping

- **Prospecting / resource characterization.** The bulk of the illustrative gaps concern south-pole knowledge: sub-meter imaging (DN-001), high-fidelity elevation mapping (L/200-ND), in situ shallow bulk water-ice distribution and abundance (L/700-ND), and highland regolith geotechnical properties (L/800-ND). These are information (not hardware) needs feeding site selection and ISRU-site identification.
- **Excavation / construction / landing operations.** Geotechnical-property and plume–surface-interaction gaps (L/800-ND, L/710-ND) bear on lander design, site preparation, and hardware-damage risk on unprepared terrain.
- **Power.** The DN-001 impact narrative ties imaging directly to surface-element power-system and energy-storage design and winter-hibernation operating power.
- **Process / framework.** The data-need / data-gap / technology-gap distinctions and the knowledge-decomposition and annual-revision mechanisms describe how NASA structures and evolves these information needs — relevant to any workshop that maps capability areas to NASA's demand signals.
