## Abstract

A DARPA program-update briefing on LunA-10, a capability study aimed at defining the commercial technical building blocks of a self-sustaining lunar economy by 2035. Summarizes work from a consortium of 13 companies organized into six initial service areas, each presented as an inputs/outputs/limitations "unit" of a future lunar economy. Presents per-performer service concepts (landers, wireless power, ISRU oxygen and metals, comms/PNT, transit, robotics, construction), a set of emerging cross-cutting topics DARPA flags for further development (recyclable ISRU, wireless power, cislunar harbor, robotics-as-a-service, networked towers, thermal-as-a-service, lunar rail), and an appendix of "white space" analytic charts quantifying production rates, cost/mass tradeoffs, and capability envelopes. The deck states no explicit TRLs; its quantitative content is in capability tables and log-scale tradeoff charts.

---

### Background and objective

LunA-10 (10-Year Lunar Architecture) is a DARPA Strategic Technology Office capability study. The briefing frames DARPA's strategic direction with two "pushes": (1) from individual self-service systems toward commercial multi-service systems, and (2) from government as sole sponsor toward government (and others) as a *customer* of commercial providers. For any given service or unit, the study asks what the inputs, outputs, and limitations are, and "What DARPA-hard technical challenges must be surmounted to create a sustainable lunar economy by 2035?" The deck describes itself as "a fast and furious summary of over 25,000 person-hours of labor." The program manager is Michael "Orbit" Nayak, Ph.D.

### Methods and scope

The study convened a consortium of industry performers, grouped by *initial* service (the deck stresses these groupings change across the program, with teaming arrangements reshuffled roughly every two weeks based on proposed technical areas/products). Each performer contributes a scalable, commercially framed "unit" with defined inputs, outputs, and cost model. A recurring analytic device is the DARPA "white space" chart, which places each performer's capability on log-scale axes against terrestrial and mission reference points to show where lunar services sit relative to known systems. Scope spans market analysis, power, mining/ISRU, comms/PNT/STM, transit/mobility, and construction/robotics; crew health, EVA, and launch-vehicle development are out of scope.

### Consortium and service structure

Six initial service areas and their performers (from the consortium slide):

- **Market Analysis and Cislunar Logistics:** Firefly Aerospace
- **Power:** Blue Origin, Honeybee Robotics, Fibertek Inc.
- **Mining & ISRU:** CisLunar Industries, Sierra Space, Helios
- **Communications, Position, Navigation, and Timing (PNT):** Crescent Space, Redwire
- **Transit and Mobility:** Northrop Grumman, SpaceX
- **Construction & Robotics:** GITAI, ICON

(The integrated-architecture diagram also names **Nokia** and **Lockheed Martin** as option/partner elements, and **Astrobotic** landers, though these are not among the six service-area boxes.)

### Key findings — performer concepts (with figures)

**Blue Origin — lander, power, comms, and ISRU.** Three complementary multi-service commercial systems: (1) a lander node as payload host and Infrastructure Platform, (2) a laser-enabled wireless power framework, (3) ISRU for construction, mining, and energy. A capability table gives: Solar Array > 10 kWe; Mast 20 m on a ~10 m lander (total ~30 m above surface); 3GPP Telecom Service 25 Mbps up to > 10 km range, max range ~100 km; Regen Fuel Cell Augmentation Kit 1.5 MWh, 7.8 kWe over 192 hrs; Laser Power Transmitter ~1 kWe delivered to 10+ km; Silicon Extraction Experiment to demonstrate production of silicon from regolith; Heat Rejection Augmentation Kit (added radiator area for payload power). A large integrated-architecture figure shows the Blue Moon Mk1 Lander (with LAMPS solar array and mast) as a hub interconnecting most other performers: Nokia/Crescent local comms, Blue Origin/Fibertek optical comms backhaul and power beaming, Redwire/Crescent RF comms/PNT satellite, Honeybee tower and solar arrays, ICON landing pads, GITAI/ICON construction rover, Sierra Space fuel cells producing GOX/LOX, CisLunar foundry, and Northrop Grumman railroad.

**CisLunar Industries — metal extraction and recycling.** Builds infrastructure and enables sustainable mining. Inputs: ISRU metals (Al, Fe), scrap, slag, alloying elements. Outputs: ingots, beams, sheet metal, extruded wire, and other metal products. (Quantitative appendix charts below.)

**Crescent Space — scalable, night-survivable comms, PNT, and STM.** A commoditized unit (product named "SCOUT") for surface area networking (SAN), space traffic management (STM), direct-to-Earth comms, PNT, and night-survivability add-ons, using a Lockheed Martin SmartSat reconfigurable software framework; Astrobotic appears as a lander partner. Product mass/power classes: MUST-MVP < 0.7 kg, < 20 W; MUST-SAN < 0.75 kg, < 40 W; MUST < 1.5 kg (+12 for STN and STM), < 60 W (+40 for STN and STM); MUST-HEAVY < 20 kg, < 125 W. Concepts include hybrid PNT, 3GPP-powered surface PNT, and two-way ranging solutions.

**Fibertek — Lunar Infrastructure Optical Node (LION).** Scalable, modular unit for surface power beaming, a high-speed surface optical backbone, a direct-to-Earth comms link, and a lunar-to-orbit comms link.

**Firefly Space — cislunar aggregation hub.** Design of a lunar "harbor" for in-space logistics, with cost modeling based on propellant demand and price; the architecture diagram shows Firefly aggregating cargo to L1.

**GITAI — Robotics as a Service (RaaS) for construction labor.** Tele-operated robotic labor charged in $/hour, using modular inchworm arms integrated with rovers for mobility; enables construction of ISRU, power, and transportation infrastructure plus maintenance/replacement, controllable from Earth with no astronaut required.

**Helios — ISRU plant to produce LOX propellant at scale.** Scalable Solid Oxide Electrolysis to extract oxygen from regolith. Products: gaseous O2, liquid O2 (LOX) propellant, and de-oxygenated regolith for further metal refinement.

**Honeybee Robotics — networked tower (LUNARSABER).** A lightweight, deployable utility tower hosting elevated payloads for navigation, sensing, and power distribution; increased line-of-sight for comms, power beaming, and PNT while simultaneously collecting solar power.

**ICON — sintering regolith for transportation infrastructure.** Laser VMX to pave roads and landing pads from raw regolith (minimal launched mass). Current production estimate: ~1 month for a 10 m diameter landing pad with 10 kW surface power. Tied to ICON's Project Olympus for NASA and commercial lunar projects.

**Northrop Grumman — lunar railway network.** Multi-purpose railway for crossmass mobility; concept designs include construction materials and track-foundation design, cargo railcars, and construction/maintenance vehicles.

**Redwire — orbital constellation for RF services.** Cislunar orbiter constellation providing communications, PNT, RF survey, SAR/MTI, and microwave space-based solar power beaming.

**Sierra Space — night-survivable oxygen plant with integrated fuel cell.** Three functions: (1) oxygen extraction from regolith (carbothermal reduction), (2) fuel-cell energy storage for surface transportation nodes (STN), (3) chemical conversion for recycling and storage. A process diagram shows regolith delivered to the ISRU plant, carbothermal reduction yielding slag and oxygen with recycled carbon looping back, and a day/night power cycle (lunar-day PV electricity to a power grid; lunar-night electricity from the fuel cell).

**SpaceX — transit and mobility.** Starship as both an Earth-to-Moon launch vehicle and a lunar surface hub; a reusable, super-heavy transport with integrated comms, power, and nighttime battery services; third-party payloads deployable from Starship's height; surface hub provides propellant and fluid transfer.

### Key findings — emerging cross-cutting topics

DARPA highlights topics needing further technical development:

- **Re-ISRU (Recyclable ISRU):** Man-made equipment on the Moon may grow by an order of magnitude over 10 years; the definition of "in-situ" resources is extended to include *defunct manmade equipment*, reused via metal recycling and robotic disassembly + foundry ingest. Product families: [1] casting (radiators, billets), [2] extrusion (lunar rail, wire, L-channel), [3] fabricated products (tower truss). A robotic-disassembly workflow is shown: Lander -> Inchworm/Rover -> Crusher -> Cislunar/ISRU (conditions: 1 part, semi-autonomous, cutting tool, desert demo site).
- **Wireless power (Tx/Rx):** Deemed technically feasible; for permanently shadowed region (PSR) exploration, cabled power solutions are limited, and wireless power (priced in $/kWh) is presented as a viable near-term commercial solution.
- **Commercial cislunar harbor:** Earth-Moon logistics plus spacecraft retirement at a cislunar hub, aggregating shared propellant, communications, and solar power.
- **Robotics as a Service (RaaS):** Inchworm robots on mobile rovers providing tele-operated labor charged hourly, for building, maintenance, and component replacement.
- **Foundational networked towers:** A centralized service node with expanded line-of-sight; can host or integrate almost any service (power, storage, data, comms, PNT, light, thermal); an "in one launch" setup paradigm.
- **Thermal as a Service:** Heat rejection/generation as a utility. Several key ISRU processes run at high temperature (> 1000 C); lunar night requires maintaining survivable temperatures for 14+ Earth days; a high percentage of thermal mass would otherwise be required on launch.
- **Lunar rail network:** Crossmass capability for regional hubs; current crossmass solution (rovers) is limited in heavy material handling, volatile storage, and travel distance on onboard power.

### Key findings — teaming examples

One example set of teaming arrangements (rotated every ~2 weeks): (1) GITAI robotic labor assembling/maintaining a Sierra Space + Helios oxygen plant; (2) demand from a SpaceX spacecraft to join a Firefly orbital aggregation hub; (3) a Honeybee solar-power tower integrated with Fibertek power beaming and Crescent comms at tower height; (4) whether CisLunar's metal products and ICON's sintered regolith have sufficient material properties to build a Northrop Grumman lunar rail network; (5) a Blue Origin lander integrated with Redwire SSA/PNT from orbit and Crescent surface comms.

### Limitations

The source is a public program-update briefing, not a technical report: figures are concept-level and the deck states no explicit TRLs. Numeric values are performer-supplied projections at illustrative power levels (10 kW / 1 MW / 1 GW) rather than demonstrated results. Groupings and teaming are explicitly transient. Several slides marked Distribution Statement A also carry a residual "Distribution Statement C" footer (US Government agencies and contractors only) — an internal inconsistency in the document's markings; content on those pages is otherwise consistent with the public-release material. Some closing/section slides are near-empty dividers.
