## Abstract

Orbital detection of water ice at the lunar optical surface from near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy. Moon Mineralogy Mapper radiance acquired in optical period 2C is searched, over shadowed pixels between 75 and 90 degrees latitude in both hemispheres, for the simultaneous occurrence of three diagnostic water-ice absorptions near 1.3, 1.5, and 2.0 micrometres, with a spectral-angle similarity screen against a laboratory pure-frost spectrum. Positive pixels occur only within 20 degrees of the poles, more than 90 percent of them within 10 degrees, and 93.2 percent of them coincide with LOLA reflectance, LAMP ultraviolet ratio, and Diviner annual maximum temperature values independently consistent with surface ice. Only about 3.5 percent of cold traps show any ice exposure. Spectral mixing modelling of the averaged polar spectra returns an abundance of about 30 weight percent for intimate ice-regolith mixing, or above 20 volume percent for subpixel patches, in the sensed layer, which is stated as less than a few millimetres deep.

### Background and objective

Small obliquity at Mercury, the Moon, and Ceres leaves polar topographic depressions permanently shadowed, holding surface temperatures below about 110 K and making them candidate cold traps for volatiles. Water ice at the optical surface had already been observed at Mercury and at Ceres, but the lunar case remained unresolved: several strong indications existed and none was unambiguously diagnostic of surface-exposed ice, and locations inferred by different methods did not always agree (Li et al., 2018, p. 1).

The paper sets out why each earlier lunar line of evidence stops short of a direct detection. Epithermal neutron counting estimates hydrogen in the upper tens of centimetres of regolith but cannot isolate the uppermost optical surface and cannot separate H2O from OH or H. Ultraviolet reflectance ratios from the LAMP instrument on LRO have been read as indicating H2O near the south pole, but hydroxyl can produce similar ultraviolet behaviour. Elevated 1,064 nm reflectance measured by LOLA near both poles is consistent with water ice, but fine particle sizes and low degrees of space weathering also raise reflectivity at that wavelength, so the interpretation is not unique.

The objective follows from that gap. Near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy measures molecular vibrations directly and can therefore discriminate H2O ice from OH and from water in other forms, including liquid, surface-adsorbed, and mineral-bound. The paper's aim is to test whether the diagnostic overtone and combination-mode features of H2O ice are present in shadowed lunar polar terrain.

### Methods and scope

The instrument is the Moon Mineralogy Mapper on Chandrayaan-1, at the time the highest spatial and spectral resolution near-infrared dataset available at global scale including the poles. Its full wavelength range is 0.46 to 2.98 micrometres, which the authors state is too limited to discriminate OH and H2O species using the fundamental vibrations in the 3 micrometre region; the study therefore works on overtone and combination modes. Radiance from optical period 2C was downloaded from the Planetary Data System and corrected for thermal contribution by the authors' own empirical model, an effect they describe as negligible in shaded polar regions. Processed data were binned at 128 pixels per degree and mosaicked from 75 to 90 degrees north and south in stereographic polar projection, and globally in simple cylindrical projection. A single M3 pixel is about 280 m by 280 m (Li et al., 2018, p. 1).

Shadowed pixels are those with local solar incidence angle of 90 degrees or greater, derived from a ray-tracing model run against LOLA topography and then verified by visual inspection of the M3 images. Reflectance inside shadow comes from sunlight scattered off crater walls and nearby topographic highs, so the regions analysed lie between directly illuminated ground and areas of deep permanent shadow. True reflectance is recovered as R_T = (I / (J / pi)) R alpha, where I is measured radiance, J is solar irradiance, R is bidirectional reflectance where the sunlight is scattered, and alpha is the amount of light scattered into the shaded region. R is assumed to be the average reflectance of illuminated terrain from 75 to 90 degrees. Alpha varies pixel to pixel with local topography and phase angle, and the authors set alpha to 1, stating that alpha introduces no absorptions and matters only for quantitative analysis of ice abundance, and that the emphasis of the study is a global and qualitative assessment (Li et al., 2018, p. 6).

Detection proceeds in two steps. Step one requires three absorption features whose centres and shoulder positions fall inside ranges tabulated from Clark's characterisation of ice absorptions for 50 to 2,000 micrometre particle sizes; cubic spline smoothing is applied to locate the features, and absorption strength is measured as the distance between the absorption centre and the continuum defined by the two shoulders. Step two computes the spectral angle between each surviving M3 spectrum and a laboratory spectrum of pure water frost, exploiting the fact that ice spectra have a strong blue continuum slope while typical lunar spectra redden because of nanophase iron from space weathering. A spectral angle below 30 degrees, a threshold set empirically from hand-picked ice-bearing and ice-free spectra, defines the final ice-bearing subset.

Water ice has four diagnostic absorptions near 1.1, 1.3, 1.5, and 2.0 micrometres in the 1.0 to 2.5 micrometre region. Only the three longer-wavelength and stronger features were used as detection criteria, and the analysis was confined to M3 bands between 1.0 and 2.5 micrometres for signal-to-noise reasons. Every wavelength in this summary was checked character by character against the source; the micrometre symbols extract as U+03BC in all 25 occurrences, with no ASCII substitution.

Three validation exercises are run. A random dataset matching the means and standard deviations of shaded polar M3 data, with five times as many pixels (about 65 x 10^6 points), was put through the same procedure. A null-hypothesis test asked whether detecting two of the three absorptions predicts the third. And all shaded M3 pixels at all latitudes, including equatorial terrain shaded near the terminator that is far too warm to hold surface ice, were tested as a negative control.

Candidate ice locations were then compared against three independent datasets: LOLA reflectance, LAMP ultraviolet band ratio, and Diviner annual maximum temperature.

### Key findings

The findings divide cleanly into what the instrument measured and what a model returned from those measurements, and the paper itself keeps the two apart. The measured quantity is the co-occurrence of three absorption features in shadowed polar pixels. Every abundance number in the paper is a spectral-mixing model output conditioned on an assumed mixing geometry, and the paper labels it that way.

What was detected.

- Three water-ice absorption features near 1.3, 1.5, and 2.0 micrometres occur together in a subset of shadowed M3 spectra. The fourth ice feature near 1.1 micrometres is present in the averages but was not used as a detection criterion.
- The sensed layer is the optical surface, stated as a depth of less than a few millimetres (Li et al., 2018, p. 1). This is a surface measurement and carries no depth profile and no column inventory. The paper explicitly contrasts it with epithermal neutron data, which senses the upper tens of centimetres and which it does not replace.
- Spatial resolution is one M3 pixel of about 280 m by 280 m. Data were binned at 128 pixels per degree.
- Areal scope searched is the shadowed fraction of 75 to 90 degrees north and south, plus shadowed pixels at all other latitudes as a control.
- No positive detections occur below 70 degrees latitude. Positives appear only within 20 degrees of a pole, and more than 90 percent of them lie within 10 degrees of a pole.
- About 0.2 percent of shaded M3 pixels returned positive detections, against a false-positive rate of 10^-4 percent measured on a size-matched random dataset of about 65 x 10^6 points. The mean spectrum of the random positives is distinct in shape from the mean spectrum of the M3 positives.
- Where two of the three absorptions were required, the third was always present in the average spectrum of the positive pixels, which is the null-hypothesis result supporting simultaneity.
- 93.2 percent of the M3-derived ice locations also show LOLA reflectance and ultraviolet ratio values consistent with water ice together with Diviner annual maximum surface temperature below 110 K. The paper calls the agreement of these four datasets a robust detection of water ice at the optical surface in those locations (Li et al., 2018, p. 4).
- Only about 3.5 percent of cold traps at both poles show ice signatures at all. This is the paper's areal statement and it is a fraction of cold traps, not a fraction of any pixel and not a mass fraction.
- Detections in the south cluster near the craters Haworth, Shoemaker, Sverdrup, and Shackleton; northern detections are more isolated. The south has more ice-bearing pixels than the north because it has more shaded terrain.
- Cold traps below 110 K that show NO ice exposure include Amundsen, Hedervari, Idel'son L, and Wiechert in the south, Bosch in the north, and most micro cold traps near 330 degrees longitude between 80 and 85 degrees north. The paper reports no bias in M3 data acquisition over those regions.
- Detections show no near-side versus far-side bias, which the authors read as evidence that earthshine did not create them.
- Candidate locations that fall outside the strict envelope, with maximum temperatures between 110 and 160 K or LOLA reflectance between 0.30 and 0.32 against a polar mean of 0.32, are attributed to noise in the M3 data.
- The 1.5 micrometre absorption minimum in the M3 ice-bearing spectra is shifted about 0.05 micrometres to longer wavelength relative to pure ice frost, a shift spanning two M3 spectral bands. The 1.5 micrometre feature is also broader and more asymmetric in the southern average than in the northern, which the authors read as suggesting larger grain sizes in the south.

What was inferred from modelling.

- Spectral mixing modelling of the weak 1.1 micrometre absorption and the blue continuum slope of the AVERAGED polar spectra indicates 30 weight percent ice or higher IF the ice is intimately mixed with regolith, or above 20 volume percent IF the ice occurs as patches within otherwise ice-free regolith (Li et al., 2018, p. 3). These are two alternative readings of the same spectra under two different assumed mixing geometries, not two independent measurements, and the abstract states the result as some ice-bearing pixels MAY contain about 30 weight percent.
- Radiative transfer modelling sets the theoretical feature-detectability floors. For intimate ice and regolith mixing, all four absorptions should appear above 30 weight percent ice and the three stronger ones above 5 weight percent. For linear areal or checkerboard subpixel mixing, all four should appear above 5 volume percent and the three stronger ones above 2 volume percent. The abstract compresses these to as little as 5 weight percent intimate or 2 volume percent areal. The paper states that actual detection limits in the M3 data depend on instrument response and signal-to-noise ratios and does not quantify them.
- Three candidate explanations are offered for the 0.05 micrometre band shift and none is selected. Water bound with minerals increases hydrogen bond strength and can shift the band, though no such shift was seen in a published mixture of 1 percent olivine with 99 percent water ice, and the authors note that mixture may not represent a lunar case where ice abundance may not exceed 30 percent. Low-density ice condensed from vapour shifts the OH fundamental stretch from about 2.94 micrometres (about 3,400 cm^-1) to about 3.09 micrometres (about 3,230 cm^-1), which would place the overtone near 1.55 micrometres and would be consistent with delivery by volatile-rich impactors or exospheric migration. The authors also state they cannot fully rule out that the shift is an artefact of the low signal-to-noise ratio of the M3 data.
- The patchy distribution and low abundance are attributed to a slow water supply combined with impact gardening. True polar wander of 2 to 3 Ga ago is invoked: at the cold traps that lack ice, ice stability depth is greater than zero under the hypothesised paleoaxis, which the authors read as indicating that surface ice is retained only at long-timescale cold traps. Simulations cited by the paper suggest a frost layer may survive no longer than 20 My at the lunar surface, and the authors state it is unclear whether solar wind bombardment, galactic cosmic rays, and interplanetary medium ultraviolet cause substantial loss over geological time.
- The lunar result is contrasted with cold traps on Mercury and Ceres, where deposits of nearly pure water ice, 100 percent, are inferred. The lunar equivalents are described as suggestive of much lower abundance, about 30 weight percent.

### Limitations

The paper's own stated limits are specific and several of them bear directly on how far its numbers can be carried.

Sensing depth bounds everything. The measurement reaches the optical surface only, a depth the paper puts at less than a few millimetres. Nothing in the paper constrains ice below that layer, and the paper does not present a depth profile, a column abundance, a deposit thickness, or a tonnage. Its own review of prior work notes that epithermal neutron counting reaches the upper tens of centimetres and that the two techniques sample different things.

The abundance analysis is not quantitatively calibrated. The scattering term alpha in the reflectance equation varies pixel to pixel with local topography and phase angle, and the authors set it to 1. They state in the Methods that alpha matters only for quantitative analysis of ice abundance and that the emphasis of the study is a global and qualitative assessment of water ice in the lunar polar regions (Li et al., 2018, p. 6). The abundance figures therefore come out of a study whose own reflectance normalisation was not set up for quantitative retrieval.

Abundance is model-conditioned and applies to averages. The 30 weight percent and 20 volume percent figures come from spectral mixing modelling of the AVERAGED northern and southern polar spectra, not from per-pixel retrievals, and the paper qualifies them with may be indicative of and may contain. Which of the two numbers applies depends on an unmeasured mixing geometry. No uncertainty band is reported on either.

Signal-to-noise is low throughout. Reflectance in shadow comes from indirect illumination, the M3 spectra of possible ice-bearing pixels are described as noisy, and about 0.2 percent positive detections sit above a 10^-4 percent random false-positive rate that is itself an estimate. The paper attributes its own weaker candidates, those between 110 and 160 K or with LOLA reflectance between 0.30 and 0.32, to noise.

Corroboration is asymmetric between the poles. LAMP data exist only for the south, so northern detections rest on M3, LOLA, and Diviner while southern detections rest on those three plus LAMP. This is stated in the Figure 4 legends and not in the running text.

The 1.5 micrometre band shift is unresolved. Mineral-bound water, low-density vapour-condensed ice, and low signal-to-noise are all left open, and the authors state there is no independent evidence that the shift is due to molecular water bound to lunar mineral surfaces, while also stating they cannot rule it out.

Detection thresholds in the actual data are not quantified. The 5 weight percent and 2 volume percent floors are radiative transfer model outputs; the paper says the real M3 detection limits depend on instrument response and signal-to-noise and stops there.

Scope is one instrument, one optical period, and the shadowed fraction of two polar caps. The supporting information carrying the absorption-position criteria (Table S1), the null-hypothesis tables (Tables S2 and S3), and eight supporting figures is not held on disk, so its contents were not read.
