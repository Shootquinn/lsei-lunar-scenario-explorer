## Abstract

Observational search for surficial water ice in lunar permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) using high-resolution ShadowCam imagery from the Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter (Danuri). The study exploits the high reflectance and forward-scattering optical signature of water ice to look for surface ice inside PSRs. It finds no evidence of widespread surface water ice above a detection limit of 20 to 30 wt%, while not ruling out widespread ice at lower content, and identifies a few small locations whose reflectance and scattering behavior could be consistent with greater than 10 wt% ice. The authors note that future missions with detection limits below 1 wt% would be needed to test the result.

---

### Background and objective

- PSRs near the poles of airless bodies (Moon, Mercury, Ceres) can cold-trap water ice at temperatures near or below 110 K. Mercury shows thick, nearly pure polar ice (tens of meters) from radar and MESSENGER; Ceres shows bright surface ice and neutron-suppressed subsurface ice. The Moon is the puzzling case: despite old, cold PSRs, radar, neutron, and visible observations have not definitively detected ice deposits.
- Prior lunar indirect evidence (LCROSS impact volatiles at Cabeus, LOLA albedo increases, LAMP and M3 surficial-ice detections) suggests patchy, low-abundance ice, but those detections are limited by low signal-to-noise, leaving open whether patchiness is real or a data artifact.
- The objective is to use ShadowCam's high spatial resolution and sensitivity in shadowed illumination to test whether lunar PSRs accumulate surface water ice as extensively as expected.

### Methods and scope

- ShadowCam onboard the Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter images PSR floors using secondary illumination, providing high-resolution reflectance across shadowed terrain.
- The search uses two optical properties diagnostic of water ice: high reflectance and forward-scattering behavior. Regions showing both are candidate ice locations.
- The Methods section also compares water-delivery and preservation factors between the Moon and Mercury (impact flux of water-rich comets and carbonaceous chondrites estimated a few to about 20 times higher on Mercury, offset by higher impact velocity and shorter H2O lifetime), to frame why the Moon may hold less surface ice than Mercury.

### Key findings

- No evidence was found of widespread surface water ice in lunar PSRs at abundances above the detection limit of 20 to 30 wt%.
- The result does not rule out widespread water ice at lower content (below the 20 to 30 wt% limit).
- A few small locations show both high reflectance and forward-scattering behavior, which could be consistent with greater than 10 wt% ice.
- The authors state that future missions with detection limits below 1 wt% would be needed to test these findings, since low-but-widespread ice remains possible.

### Limitations

- The detection limit of the method is 20 to 30 wt% water ice, so the study constrains only high-abundance surface ice; it is explicitly unable to detect or exclude low-content widespread ice.
- The method senses surficial (surface optical) ice, not subsurface ice, and relies on optical reflectance and scattering rather than direct composition measurement.
- Candidate ice locations are few and small; the greater than 10 wt% inference for them is a consistency argument from optical properties, not a confirmed measurement.

### Topic mapping

- The introduction contrasts lunar PSRs with the confirmed ice on Mercury and Ceres, reviews the inconsistent prior lunar detections (LCROSS, LOLA, LAMP, M3), and states the data-quality limitation motivating a higher-resolution search. The Methods section describes the ShadowCam reflectance and forward-scattering search and the Moon-versus-Mercury delivery and preservation comparison. The Results present the null finding above 20 to 30 wt%, the inability to exclude low-content ice, and the few small candidate greater-than-10 wt% locations, closing with the sub-1 wt% detection requirement for future tests.
