## Abstract

Proposes a conceptual system model for the formation and modification of lunar polar water ice, maps a derived Ice Favorability Index over both poles from 80 to 90 degrees latitude, and tests the model with 3-dimensional stochastic impact-gardening simulations over a 0.25 km2 area at 1 m horizontal and 0.1 m vertical resolution. Sources, capture, and retention terms are combined into a unitless index; simulations start from a pure surface ice layer and run 3.5 Gyr of gardening at present-day flux. Results are then passed through terrestrial mining software to produce block models and grade/tonnage curves against a defined selective mining unit. The paper's own stated position is that its outputs are relative and comparative rather than predictive of concentration or stratigraphy at any specific lunar location.

---

### Background and objective

Water ice is present at both lunar poles, within and probably peripheral to the permanently shadowed regions, which are the areas receiving no direct sunlight over the 18.6 year precessional cycle. The paper's opening premise is that existing and near-future orbital datasets cannot determine the horizontal and vertical distribution of that ice at the meter to hundred-meter scales that matter to a mining operation, and that no coherent geologic model has been put forward for how the deposits formed and evolved.

The paper names two limitations blocking prospecting and extraction. The first is the coverage of remote sensing data and the ability to interpret it uniquely. Ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared spectrometry senses only microns to millimeters of the upper regolith and is not expected to reach better than 100 m horizontal resolution in the foreseeable future. Neutron spectroscopy, principally the Lunar Prospector Neutron Spectrometer, reaches roughly a meter deep but at much coarser horizontal resolution. Radar from Mini-SAR and Mini-RF reaches meters deep at better spatial resolution but has proved ambiguous and non-unique: some studies argue for thick pure ice deposits while others set upper limits of 5-10 wt% ice mixed into the regolith, and later work has argued that radar anomalous craters are consistent with rocks rather than thick ice layers. The Chang'E-2 radiometer may have detected subsurface ice, but concentrations and depths are not clear from it.

The second limitation is the absence of a geologic model. Terrestrial ore deposit models rest on field mapping, quantitative exploration results such as drill core, and a process understanding of mineralization and subsequent modification. The paper frames the lunar case against the Joint Ore Reserves Committee code, reproducing the JORC classification pyramid as Figure 1 and noting that progressing down its vertical axis is a function of increasing geological knowledge and confidence, which is what the lunar case currently lacks (Cannon, 2020, p. 2).

Four stated goals follow: propose a system model for processes in and around the permanently shadowed regions; create regional-scale maps of ice Terrain Types and an Ice Favorability Index; carry out 3-dimensional stochastic impact simulations of those Terrain Types at mining scale; and define selective mining units and produce reference block models for a hypothetical extraction zone.

### Methods and scope

The system model treats three ice sources. Delivery by carbonaceous asteroids and, to a much lesser extent, comets is taken as dominant for thick surface layers. Volcanic outgassing from the interior is treated as sporadic and far more intense early. Solar wind and Earth wind implantation combined with micrometeoroid impacts is treated as forming only thin, transient layers vulnerable to removal. Because impact supply was much greater between 4.5 and 3.5 Ga than afterward, older surfaces are modeled as having received a greater cumulative supply, with a strong uptick between about 3.0 and 3.5 Ga.

Capture is split by cold trap size. Macro cold traps are larger than 10^4 m2 and are treated as more or less synonymous with the classically mapped permanently shadowed regions; micro cold traps are smaller than 10^4 m2 and arise where rough topography in an otherwise illuminated region creates small trapping areas. Seasonally shadowed regions are noted as greatly expanding the area where ice can be captured, with retention in them identified as the problem. An ice pump mechanism, in which a narrow band of temperature conditions lets ice diffuse down the regolith column after surface trapping, is noted with the authors' caveat that its efficiency may be limited.

Retention defines the three Terrain Types used throughout the paper. Terrain Type 1 is a macro cold trap with ice stable at 0 m depth. Terrain Type 2 is micro cold traps with ice stable at or above 1 m depth. Terrain Type 3 is micro cold traps with ice stable deeper than 1 m. The division is justified on natural breaks in published ice stability depth histograms at 0 m and around 1 m, and on a change in extraction strategy, since infrared and microwave heating is expected to be effective in Terrain Types 1 and 2 but not in Terrain Type 3.

Impact gardening is the modifying process the paper treats as dominant at these scales, over space weathering, volcanism, and tectonism. Its modeled effects on an emplaced ice layer are puncturing and comminution of pure layers, partial vaporization with most vapor escaping the cold trap or the Moon, downward and outward displacement of non-vaporized ice into dry regolith, excavation of both dry and icy material to the surface, seismic compaction at depth, and formation of icy agglutinates near the surface. The paper states that regolith in permanently shadowed regions was unlikely to have been cemented like permafrost because gardening continued.

Obliquity and true polar wander are carried as complications. The paper adopts roughly 5.5 degrees of true polar wander in ancient history, producing 9 permutations of Terrain Type evolution across the poles that are mapped in Figure 3.

The Ice Favorability Index is defined in Appendix A as a unitless product of a Source term, a Capture term, and a Retention term, computed twice, once for the paleopole and once for the present-day pole, and blended by a parameter for the fraction of total ice deposited before true polar wander. Inputs are published ice stability depth maps, published latitude-dependent micro cold trap areal fractions digitized from their source plots, a published impact flux chronology, and a crater retention age estimated by a moving-window count of craters larger than 1 to 2 km in six size bins, converted to model ages against isochrons at 0.1 Gyr intervals. The index is computed on a stereographic grid from 80 degrees to each pole, 1000 by 1000 cells, at approximately 532 m per cell.

The 3-dimensional stochastic cratering simulations use a grid of 500 by 500 by Z cells representing 0.25 km2 at the lunar poles, with 1 m horizontal and 0.1 m vertical resolution, described as a reasonable size for a small to medium-sized mining outpost. Each cell carries a fractional ice content in weight percent. Craters between 20 and 100 m in diameter are placed randomly using a published production function, with depth-to-diameter ratio 0.2, transient cavity diameter 0.84D, ejecta lobes extending to 0.1 of the transient cavity diameter, ejecta thrown to 1 crater diameter with thickness falling off as 0.14 R^0.74 (r/R)^-3.0, and a linear diffusive erosion rate of 0.4 mm/Myr. Sub-resolution gardening is parameterized from published results as full homogenization of the upper 10 cm, partial mixing of the upper 50 cm, and thorough mixing of the upper 1.5 m, all on 1 Ga timescales.

Initial conditions differ by scenario. The Terrain Type 1 run begins with a 10 cm layer of pure ice over the entire grid surface with ice stable throughout the column, and 3.5 Gyr of gardening at present-day flux is simulated with a timestep of 10^5 years. The micro cold trap runs seed the surface with a 5 percent areal fraction of cold traps, each starting with 10 cm of pure ice, and let ice stability depth increase linearly away from those traps so that 40 percent of the grid has subsurface cold trap area. Terrain Type 2 sets stability depth outside the traps at 50 cm, halfway between the 0 and 1 m bounds; Terrain Type 3 sets it at infinite depth. Cold trap locations are held fixed over time, which the authors state is not physically realistic because impacts would both destroy and create micro cold traps.

Two selective mining unit sizes are proposed: 1 by 1 by 0.1 m for drilling and excavation methods, and 10 by 10 by 1 m for heating methods using volatile capture tents. Simulation output was exported to the terrestrial mining package Leapfrog Geo, and block models and grade/tonnage curves were produced against the 10 by 10 by 1 m unit.

### Key findings

Every concentration number below is a simulation or model output, not a measurement. The paper is explicit at the opening of its discussion that the system model and simulations "cannot be used to predict quantitative ice concentrations or stratigraphies for specific locations" on the Moon (Cannon, 2020, p. 7), and it states in its methods that the significance of the simulation output lies in the changes over time, the resulting distributions, and the relative differences between runs, rather than in the absolute weight-percent values (Cannon, 2020, p. 5).

The paper's headline concentration result is a set of whole-column means. After 3.5 Gyr of gardening from an initial 10 cm pure ice layer, the simulations end with column mean ice contents of 1.2 wt% for Terrain Type 1, 0.04 wt% for Terrain Type 2, and 0.01 wt% for Terrain Type 3, with no thick coherent layers remaining (Cannon, 2020, p. 7). The scale attached to these numbers is the full modeled regolith column over a 0.25 km2 grid.

The depth profiles carry different numbers at a different scale, and the difference matters. Figure 7a plots mean ice concentration against depth for Terrain Type 1 at seven times from 0.5 to 3.5 Gyr, on a linear axis running 0 to about 27 wt% and a depth axis running to 300 cm. Figure 7b compares the three Terrain Types at the end of the run on a logarithmic axis from 0.01 to 10 wt% over the same depth range. Readings from both panels follow.

- Figure 7a, decay of the maximum with time: the maximum mean concentration falls from about 26 wt% at 0.5 Gyr, to about 12 wt% at 1.5 Gyr, to about 10 wt% at 2.0 Gyr, to about 7.5 to 8 wt% at 3.5 Gyr, while the depth at which it occurs migrates downward to roughly 50 cm.
- Figure 7a, the 3.5 Gyr profile: about 5.5 wt% at the very top of the plotted range, rising to the maximum near 50 cm, then falling below 5 wt% by about 150 cm and approaching 0.5 wt% at 300 cm.
- Figure 7b, Terrain Type 1: near 8 wt% between about 50 and 100 cm, declining to about 0.4 wt% at 300 cm.

- Figure 7b, Terrain Type 3: a maximum near 0.09 wt% around 100 cm, declining to about 0.008 wt% at 300 cm.

The ordering of the three Terrain Types spans about two and a half orders of magnitude in mean concentration at every depth, which is the paper's stated favorability ranking of Terrain Type 1 over 2 over 3. The paper attributes the Terrain Type 1 advantage to greater cold trap area and to ice being stable throughout the column over the entire grid, and the Terrain Type 2 advantage over Terrain Type 3 to comparatively shallower stability depths.

Depth is the paper's central practical finding and it is stated as a general trend rather than a location-specific prediction. In all three Terrain Types the highest average ice concentrations occur at some depth below the surface, at least 50 cm, with the maximum located about 50 to 100 cm deep (Cannon, 2020, pp. 6-7). This holds even where ice is stable at the very surface, because gardening is more efficient at shallow depths where smaller impacts are more abundant, and because erosive space weathering processes not included in the simulations act at the upper surface. The abstract states the best deposits for extraction likely exist tens of cm deep or more. The paper links the resulting dry-over-wet structure to the favored 2-layer model from neutron spectroscopy and to a centimeters-thick desiccated layer inferred from LADEE data.

The distribution finding is that there is no Earth-analogue ore body. High-concentration material in the simulations is randomly located rather than spatially clustered, and gardening is expected to drive ice distributions toward homogeneity at all spatial scales. Figure 8 shows the Terrain Type 1 block model cropped to the inner 300 by 300 m at a block size of 10 by 10 by 1 m, with panel b filtering to blocks above 15 wt% ice. The filtered panel is raster-only; reading it visually, roughly 40 isolated high-yield blocks are scattered across the 300 by 300 m footprint with no coherent body, and the block colour scale runs from 0 to 30 or more wt% ice with a single block reaching the top of that scale.

The Ice Favorability Index is a ranking, not a concentration. It is unitless, and the paper states in Appendix A that it is not intended as a quantitative measure of ice resources or reserves and should not be used in isolation to select landing sites or mining locations, its stated intent being to guide further site analysis and ground prospecting (Cannon, 2020, p. 8). Figure 4, the index map for both poles from 80 to 90 degrees latitude, is raster-only; its colour bar is qualitative, running from a minus sign to a plus sign with no numeric scale printed, so no index value can be read off the map at any location.

Two named sites anchor the index. Cabeus crater, the LCROSS impact site, carries some of the highest index values at either pole, attributed to its ancient age and to having large macro cold trap areas with near-surface ice stability at both the paleopole and the present-day pole. On the strength of that, the paper offers its one bridge from index to concentration: the modeled LCROSS water amounts at Cabeus, cited as approximately 5 percent, may be a reasonable conservative estimate for the maximum amount of ice to be expected in regional-scale polar deposits (Cannon, 2020, pp. 4-5). That statement is an extrapolation from a single modeled point measurement to a regional scale, offered as a face-value reading of the index, and the paper attaches no uncertainty band to it. Shackleton crater carries some of the lowest index values of any south-pole permanently shadowed region, attributed to its young relative age and to its not having been a permanently shadowed region at the modeled paleopole. Substantial areas of elevated index lie outside the permanently shadowed regions themselves, where ice could have been captured in micro cold traps and may sit at shallow depth in the regolith column.

Terrain Type persistence across true polar wander is rare. Regions where ice was stable at the surface at both the paleopole and the present-day pole, which the paper identifies as most favorable for rich deposits, occupy less than 1 percent of the area from 80 to 90 degrees latitude, and the paper notes those regions are deep inside large steep-walled craters that may only be accessible with nuclear-powered architectures.

Concentration and inventory figures the paper carries from other work are listed below. Each is a cited model output or a cited detection reported by the authors, not a result of this study, and each is recorded here with the status the paper itself gives it.

- Radar-derived upper limits of 5-10 wt% ice mixed in the regolith. Cited as the position of one group of studies, against another group arguing for thick pure ice; the paper reports later work concluding that the radar anomalous craters behind the thick-ice reading are consistent with rocks.
- A steady-state concentration of approximately 4 wt% ice within the regolith column from combined space weathering and solar wind implantation. A cited calculation, and the paper immediately qualifies it by noting that additional processes are needed to go from H to OH and H2O, which potentially limits the potency of this source.
- Delivery to the poles of up to 5.0 x 10^14 kg H2O from asteroids and 7.8 x 10^13 kg from comets per Ga. A cited model, with the paper's own caveat that the source used much lower present-day impact fluxes.
- Nonuniform ice layers of mm to cm thickness deposited by a 2 km diameter pure ice impactor. A cited model of a single impact event.
- Volcanic outgassing sufficient to create ice deposits approximately 1.5 m thick if emplaced instantaneously, from suggested activity spikes at approximately 3.8 and 3.5 Ga. A cited model, which the paper notes is sufficient on its own to explain the neutron spectroscopy data, and which it qualifies by observing that collisional atmospheres may not have formed if volcanism was less clustered than proposed.
- A predicted micro cold trap areal fraction of approximately 5-15 percent for polar regions above 80 degrees latitude. A cited model, and the input to the paper's own capture term.
- Approximately 5.5 degrees of true polar wander in ancient history, near 4 Ga. Cited as compelling evidence, and adopted as a modeling assumption.
- Ice stability depths spanning ice stable at the upper surface down to 2.5 m deep. Cited modeled depths against sublimation on billion-year timescales, supplied to the authors directly and used as the retention input.

Two further practical trends are stated. Intercrater plains are generally more favorable for hosting ice than the ejecta, rims, and floors of small craters, because the initial ice layer there has not been disrupted, which the paper notes is fortuitous since small craters are trafficability hazards. And there may be an optimum surface age, because younger and less gardened surfaces retain less mixed ice while supply rates from impacts and volcanism declined sharply until plateauing around 3.0 Ga, so young craters such as Shackleton are probably not ideal for lack of supply.

The Appendix A formulations are reproduced below for reuse, in the article's own notation and precision.

- Micro cold trap areal fractions were digitized from their source plots for the 15 degree roughness case, describing roughness on roughly 10 m scales, and fitted as third-degree polynomials in latitude x in degrees. The surface fraction is 9.985 x 10^-5 x^3 - 0.02231 x^2 + 1.663 x - 41.32. The subsurface fraction is -4.925 x 10^-5 x^3 + 0.01305 x^2 - 1.108 x + 30.53.
- The crater chronology function is N(1) = 5.44 x 10^-14 [exp(6.93T) - 1] + 8.38 x 10^-4 T, where N(1) is the number of craters at or above 1 km diameter per km2 and T is crater accumulation time in Gyr.
- The relative cratering rate is the derivative of that function, 3.76992 x 10^-13 [exp(6.93T)] + 8.38 x 10^-4.
- The index itself is the product of a Source term, a Capture term, and a Retention term, computed once at the paleopole and once at the present-day pole and blended by the fraction of total ice deposited before true polar wander.
- The Capture term is 1 where ice is stable at the surface, and the surface cold trap areal fraction otherwise.
- The Retention term deals with the subsurface only, through a stability quantity equal to the subsurface areal fraction plus (1 minus that fraction) multiplied by a normalized ice stability depth, where the normalization runs from 1 for ice stable at the surface to 0 for ice stable at 2.5 m or deeper.
- The Source terms are integrations of the relative cratering rate, from a cell's modeled age to the wander event for the paleopole case and from the wander event or the modeled age, whichever is younger, to the present for the present-day case, both normalized to a maximum of 1 against the oldest cell in the grid. The paleopole source term is set to 0 for cells younger than the wander event.
- Placing true polar wander at approximately 3.9 Ga gave the best match between the index and published epithermal neutron suppression maps, yielding a pre-wander deposition fraction of 0.66. The index is identified as version 1.

### Limitations

The authors state their own boundary plainly at the head of the discussion: the model and simulations cannot predict quantitative ice concentrations or stratigraphies for specific lunar locations, and what they offer are general trends. The methods section adds that the absolute weight-percent outputs are not the point, and that the relative differences between runs are.

The initial condition is a single simple configuration chosen from a large number of free parameters and unknowns: pure ice over dry regolith, subjected to gardening. The authors name what is not included and intend for future work, specifically stochastic ice deposition events and changes in ice stability depth during a run, while stating they do not expect the basic behavior to change.

Cold trap locations are held fixed through the micro cold trap runs, which the authors state is not physically realistic because impacts would destroy existing micro cold traps and create new ones. Distal mixing effects, heterogeneous crater rays, and the detailed effects of small crater destruction are outside the scope. Erosive space weathering processes at the very surface, including sublimation and sputtering, are not in the simulations, and the authors note their omission would make the modeled desiccated layer thinner than reality.

The paper carries an unresolved tension in its own source literature about whether surface ice persists at all. One cited work challenges the concept of ice stability at cold trap surfaces, calculating that surface ice layers persist for thousands of years rather than billions because of space weathering. The paper states it remains unclear how a dynamic and possibly transient surface layer relates to the deeper diffuse ice inferred from LCROSS, the Lunar Prospector Neutron Spectrometer, and the Chang'E-2 radiometer, particularly if a desiccated layer separates the two reservoirs. Its own scope is declared to be the greater depths rather than the space-weathered surface.

The Ice Favorability Index is a ranking with no numeric scale on its published map, is explicitly not a resource or reserve measure, and is labelled version 1. Its true polar wander timing is described in the paper's own figure caption as poorly constrained, and the value adopted was selected to fit published neutron suppression maps rather than measured independently.

No uncertainty bands are printed on any simulation result, on the column means, on the depth profiles, or on the grade/tonnage curves. Every run is a single realization of a stochastic process and no ensemble spread is shown.

### Topic mapping

Section 1 states the two limitations blocking lunar ice prospecting, surveys the remote sensing record with its depth and resolution bounds, introduces the JORC framing through Figure 1, and lists the paper's four goals. Section 2 builds the system model: 2.1 covers the three ice sources and their decline over time, 2.2 defines macro and micro cold traps at the 10^4 m2 boundary and covers seasonal shadow and the ice pump, 2.3 defines the three Terrain Types by ice stability depth and develops impact gardening as the dominant modifier, 2.4 covers obliquity and true polar wander with the 9 Terrain Type permutations mapped in Figure 3, and 2.5 introduces the Ice Favorability Index and discusses Cabeus and Shackleton against Figure 4.

Section 3 covers the simulations: 3.1 gives the grid, resolution, crater parameterization shown in Figure 5, gardening parameterization, and the three scenario initial conditions; 3.2 reports the results shown in Figures 6 and 7, including the depth of maximum concentration and the ordering of the three Terrain Types; 3.3 defines the two selective mining unit sizes and produces the block model in Figure 8 and the grade/tonnage curves in Figure 9.

Section 4 draws four trends: 4.1 that the best deposits sit at depth, 4.2 that there are no Earth-analogue ore bodies and that column means are low, 4.3 that intercrater plains are more favorable than small crater ejecta, rims, and floors, and 4.4 that there may be an optimum surface age balancing supply against gardening exposure. Section 5 summarizes and states that the block models are included as reference cases for planning lunar mining architectures.

Appendix A gives the full Ice Favorability Index formulation: A.1 the ice stability depth map inputs and their 2.5 m range, A.2 the micro cold trap areal fraction polynomials, A.3 the impact flux chronology function and its derivative, A.4 the crater retention age method, A.5 the index product and its paleopole and present-day blending, A.6 the source term integration, A.7 the capture term, and A.8 the retention term.

Figures carry content the text does not state. Figure 2 is the system model overview, with impact rate over time on a logarithmic axis from 1 to 10^4 normalized to the present, the true polar wander marker placed near 3.9 Ga, relative asteroid and comet contributions shown as areas, the three Terrain Type block diagrams with their stability depth definitions, and a three-stage cross section running from a thick impact-deposited ice layer, through gardening dispersal and space weathering erosion, to a present state of dynamic surface layer over desiccated layer over ice-rich regolith at depth.
