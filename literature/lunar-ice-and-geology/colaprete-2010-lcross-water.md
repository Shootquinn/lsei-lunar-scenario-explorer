# Detection of Water in the LCROSS Ejecta Plume

## Abstract

Reports direct spacecraft-impact measurements of water and other volatiles ejected from the
permanently shadowed lunar crater Cabeus by the LCROSS mission's Centaur impactor (9 October
2009). Near-infrared (NIR) and ultraviolet/visible (UV/Vis) spectrometers on a trailing
Shepherding Spacecraft recorded absorption and emission features over a 4-minute post-impact
window. Derives a water-ice concentration in the target regolith, a total water mass within the
instrument field of view, and relative abundances of several co-ejected volatile species, and
compares the result with prior orbital neutron-spectrometer estimates for the same site.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Orbital neutron-spectrometer data (Lunar Prospector Neutron Spectrometer, LPNS) had previously
indicated elevated hydrogen concentrations within 20 degrees of the lunar poles, and later
Earth- and spacecraft-based observations (cited as refs. 3-5) suggested trace ice or bound OH
elsewhere on the Moon. Clementine radar data were consistent with volumetric scattering by
relatively pure (~90%) water ice 1-2 m thick in Shackleton crater, but neither the chemical form
nor the origin of the hydrogen signal had been established from orbit. The Lunar Crater
Observation and Sensing Satellite (LCROSS) mission was designed to provide direct ground truth
at a single location by crashing a spent rocket stage into a permanently shadowed region (PSR)
and observing the ejected debris with instruments on a trailing spacecraft. The target, the
south-polar crater Cabeus, was selected using two principal criteria: the altitude at which
ejecta would first be illuminated by sunlight, and prior evidence of elevated hydrogen from LPNS
and the Lunar Exploration Neutron Detector (LEND). This paper reports the NIR and UV/Vis
spectrometer observations of the resulting ejecta plume.

### Methods and scope

The impactor was the spent Centaur upper stage of the Atlas V rocket that had carried both the
Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and LCROSS to the Moon. After LRO separated, the LCROSS
Shepherding Spacecraft (SSc) controlled the Centaur for about 4 months, performed a solar
bake-out of residual water, calibrated instruments, and targeted the impact. About 9 hours
before impact the SSc separated from the Centaur, braked to build a 4-minute separation, and
oriented its instruments toward the target. The Centaur struck Cabeus on 9 October 2009; the SSc
carried nine instruments (cameras, spectrometers, a radiometer) and began recording about 1 hour
before impact. This paper analyzes the nadir-viewing NIR spectrometer (1.25-2.35 micrometers,
resolving power 35-70) and UV/Vis spectrometer (0.26-0.65 micrometers, resolving power 300-850),
both with a coaligned 1-degree field of view (FOV). Water and other volatile abundances were
derived by fitting a linear mixing model (library absorbers: H2O gas and ice, OH, CO2, light
hydrocarbons, sulfur species, plus a blackbody continuum) to relative-reflectance NIR spectra
referenced against a 180-scan preimpact baseline, using chi-squared minimization and HITRAN
absorption cross sections. UV/Vis water evidence came from a hydroxyl (OH) emission feature near
0.3093 micrometers, measured against a local continuum and a preimpact reference. Ejecta cloud
mass, grain size, and optical depth were modeled with the multistream radiative-transfer code
DISORT (Mie theory, log-normal grain-size distribution, silicon-dioxide refractive indices,
grain density 3000 kg/m^3), calibrated against UV/Vis brightness and visible-camera imagery of
cloud diameter.

### Key findings

- **Headline water concentration:** the mean water-ice concentration in the regolith at the
  Cabeus impact site is **5.6 +/- 2.9% by mass (1 SD)**. This is a propagated-error average of
  the total-water-mass-to-dust-mass ratio computed independently for three post-impact time
  periods (Table 1): 4.5 +/- 1.4% (0-23 s), 6.4 +/- 1.7% (23-30 s), and 7.2 +/- 1.9% (123-180 s).
  The paper states this figure explicitly in both the abstract and body text; both bounds of the
  uncertainty are reported here as printed, unrounded.

- **Measurement method for the concentration figure:** derived from (a) NIR absorption/emission
  features attributed to water vapor and ice near 1.4, 1.87, 1.9-2.0, and 2.2 micrometers,
  separated from mineral-bound OH and metal-bound OH contributions by spectral fitting, and (b)
  a UV/Vis OH emission line at 0.3093 micrometers (band interpreted as either photo-dissociative
  emission from an expanding, ~1000 K water-vapor cloud, or resonant fluorescence). The
  water-mass estimates from each period were divided by an independently modeled dust mass in
  sunlight (from UV/Vis cloud-brightness radiative-transfer fits) to yield the mass-fraction
  ("total water %") column in Table 1.

- **Total water mass in the field of view:** the maximum combined water vapor + ice mass
  observed within the instrument FOV was **155 +/- 12 kg**, occurring during the 23-30 s period
  when ice-grain absorption was strongest (gas 24.5 +/- 8.1 kg + ice 131 +/- 8.3 kg). Water vapor
  column density during the 123-180 s period was ~5.1 (+/- 1.4) x 10^19 molecules/m^2 (1 SD),
  corresponding to a vapor mass of 53 +/- 3 kg (text) / 52.5 +/- 2.6 kg (Table 1) averaged over
  an FOV area ranging from ~3.8 x 10^7 to 2 x 10^7 m^2 across that period. Ice mass during
  123-180 s averaged 15.8-16 +/- 2.2/2 kg. Averaged across the full observation, gas mass was
  53 +/- 15 kg and ice mass 68 +/- 10 kg (Table 1, "Average" row), against an average dust mass
  of 2175 +/- 544 kg. An independent cross-check using the OH prompt-emission band strength at
  impact +180 s gave a total OH column of 2.2 x 10^16 molecules/m^2, corresponding to
  23 +/- 11 kg of water vapor, stated as consistent with the NIR-derived value for about the same
  period.

- **Ejecta/dust mass and impact geometry:** total ejecta cloud mass in sunlight was estimated at
  ~4300-6500 kg under a uniform-fill assumption, or 2500 kg (low) to 3800 kg (high) once a 58%
  area-filling factor is applied to correct for FOV underfilling before ~20 s, giving an average
  of 3150 +/- 790 kg. Visible optical depth (before ~30 s, area-weighted grain radius 2.5
  micrometers) was 0.002-0.003, implying a grain number density of ~1.4 x 10^6 to
  2.1 x 10^6 grains/m^2. After ~30 s, total cloud brightness dropped by a factor of ~3.2 and
  stayed roughly constant for ~200 s more; optical depth in this later period was ~9.3 x 10^-4,
  corresponding to a cloud mass of 1850 kg, stated as consistent with LRO Diviner-derived optical
  depth ~90 s after impact (cited as ref. 23). The debris cloud reached sunlight ~1 s after
  impact; UV/Vis peak brightness occurred ~17 s after impact; elevated brightness persisted
  substantially above preimpact background for a further ~223 s. The height debris needed to
  climb to reach sunlight at the Centaur impact site/time was 833 m, a function of Cabeus's
  comparatively low latitude (~-81.5 degrees S) and a cleft in the crater-rim topography on the
  sunward side at impact time; this geometry also gave the nadir spectrometers a dark background.
  Floor temperatures in the selected impact area were reported as less than 50 K, described as
  making the site "a likely cold trap for volatiles." A hot (~1000 K) vapor cloud emerged
  immediately after impact, followed by low- and high-angle ejecta with thermal emission
  consistent with ~300-1000 K.

- **Impactor mass, velocity, energy, and final crater size are not reported in this paper.** The
  text identifies the impactor only as "the spent upper stage of the Atlas V rocket, the
  Centaur," and cites a companion paper (ref. 20: Schultz et al., *Science 330*, 468, 2010) for
  cratering-experiment details. A fragment of that companion article ("The LCROSS Cratering
  Experiment") is appended at the end of this same PDF (a separate REPORTS-section paper by
  Schultz, Hermalyn, Colaprete, Ennico, Shirley, and Marshall) and states in its own abstract
  that the Shepherding Spacecraft imaged "a 25-to-30-meter-diameter crater" before impact; that
  figure originates from the companion paper, not from the Colaprete et al. water-detection
  analysis summarized here.

- **Other volatiles detected (Table 2), reported as column density and abundance relative to
  H2O gas** (H2O column 5.1 (+/-1.4) x 10^19 molecules/cm^2 = 100%, from the spectral fit for
  the 123-180 s period): H2S 8.5 (+/-0.9) x 10^18 cm^-2 (16.75%); NH3 3.1 (+/-1.5) x 10^18
  (6.03%); SO2 1.6 (+/-0.4) x 10^18 (3.19%); C2H4 1.6 (+/-1.7) x 10^18 (3.12%); CO2
  1.1 (+/-1.0) x 10^18 (2.17%); CH3OH 7.8 (+/-4.2) x 10^17 (1.55%); CH4 3.3 (+/-3.0) x 10^17
  (0.65%); OH 1.7 (+/-0.4) x 10^16 (0.03%). These species correspond to the abstract's summary
  categories of "light hydrocarbons, sulfur-bearing species, and carbon dioxide." The paper notes
  that the H2S/H2O, NH3/H2O, SO2/H2O, and CO2/H2O ratios are higher, in some cases by orders of
  magnitude, than ratios reported in comets, the interstellar medium, or predicted from gas-gas
  chemistry in protoplanetary disks, and are instead more comparable to abundances seen in
  molecular hot cores -- interpreted as consistent with (but not proof of) some in-situ volatile
  formation on cold grain surfaces within the Cabeus PSR, in addition to possible cometary or
  asteroidal delivery. Separately, the paper cites an LRO Lyman Alpha Mapping Project (LAMP)
  result (ref. 31) of ~140 kg of H2 released at impact, corresponding to an initial regolith H2
  concentration of 1.4% -- reported as an external comparison, not derived from the instruments
  analyzed in this paper.

- **Cross-comparison with neutron data:** LEND neutron data indicate the Cabeus floor contains
  ">2 weight percent water," described as "about a third the value derived from LCROSS" (i.e.,
  relative to the 5.6% LCROSS figure). The paper lists four caveats on this comparison: (i) the
  LEND result is strongly model-dependent (e.g., assumptions about desiccated-top-layer
  thickness); (ii) the LCROSS sample depth was possibly deeper than neutron spectroscopy can
  effectively sense (neutron sampling limited to shallower than ~0.7 m); (iii) persistent
  conduction of impact heat into the regolith caused continued volatile release, complicating
  direct comparison; (iv) the LEND and LPNS instrument footprints were never smaller than 10 km
  across, so spatial gradients finer than 10 km are smoothed out in the neutron data. Combining
  the LCROSS result with LEND and LPNS, the paper states this "suggests that there is some
  spatial heterogeneity at scales <10 km."

- **Representativeness of Cabeus for PSRs generally:** the paper frames the LCROSS mission's
  goal explicitly as providing "ground truth at one location -- the crater Cabeus." It makes no
  stated claim that the measured 5.6 +/- 2.9% concentration, or the volatile-abundance ratios,
  generalize to other permanently shadowed regions on the Moon. The only generalization offered
  concerns sub-Cabeus spatial heterogeneity (the <10 km-scale statement above), not
  cross-PSR representativeness.

### Limitations

The paper states its own caveats explicitly. It is a single-site measurement: LCROSS provided
ground truth for Cabeus only, and no claim is made that this concentration applies elsewhere.
Mixing-ratio estimates for the earliest period (0-23 s) are described as "likely to be
overestimates," because the ejecta cloud was still smaller than the instrument FOV during that
interval (cloud filled about half the FOV at 8 s and the entire FOV only by ~20 s), which
inflates the apparent water fraction relative to the observed (partial) ejecta mass. Vapor- and
ice-mass values derived before the cloud fully filled the FOV (~20 s) are labeled lower limits,
since some water lay outside the field of view. The analysis cannot cleanly separate water vapor
sublimating from ice grains in sunlight from vapor sublimating off the heated regolith at and
near the crater; both sources are folded into the same mixing-ratio estimate. The total water
vapor mass carries a stated uncertainty of +/-28% (1 SD), dominated by ~28% uncertainty in the
radiance measurement at the 1.87-micrometer band center; independent per-parameter fit errors on
water vapor are given asymmetrically as +5/-5% (68% confidence), +7/-10% (95% confidence), and
+12/-24% (99.7% confidence). The 1.5-micrometer ice-feature fit is uncertain to ~14% (root-mean-
square of an 11% fitting-uncertainty term and a 9% radiance-uncertainty term); the UV/Vis
continuum optical-depth fit is uncertain to ~25% (5% fitting uncertainty, 24% radiance-
measurement uncertainty). Derived masses depend on modeling choices: an assumed grain-size
distribution (area-weighted mean radii 1-35 micrometers, variance 1.5), silicon-dioxide
refractive indices as an analog for grain composition, an assumed grain density of 3000 kg/m^3,
and a five-layer DISORT radiative-transfer geometry; the paper notes the ice-mass result is
"mostly insensitive" to how ice is arranged among the modeled layers, given the low optical
depths involved, but the underlying grain-size and density assumptions are not independently
verified. Some UV/Vis emission features are noted elsewhere in the broader LCROSS instrument set
as not yet confidently identified (that discussion belongs to the companion cratering-experiment
paper rather than this one). The neutron-spectrometer comparison (LEND, LPNS) is limited by
model dependence, a sampling-depth mismatch with LCROSS, ongoing impact-heat-driven volatile
release, and coarse (>10 km) instrument footprints that smooth finer spatial structure.
