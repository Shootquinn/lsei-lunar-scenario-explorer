# Speyerer & Robinson 2013 - Persistently Illuminated Regions at the Lunar Poles
## Comprehensive Technical Summary

## Abstract

Derives illumination statistics for the lunar polar regions directly from Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) Wide Angle Camera (WAC) and Narrow Angle Camera (NAC) images rather than from digital-elevation-model (DEM) lighting simulations. Covers one full lunar year (12 lunations, 15 February 2010 to 5 February 2011) at 100 m/pixel within roughly 2 degrees of each pole, supplemented by meter-scale NAC mosaics near each solstice. Reports localized sites illuminated for up to nearly 94% of the year with a longest single eclipse of 43 hours, identifies specific named sites (Shackleton crater rim, Whipple crater rim, Hinshelwood crater rim, Peary crater rim) with coordinates and illumination percentages, and cross-validates its image-derived maps against three prior DEM-based simulation studies (Noda et al. 2008, Bussey et al. 2010, Mazarico et al. 2010), finding both peaks the simulations missed and shadowed pockets the simulations had marked as lit.

---

### Background and objective

The Moon's spin axis is tilted 1.5 degrees relative to the ecliptic normal (versus Earth's 23.3 degrees plus or minus 1.3 degrees), which leaves many polar crater interiors and depressions in permanent shadow while select crater rims and local massifs stay sunlit for an extended portion of the year (p. 122). Prior work established that permanently shadowed regions are cold traps (approximately 220 K plus or minus 10 K) of interest for volatile prospecting, while nearby topographic highs offer a benign thermal environment and extended solar access for long-duration missions and outposts (p. 122). The paper's stated objective is to use LROC's multi-temporal, high-resolution imaging to identify and characterize persistently illuminated regions near both poles, complementing earlier Clementine-image and DEM-simulation studies with direct observation of actual surface lighting.

Earlier illumination work is summarized in Section 2: the 1994 Clementine mission (71-day orbit, 19 February to 3 May 1994) produced UVVIS images at 250-500 m ground sampling distance, from which Shoemaker et al. (1994) estimated at least 30,000 km2 of the south polar region in persistent shadow, and Nozette et al. (1996) built composite illumination maps from more than 50 summed UVVIS images per pole (p. 122). Bussey et al. (1999, 2005) further quantified illumination from the same Clementine dataset. Clementine's approximately 5-hour orbital period limited that dataset to 59 south-pole and 53 north-pole images with roughly 10-hour temporal spacing (p. 123). Later studies used DEM-based lighting simulations from Earth-based radar interferometry (Margot et al. 1999; Garrick-Bethell et al. 2005; Bryant 2009) and from laser altimetry, specifically Kaguya's Laser ALTimeter (LALT, Noda et al. 2008; Bussey et al. 2010) and LRO's Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA, Mazarico et al. 2010).

### Methods and scope

LROC WAC has a 90-degree field of view (monochrome mode) giving a swath approximately 100 km wide at 100 m ground sampling distance; the 50 km mapping orbit lets it image each pole every 1.9 hours nominally (average observed spacing 2.1 hours due to spacecraft/instrument anomalies) (p. 123). As of 15 September 2012, LROC had acquired 24,270 WAC and 31,673 NAC observations within 2 degrees of the poles. Over the one-year study period the WAC acquired 3,548 north-pole and 3,807 south-pole observations; after adding images with matching lighting geometry from other times to fill temporal gaps ("temporal fill," 234 north / 229 south additions), the counts rose to 3,782 and 4,036 respectively (Table 1). Table 1 gives, for each pole with and without temporal fill: number of observations, mean and longest orbit-count temporal gap, and mean and longest gap in hours. With temporal fill the longest gap fell to 9.41 hours (north, 5 orbits) and 7.53 hours (south, 4 orbits); without fill the longest gaps were 129.95 hours (north, 69 orbits) and 131.83 hours (south, 70 orbits).

Each WAC image was radiometrically calibrated and map-projected (polar stereographic, 100 m/pixel, nearest-neighbor interpolation) using an improved WAC distortion model, a 30 m LOLA DEM, and spacecraft ephemeris from radio tracking, orbit crossovers, and Earth-based laser ranging (p. 123-124). Illumination classification used a multi-stage spatial-filter pipeline rather than a single global threshold, because scattered light (natural and instrumental) makes some illuminated pixels dimmer than nearby shadowed-but-scattered-light-affected areas (Fig. 4, p. 124): a 5x5 high-pass filter plus threshold isolated small high-contrast illuminated features (Fig. 4C); a 5x5 low-pass filter plus threshold filled in and smoothed that binary result while a majority-pixel rule kept illuminated area from growing past the true terminator (Fig. 4D); a conservative empirically-chosen global threshold on the original image captured smooth illuminated surfaces (Fig. 4E); the two binary results were merged into a final illumination frame (Fig. 4F). A subset of frames was manually inspected to confirm classification accuracy.

Due to LRO orbit inclination drift and an altitude standard deviation of about 6.5 km near the poles, near-complete multi-temporal coverage (greater than 90%) was limited to within 1 degree of the poles, though the resulting maps extend to 88 degrees for regional context (p. 124, 125). Two map products were derived at 100 m/pixel over the full lunar year: a ratio-based illumination map (fraction of observing instances a pixel was lit, no interpolation, Fig. 5B) and a time-weighted map (linearly interpolating gaps and weighting sparse-observation periods, such as the Mini-RF radar campaign window, twice as heavily as normally-sampled periods, to reduce bias from non-uniform temporal sampling, Fig. 7B). These are a factor of two higher spatial resolution than prior Clementine-based maps (500 m/pixel, Nozette et al. 1996; Bussey et al. 1999, 2005) and prior DEM-simulation maps (Kaguya/LALT 474 m/pixel, Noda et al. 2008; Bussey et al. 2010; LRO/LOLA 240 m/pixel, Mazarico et al. 2010). Illumination profiles (Fig. 8) were also produced for individual high-priority pixels, plotting illuminated/shadowed/off-field-of-view state against subsolar latitude and longitude so that the illumination state for an arbitrary future Sun position could be predicted or left as "unknown" where the profile has a gap.

Around each solstice, when shadows are minimized, the narrow-field NAC (2.85 degrees each camera, 5.7 degrees combined) acquired hundreds of images that were compiled into meter-scale mosaics of the 85.5-90 degree N/S zone (Fig. 9); most polar NAC observations are cross-track-summed by a factor of two, trading a factor-of-two loss in cross-track spatial resolution for improved signal-to-noise. Long-exposure NAC observations (Fig. 10, 24.2 ms exposure) were also used to detect indirect (single-reflection) illumination of otherwise-shadowed terrain, distinguishing it from terrain that is "doubly shadowed" (receiving neither direct nor single-reflection indirect light) (p. 127).

To test whether roving or distributing assets over an area changes the illumination statistics, the authors additionally recorded an m x n area as illuminated if any pixel within it was illuminated, at five area sizes: 0.01, 0.25, 1.00, 2.25, and 4.00 km2 (p. 125, Section 5). Results for this method are tabulated for 44 north-pole points (Table 3) and 51 south-pole points (Table 4), organized by longitude, each giving latitude/longitude, number of WAC observations, longest temporal gap (hours), ratio-based and time-weighted illumination percentage at each of the five area sizes, and longest period in shadow (hours) at each of the five area sizes (longest-shadow values are reported as unavailable for several rows where temporal coverage gaps left the region's state ambiguous during a shadow/illumination transition).

### Key findings

**Headline result.** "We identified localized regions where the lunar surface remains illuminated for nearly 94% of the year with the longest eclipsed period lasting only 43 h" (abstract, p. 122). The paper states explicitly that no location on the Moon is truly permanently illuminated: even the best topographic highs are shadowed briefly by nearby terrain, and during lunar eclipses the entire polar region goes dark (Section 6, p. 135).

**Comparison with prior DEM-simulation studies at the single highly-illuminated point closest to each pole (Table 2, p. 127):**

| Study | Pixel scale (m) | North pole | South pole |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noda et al. (2008) | 474 | 87% (89.4N, 127.3E) | 84% (89.8S, 207.5E) |
| Bussey et al. (2010) | 474 | - | 82% (89.44S, 218.2E) |
| Mazarico et al. (2010) | 240 | 84.56% (89.37N, 126.80E) | 89.01% (89.45S, 222.69E) |
| This work, ratio-based | 100 | 63.3% (89.370N, 127.6E) | 71.6% (89.740S, 201.2E) |
| This work, time-weighted | 100 | 63.9% (89.370N, 127.6E) | 71.7% (89.740S, 201.2E) |

The image-derived, higher-resolution (100 m/pixel) values are consistently lower than the coarser-DEM simulation values at the same nominal points. The authors attribute this to two competing effects of finer spatial resolution: it can reveal small illuminated peaks that lower-resolution data/models miss (raising estimates), but on a single-pixel basis it can also show illumination "marching around" a small peak, lowering the pixel's own illuminated fraction relative to a coarser pixel that averages over the peak (p. 125-126, Section 3.2).

**Named south-pole sites.** A 0.01 km2 (100 m x 100 m) region at 89.740S, 201.2E on the edge of Shackleton crater was illuminated in 71.6% of images acquired over the year (time-weighted 71.7%), with a longest eclipse of 145 hours (p. 125, and Table 4 row S30). A 0.01 km2 portion of a massif at 89.418S, 221.3E, about 10 km off the Shackleton rim, was illuminated in 64.2% of WAC frames (time-weighted 63.3%) (p. 125; Table 4 row S46 gives 64.2%/63.3% at the 0.01 km2 size and a longest shadow of 308 hours at that size).

Shackleton crater itself is a 21 km diameter crater near the south pole (89.655S, 129.2E) superimposed on an irregularly shaped massif, with its interior perpetually shadowed. The WAC-derived estimate is that 235 km2 of the interior remained shadowed during the year studied, consistent with a LOLA-simulation estimate of 233 km2 (Mazarico et al. 2010) (p. 129). The paper reports, citing other authors, an age of approximately 3.6 Gyr (Spudis et al. 2008) refined to approximately 3.69-3.60 Gyr (Zuber et al. 2012), and notes Zuber et al. (2012) found the crater floor brighter than surrounding terrain, speculating this could reflect a maturity contrast or a micrometer-thick ice-covered surface layer over approximately 20% of a LOLA sample area (p. 129-130; this specific finding is attributed to Zuber et al., not derived by the present study).

Bussey et al. (2010), using a 474 m/pixel Kaguya DEM, had identified two points about 10 km apart (89.68S, 194.0E and 89.44S, 218.2E) collectively illuminated for about 94% of the year, but simultaneously eclipsed for 60 hours in winter (p. 130).

**Three-station cluster on the Shackleton rim (Section 5.1, Fig. 15, p. 130-131).** Using the m x n area method, the authors identified three 0.01 km2 stations on the Shackleton crater rim: Station 1 (89.685S, 196.7E), Station 2 (89.740S, 201.2E), and Station 3 (89.808S, 205.9E). Stations 1 and 2, 1.8 km apart, were collectively illuminated for 86.9% of the year with a maximum simultaneous shadow period of 63 hours. Stations 2 and 3, 2.1 km apart, were illuminated for 85.5% of the year with a maximum simultaneous shadow period of 60 hours. Combined, at least one of the three stations was illuminated for 92.1% of the year, and the longest period all three were simultaneously in shadow was 43 hours. Adding about 100 m of mobility at each station raised the combined illumination fraction to 93.8% of the year. In the Conclusions, the paper restates this cluster as receiving sunlight for 93.7% of the year and remaining continuously illuminated for 4,960 hours (over 58% of the lunar year) (p. 135) -- a slightly different framing (continuous-illumination duration rather than combined-illuminated-fraction) than the 92.1%/93.8% figures given in Section 5.1, and the reader should note both are the source's own numbers rather than a computation performed here.

**Second south-pole cluster, along the 205E meridian (Section 5.1, p. 131-132).** A 0.01 km2 region at 89.418S, 221.3E and one at 89.500S, 222.1E, 2.5 km apart, were illuminated for 63.3% and 45.6% of the year respectively as single points. Combined (traversing between them or operating distributed panels at both), they remained illuminated for 91.8% of the year with a longest shadow period of 107 hours; adding about 100 m of mobility at each raised this to 94.2% of the year illuminated, with the longest eclipsed period falling to 78 hours, and the longest period of uninterrupted illumination between the sites reaching 5,592 hours (66% of the lunar year).

**North-pole sites, Peary crater rim (Section 5.2, p. 132-134).** A single 0.01 km2 point anywhere examined near the north pole remained illuminated for less than 64% of the year, and no 0.01 km2 region was found with fewer than 298 hours of persistent shadow -- a less benign thermal environment than the best south-pole sites. Three specific regions on the Peary rim were identified: (1) Whipple crater's rim (superimposed on Peary's rim), 89.366N, 128.0E, where a 2.0 km x 2.0 km (4.0 km2) region was illuminated in 85.9% of WAC observations with a longest persistent-shadow period of 207 hours; (2) a region at 89.816N, 117.3E, on the Peary rim halfway between Whipple and Hinshelwood craters, a 2.0 km x 2.0 km (4.0 km2) area illuminated for 80.7% of the year with a longest eclipse of 142 hours; (3) near Hinshelwood crater (also superimposed on the Peary rim), two 0.01 km2 regions 1.5 km apart (89.636N, 331.0E and 89.660N, 324.1E) collectively illuminated for 80.1% of the year with a longest eclipsed period of 284 hours. Clementine-era images had shown these three regions illuminated in all 53 north-pole UVVIS images studied (Nozette et al. 1996; Bussey et al. 2005), but later DEM-based simulations found them eclipsed for significant periods in northern winter (p. 133-134).

**Tables 3 and 4 (full point lists).** Beyond the sites discussed in text, Tables 3 (44 north-pole points, ID N01-N44) and 4 (51 south-pole points, ID S01-S51) tabulate ratio-based and time-weighted illumination percentage and longest shadow duration at all five area sizes for each point. Representative additional rows for the specific sites named in text: Table 3, N12 (89.370N, 127.6E, the Whipple-crater point in Table 2): 63.3%/85.9% ratio-based illumination at 0.01/4.00 km2, longest shadow 303/207 hours at those sizes. Table 3, N02 (89.816N, 117.3E): 18.7%/80.5% ratio-based at 0.01/4.00 km2, longest shadow 4,065/142 hours. Table 4, S30 (89.740S, 201.2E, Station 2): 71.6%/87.9% ratio-based at 0.01/4.00 km2, longest shadow 145/62 hours. Table 4, S28 (89.685S, 196.7E, Station 1): 69.0%/89.2% ratio-based, longest shadow 147/60 hours. Table 4, S33 (89.808S, 205.9E, Station 3): 63.0%/89.5% ratio-based, longest shadow 155/62 hours. Table 4, S46 (89.418S, 221.3E): 64.2%/92.5% ratio-based, longest shadow 308/89 hours. Across nearly every row in both tables, moving from the 0.01 km2 single-pixel size to the 4.00 km2 area size increases the illumination percentage and shortens the longest shadow duration, which is the tabular basis for the paper's mobility/distributed-array argument.

**Spatial pattern of the polar illumination maps (Figs. 5B, p. 126, and 7B, p. 128).** Both B panels are pairs of polar stereographic maps, north pole left and south pole right, each a disk centred on the pole with a white graticule of eight radial spokes at 45 degree spacing (labelled 0E, 90E, 180E, 270E) and three concentric latitude circles, the outermost labelled 88N or 88S. In the north maps 180E is at the top and 0E at the bottom; in the south maps 0E is at the top and 180E at the bottom. Figure 5B uses a linear black-to-white greyscale labelled "Percent of WAC observations" with ticks at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and >70. Figure 7B uses the same ramp and ticks, labelled "Time-weighted illumination, %". Black is 0 percent and white is 70 percent or above; the top of the ramp is open-ended, so values above 70 percent render identically.

High-illumination terrain clusters rather than scattering. In both poles and in both figures the bright pixels form a small number of narrow, spatially connected features, crater rim arcs and ridge crests, rather than isolated patches spread across the map; most of each disk is dark, and several large craters render as solid black with no internal structure. At the south pole the brightest terrain is organised around a large crater sitting essentially on the pole, rendered as a solid black disk immediately adjacent to the graticule centre toward the 90E-to-180E side. A continuous bright arc follows that crater's rim, brightest on the side facing 270E to 0E, and from it a broad connected bright plateau extends into the sector between 180E and 270E past the middle latitude circle; a second bright massif occupies the sector between 90E and 180E at roughly one third to one half of the map radius, while the sector between 0E and 90E beyond the middle circle is almost entirely black. At the north pole the bright terrain is narrower, more fragmented and less extensive: the brightest continuous feature is a short arc immediately adjacent to the pole running toward roughly 90E to 135E, joining the southwestern rim of a large black crater lying between the 90E and 180E spokes at about 0.35 to 0.5 of the map radius, with a second bright arc on the eastern rim of another large black crater toward 270E to 315E at a similar distance. The remainder of the north map is mid-grey to dark cratered surface with small bright crater rims near the map edge.

Figures 5B and 7B show the same spatial pattern as each other at both poles; the shapes and locations of the bright features are indistinguishable by eye, and the time-weighted rendering differs only in overall tone, with slightly less mid-grey area at the south pole.

Figures 5B and 7B carry no site markers, labels or coordinates, so they do not themselves nominate candidate sites; the discrete candidate points are marked in Figs. 13 and 14 and tabulated in Tables 3 and 4. The tabulated points lie within about 0.9 degrees of the pole, whereas the maps extend to 88 degrees, and both maps show bright connected terrain outside the innermost latitude circle where no tabulated point falls. No per-pixel or per-area illumination percentage can be recovered from the printed figures, because the greyscale is open-ended above 70 percent and the panels are reproduced as lossy-compressed rasters with white graticule lines drawn over the terrain; all illumination percentages remain sourced from Tables 2, 3 and 4.

**Validation against DEM-based simulations (Section 4.2, p. 128-130).** A 240 m/pixel LOLA simulation (Mazarico et al. 2010) was compared against WAC and NAC images acquired at the same moment (18 March 2011, 15:59 UTC) near the south pole (Fig. 11). The LOLA simulation accurately located the major terminator boundary across the Shackleton crater rim but failed to resolve some nearby local depressions and small craters that remain in shadow; the WAC-derived binary image more accurately captured both the major shadow boundaries and sub-100-m-scale shadowed features. Separately, a lighting simulation from a 474 m/pixel Kaguya DEM (Bussey et al. 2010) had predicted a point ("Point B") eclipsed by the horizon for 276 hours; NAC images acquired with subsolar points inside that predicted eclipse window (1.45N, 62.0E and 1.55N, 61.5E) revealed a small illuminated peak within the region, reducing the point's longest eclipse to fewer than 180 hours (Fig. 12). Conversely, NAC images also revealed small craters in shadow at times when models had predicted them illuminated (Fig. 11), demonstrating that the resolution mismatch produces errors in both directions.

Mazarico et al. (2010) is also cited for a finding on measurement height: raising the observation point 10 m above the surface at 89.45S, 222.69E reduced the longest period in shadow from 414 hours to 66 hours, illustrating the sensitivity of illumination statistics to mast/antenna height (p. 130; this is the cited study's finding, reported here as background rather than a result of the present analysis).

**Direct and indirect illumination mapping (Section 4.1, Fig. 10, p. 127).** Long-exposure (24.2 ms) NAC images can reveal terrain in permanent shadow that receives indirect, single-reflection illumination from sunlight reflecting off sloping nearby topography. A mosaic of such images covering the permanently shaded interior of Shoemaker crater (image center 87.98S, 46.2E) shows a majority of the crater floor indirectly illuminated by light reflecting off the crater rim, while some regions remain "doubly shadowed" (receiving neither direct nor single-reflection indirect light). The paper notes this direct/indirect mapping aids identification of safe landing sites, traverse options, and potential cold traps.

### Limitations

The near-complete multi-temporal coverage (greater than 90%) that the ratio-based and time-weighted maps rely on was achieved only within 1 degree of each pole; the maps shown extend to 88 degrees for context but with correspondingly less temporal density outside the 1-degree zone (p. 124). Some Table 3/4 rows report "unavailable" for longest shadow duration at certain area sizes because temporal coverage gaps left the region's illumination state ambiguous during a transition between shadowed and illuminated. The study covers exactly one lunar year (15 February 2010 to 5 February 2011); the authors note that additional LROC WAC observations beyond that year could reduce sampling-gap uncertainty in the illumination profiles. Image acquisition frequency was reduced during two Mini-RF radar mapping campaigns (June-August 2010, alternating orbits, 3.8-hour spacing instead of 1.9 hours; and a second north-pole campaign in November 2010), which is one source of the temporal gaps the time-weighted map compensates for. The higher spatial resolution of this dataset relative to prior DEM simulations can either raise or lower single-pixel illumination estimates depending on whether it resolves a small unmodeled peak or instead captures a pixel's line moving across a peak's shadow boundary; the authors present this as an open limitation of point-based illumination statistics rather than resolving it. The paper explicitly states no location on the Moon is truly permanently illuminated, including the best-performing clustered sites identified.

### Topic mapping

Sections 1-2 (Introduction, Previous studies) cover the physical basis for polar illumination asymmetry and the history of Clementine-image-based and DEM-simulation-based illumination studies. Section 3 (Broad-scale, multi-temporal mapping) covers WAC data volume, the illumination-classification image-processing pipeline, and construction of ratio-based and time-weighted illumination maps and profiles. Section 4 (Meter-scale mapping and analysis) covers NAC-based direct/indirect illumination mapping and validation of the WAC/NAC maps against three prior DEM-simulation studies. Section 5 (Discussion) covers named-site results for Shackleton crater and its rim, a second south-pole site cluster, and the Peary/Whipple/Hinshelwood north-pole sites, including the m x n area/mobility method and Tables 3-4. Section 6 (Conclusions) restates the headline illumination-fraction and eclipse-duration figures and states that no location on the Moon is permanently illuminated.
