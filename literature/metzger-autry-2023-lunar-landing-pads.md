## Abstract

Estimates the cost of building lunar landing pads and compares construction methods using a unified economic trade-study framework that converts transportation cost, energy cost, program delay cost, development cost, and operations cost into a single cost metric. Physics-based models of rover grading/compacting, microwave sintering, oven-baked pavers, gravel/rock breakwater construction, and polymer infusion are combined with economic assumptions (transportation cost per kilogram, program value, discount rate, reliability) to find a cost-optimal hardware scale for each method under varying economic scenarios. Finds that transportation cost and program delay cost dominate total cost, that microwave sintering (inner and outer zones, denoted SiSi) is generally the most economical method, that polymer infusion becomes competitive for the outer zone once transportation cost drops below about $110K/kg, and that a landing pad for the Artemis Basecamp could cost from roughly $548M down to about $27.5M in appropriated (budget line item) cost depending on transportation-cost assumptions and programmatic schedule/power constraints (p. 1).

### Background and objective

Rocket landing and launch exhaust on the Moon ejects dust, sand, gravel, and rocks at high velocity, threatening spacecraft, science instruments, habitats, and other infrastructure in an effective "sandblasting" effect, and in the case of powerful engines may accelerate material to lunar orbital velocity, potentially producing a persistent debris cloud in cislunar space (p. 1). Landing pads are proposed as the mitigation, providing an ejecta-free surface for landing and launching. Many construction techniques have been proposed, some requiring large mass transported from Earth, others requiring large energy consumption on the lunar surface, and others being slow to construct, each imposing direct or indirect costs (p. 1-2). The paper's objective is to present a trade-study method that converts all of these disparate factors, transportation mass cost, energy lifecycle cost, and time-driven "program delay cost," into comparable cost metrics so construction methods can be fairly compared and an overall price range for lunar landing pads can be estimated (p. 2).

The paper adopts a two-zone landing pad strategy from prior work (van Susante and Metzger, ref. 36): an inner zone near the touchdown point that must withstand high plume stagnation temperature and pressure, and a larger outer zone that must resist erosion from the radially expanding, accelerating plume gas (p. 3). For illustration the paper uses an inner-pad radius of 12 m and an outer-pad radius of 27 m, derived by adding 7 m of margin (for multi-engine landers with off-centerline engines and landing-accuracy uncertainty) to the 5 m/20 m radii used in the earlier van Susante and Metzger study of a 40 t lander (p. 3).

### Methods and scope

The trade study models candidate construction methods for the inner zone (microwave sintering, oven-baked pavers) and outer zone (microwave sintering, oven-baked pavers, gravel/rock breakwater, polymer infusion), after an assumed common first step of site grading and compacting (p. 3-5). Methods excluded from quantitative analysis due to technological immaturity include solar sintering, infrared/induction sintering, fabric mats and flexible sheets, and a fabric-over-polymer-base concept published too late for inclusion (p. 3-4).

Physics-based parameters for each method (rover energy per distance, grading/compacting rates, magnetron mass-to-power ratio, sintering energy flux, polymer infusion mass fractions, paver oven thermal properties, etc.) are documented in Appendix A and summarized in Table A-1 (p. 35-36), drawn from existing terrestrial technologies and available experimental and physics-based estimates since none of the lunar construction technologies are yet mature (p. 5). Eight combined inner/outer pad construction cases are defined (Table 2, p. 7): SiSi, SiGr, SiPa, SiPo, PaSi, PaGr, PaPa, PaPo (Si = sintered, Pa = pavers, Gr = gravel/rock, Po = polymer).

The economic model (Table 3, p. 10) combines five cost categories, transportation, program delay, hardware development, energy, and operations, using baseline assumptions of a $160B lunar program budget plus $3B/year operating cost over a 20-year program, a 3.5% federal discount rate, 75% of program delay mitigable through proactive management, a $1.684M/kg hardware development cost rate (from the Global Security Advanced Missions Cost Model, ref. 61), and a baseline transportation cost of $300K/kg (p. 9-11). Reliability cost is quantified using the Feasibility of Objectives Technique and Minimization of Effort Algorithm of MIL-HDBK-338B (ref. 63) combined with the Mettas (ref. 64) cost-reliability model, normalizing all methods to 99.00% reliability (p. 12-14, Table 4 p. 13). For each construction method and economic scenario, hardware scale (and thus power, mass, and construction time) is optimized to find the minimum total cost (p. 15-17).

Nine combinations of three transportation-cost levels (expensive $1M/kg, moderate $100K/kg, cheap $300/kg) and three program-delay-cost levels (high, based on $160B outpost at 20x value; moderate/low, at 1x value; none) are examined (Figure 8, p. 21). A second, "programmatically realistic" version of the model (Table 6, p. 24) imposes a maximum construction time (270 days, tied to Artemis's assumed annual launch cadence) and a maximum available power (50 kW in most cases) and minimizes appropriated (budget line item) cost rather than total cost.

### Key findings

- Non-optimized hardware sets (Table 1, p. 6) show sintering the inner pad takes 4.1 days and 19.7 MWh with 4.7 t mass from Earth (using 200 kW and four rovers), and sintering the outer pad takes 14.1 days and 67.8 MWh with 4.7 t mass; pavers for the inner pad take 39.5 days and 41.3 MWh with 1.8 t mass; pavers for the outer pad take 21.8 days and 55.9 MWh with 1.8 t mass; gravel/rock outer pad takes 35.4 days and 0.81 MWh with 1.6 t mass; polymer outer pad takes 0.56 days and 0.011 MWh with 7.6 t mass (p. 6).
- Making pavers in an oven unexpectedly required more energy than microwave sintering for the inner pad but less energy than sintering for the outer pad, even at equal pad thickness, because ovens lose more energy to the environment the longer heat must diffuse to the pad's center while microwaves penetrate the full sintering depth immediately; the crossover thickness where sintering becomes more efficient than baking falls between 2.54 cm (outer pad) and 7.62 cm (inner pad) under this model (p. 6).
- Grout for inner-zone pavers requires an estimated 84 kg from Earth and, applied at 1 cm/sec along paver joints, adds 55 hours to construction, about two-thirds of total construction time for that case (p. 6).
- Non-optimized construction energies for the eight combined cases (Figure 3, p. 9) range from about 20-21 MWh (SiPo, SiGr) up to about 110 MWh (PaSi, the tallest); total mass from Earth (Figure 4, p. 9) ranges from about 1.8 MT (PaPa, lowest) to about 12.5 MT (SiPo, tallest).
- In the non-optimized baseline economic scenario, cost is dominated by delivery (transportation) cost and program delay cost, with development, energy, and operations costs barely visible on the cost chart (Figure 5, p. 15); SiPo shows high delivery cost but low delay cost due to its speed.
- After hardware-scale optimization in the baseline scenario (Figure 7, p. 17), SiSi is the most economical method at $2.64B full cost, or $1.31B "appropriated cost" (full cost minus program delay cost and energy cost, which would not appear in a budget line item) (p. 16-17).
- Across six/nine varied economic scenarios (Section 8, Figure 8, p. 18-21), SiSi is generally least expensive under expensive ($1M/kg) transportation, with appropriated costs of $5.28B (high delay cost), $1.23B (low delay cost), and $409M (no delay cost); under moderate ($100K/kg) transportation SiPo becomes cheapest under high delay cost ($1.58B appropriated) while SiSi remains cheapest under low/no delay cost ($392M and $130M appropriated respectively); under cheap ($300/kg) transportation SiPo is cheapest in all delay-cost cases, with appropriated cost falling to $123M (high delay), $30M (moderate delay), and $12M (no delay cost) (p. 18-21).
- SiPo (sintered inner, polymer outer) becomes less expensive than SiSi when transportation cost drops below about $110K/kg (Figure 9, p. 22), with corresponding mass (Figure 10, p. 22) and construction time (Figure 11, p. 23) shown as functions of transportation cost; Figure 11 shows construction time for the least-expensive system falling from roughly 45 days near $1M/kg (SiSi branch) to about 1 day near $1,000/kg (SiPo branch).
- Example cost-optimized (unconstrained) systems for a $160B outpost at 4x expected value (Table 5, p. 23): at $1M/kg transportation, SiSi costs $4,459M total / $2,383M appropriated in 41.6 days; at $100K/kg, SiPo costs $1,503M / $1,110M in 6.8 days; at $10K/kg, SiPo costs $292M / $143M in 2.5 days; at $2K/kg, SiPo costs $181M / $81M in 1.4 days.
- Example programmatically constrained systems representing Artemis realism (Table 6, p. 24), with schedule capped at up to 270 days and power capped at 50-100 kW: appropriated cost is $548M at $1M/kg transportation, $229M at $300K/kg, $130M at $100K/kg, $47.4M at $10K/kg, and $27.5M at $2K/kg; the paper states this method produces landing-pad cost estimates "from $130M to $548M depending on transportation cost" and that appropriated cost may drop below $50M as the cislunar economy develops (p. 23-24). These estimates exclude the cost of technology maturation from current TRL-3/4 to TRL-6 (p. 24).
- The conclusions restate a headline case: at $100K/kg transportation (expected during Artemis), a landing pad would need about $299M on the program's budget line item; at $10K/kg or lower it could fall to about $46M or less (p. 27-28, Section 15).
- Reliability cost factors (Table 4, p. 13) are all below 2x even though baseline reliabilities before improvement range from 68.75% (PaPa) to 99.00% (SiPo), because only the least-reliable subsystems require extra development cost to reach parity; the paper concludes reliability is not a significant cost driver except when transportation cost is extraordinarily cheap (p. 13-14).
- The program delay cost is derived from an assumed lunar program present value of $117.68B (at $160B budgeted over 20 years, 3.5% discount rate), scaled by 1x, 4x (baseline), and 20x return-on-investment multipliers to $470.7B (4x) and $2.35T (20x) present value; the baseline program delay cost is set at 25% of program value (i.e., 75% of delay is assumed mitigable) multiplied by the discount rate and delay time (p. 10-11).
- Baseline transportation cost is set at $300K/kg for a pad built in the 5-to-10-year timeframe, against a stated context of roughly $1M/kg in the next five years, $1M/kg down to $100K/kg within 10 years, and $500K/kg down to $2K/kg within 20 years (p. 12).
- Energy cost in the baseline scenario is derived as $4.14M/kW/year (from solar photovoltaic mass-to-power ratio, hardware development cost rate, and transportation cost, amortized over a 20-year photovoltaic lifespan), equivalent to $473K/MWh (p. 14-15).
- Strategic technology improvement recommendations by method and transportation-cost regime are tabulated in Table 7 (p. 25); for example, under cheap transportation the paver method's most strategic improvement is lower energy usage (no-delay-cost case) or improved construction speed per hardware mass (high-delay-cost case), and polymer's outer-zone method is already "currently best" under cheap transportation.
- Table A-1 (p. 35-36) input parameters include: density of soil before compaction 1500 kg/m3; density of regolith after compaction 2200 kg/m3; sintered pad density 2200 kg/m3; available sintering power (outpost capability) 200 kW; inner pad energy application 21.73 kWh/m2; outer pad energy application 18.45 kWh/m2; inner pad sintered thickness 7.62 cm; outer pad sintered thickness 2.54 cm; magnetron efficiency 0.5; terrestrial magnetron power/mass ratio 3/260 kW/kg.
- The appendix text discussing Figure A-4 (p. 43) states the model found "that an energy per area of 21.73 kWh/m2 was needed to produce a 3 cm thick sintered layer, or 18.45 kWh/m2 for a 1 cm thick sinter," tying the same two energy-application figures to 3 cm and 1 cm layer thicknesses. Table A-1, by contrast, assigns 21.73 kWh/m2 to the "Inner Pad Energy Application" (with inner sintered thickness listed as 7.62 cm) and 18.45 kWh/m2 to the "Outer Pad Energy Application" (with outer sintered thickness listed as 2.54 cm). Both statements are as printed in the source; the paper does not reconcile the discrepancy between the layer thicknesses named in the appendix text (3 cm, 1 cm) and those named in Table A-1 (7.62 cm, 2.54 cm) for the same two energy figures.
- Figure A-4 (p. 43) plots the model's predicted temperature-versus-depth profile after microwaving 200 kW/m2 at 2.45 GHz into lunar soil for 5.34 minutes: temperature starts near 1450 degrees C at the surface, falls to about 1050 degrees C by 0.006 m depth, about 700 degrees C by 0.03 m, and about 500 degrees C at 0.10 m depth; the caption notes the average temperature exceeds the sintering temperature within the top 1 cm.
- A weakness identified in the trade-study method itself is that it assumes all costs are smoothly incrementable, whereas real recovery of program delay cost depends on the discrete launch cadence and real energy cost depends on outpost-level power architecture decisions (p. 26).

### Limitations

The authors state the construction methods studied are not exhaustive and do not represent a final determination of the best method (p. 26). Several promising methods (solar sintering, induction heating, fabric-over-polymer pads) were excluded due to insufficient technological maturity or data (p. 3-4). Model parameters are based on comparisons to terrestrial technologies rather than measurements of mature lunar construction systems, since none of the technologies are yet flight-proven (p. 5, p. 26). The trade study ignores launch-failure risk during equipment transport, assuming this risk is offset by the increased in-situ value of successfully delivered, "de-risked" hardware (p. 11). The cost estimates exclude technology maturation cost from current TRL-3/4 to TRL-6, assumed to be partly borne by other programs or private investment (p. 24). Program delay cost recovery is treated as smoothly incrementable in the base model even though real recovery is constrained by discrete launch cadence, and the authors caution the calculated program delay cost is "simply a metric" not meant to be followed blindly, intended to inform rather than substitute for higher-level programmatic decisions (p. 12, p. 26). The authors disclose a competing interest: the University of Central Florida has applied for a patent on a microwave-sintering energy-reduction technology invented by Metzger, who is partially supported by a related NASA STTR award, though the manuscript states the improvements from that new technology are not included in the analysis (unnumbered Declaration of Competing Interest section, following Section 15).

### Topic mapping

Sections 1-3 (p. 1-5) cover the debris/plume hazard motivating landing pads, the two-zone pad requirement, and candidate inner/outer zone construction methods (microwave sintering, oven-baked pavers, gravel/rock breakwater, polymer infusion). Section 4 and Appendix A (p. 5-6, p. 35-44) document the physics-based parameter set (energy, mass, rate) underlying the model, including Table A-1's soil density, sintering power, and energy-application figures. Sections 5-7 (p. 6-17) present non-optimized and then hardware-scale-optimized results and costs for the eight combined construction cases under the baseline economic scenario. Section 8 (p. 17-21) varies the economic scenario across transportation-cost and program-delay-cost combinations. Sections 9-12 (p. 21-25) identify the least-expensive methods as a function of transportation cost, present example optimized and programmatically constrained construction systems (Tables 5 and 6), and tabulate strategic technology improvements (Table 7). Sections 13-15 (p. 25-28) evaluate the trade-study method itself, note its limitations, and state conclusions including the headline appropriated-cost estimates of roughly $299M (at $100K/kg transportation) and $46M (at $10K/kg or lower). Appendix B (Table B-1, B-2, p. 45) documents the reliability calculation details underlying Table 4.
