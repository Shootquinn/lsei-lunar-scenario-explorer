## Abstract

Direct spectroscopic observation of the debris produced when the LCROSS Centaur upper stage struck the persistently shadowed floor of Cabeus crater on 9 October 2009, reported from the two nadir-viewing spectrometers on the trailing Shepherding Spacecraft over a four-minute window. Near-infrared absorption bands assigned to water vapor and water ice, and ultraviolet emission assigned to the hydroxyl radical, are used with a linear spectral mixing model and a DISORT radiative transfer model to derive water masses, an ejecta dust mass, and a water-to-dust mass ratio inside the instrument field of view. The peak combined water vapor and water ice mass in the field of view is 155 plus or minus 12 kg, and the mean water mass fraction across three post-impact averaging periods is 5.6 plus or minus 2.9 percent by mass. Eight volatile species besides water are given column abundances from the same spectral fit.

### Background and objective

The prior evidence for polar lunar hydrogen was indirect and did not identify the compound carrying it. Lunar Prospector neutron scattering had shown raised hydrogen concentrations within 20 degrees of both poles, and later Earth-based and spacecraft observations had added evidence for trace ice or bound hydroxyl, but the paper states that neither the chemical form of the hydrogen-bearing compounds nor their origin had been determined, with proposed sources including solar wind, asteroids, and comets. Clementine radar had returned a bright signal from the interior of Shackleton crater consistent with volumetric scattering by water ice, a process the paper notes would require that ice to be relatively pure, near 90 percent, and 1 to 2 m thick.

LCROSS was built to convert that indirect evidence into ground truth at a single location. The stated goal is to provide ground truth at one location, the crater Cabeus, by crashing a spent rocket stage into the Moon and observing the ejected debris, including any hydrogen species, from instruments on a trailing spacecraft.

Site selection turned on two criteria and one of them is geometric rather than compositional. The two most important criteria were the altitude at which ejecta would be illuminated by sunlight, and evidence of elevated hydrogen from previous measurements. The height required for debris to reach sunlight was 833 m at the Centaur impact site and time, a figure set by the comparatively low latitude of Cabeus at about 81.5 degrees S and by a cleft in the crater rim topography on the sunward side at the time of impact. That same geometry gave a dark background for the nadir spectrometer measurements. Substantial hydrogen had been indicated in Cabeus by both the Lunar Prospector Neutron Spectrometer and the Lunar Exploration Neutron Detector, and the area selected for impact showed temperatures below 50 K, which the paper describes as making it a likely cold trap for volatiles (Colaprete et al., 2010, p. 463).

The impactor and the observing platform were separated deliberately, and the impactor was cleaned first. The impactor was the spent Centaur upper stage of the Atlas V that carried both LRO and LCROSS to the Moon. After LRO separated, the Centaur vented its remaining fuel and the LCROSS Shepherding Spacecraft took control for the next four months, performing maneuvers that let the sun bake out residual water, running instrument calibrations, and finally targeting the Centaur at the planned impact site. About 9 hours before impact the Shepherding Spacecraft separated from the Centaur and performed a braking burn to build a 4-minute separation between itself and the Centaur at impact. Its nine instruments were powered on about 1 hour before impact. The residual hydrogen, oxygen, and water on the Centaur at the time of impact are given in the notes as less than 0.5, less than 1.25, and less than 1.5 kg respectively, which is the contamination floor against which the reported water masses should be read.

### Methods and scope

Two instruments produced every number in this paper, and both look straight down through the same small field. The observations reported here come from the nadir near-infrared spectrometer and the nadir ultraviolet and visible spectrometer, which the notes describe as two down-viewing spectrometers with a coaligned 1 degree field of view. The near-infrared instrument covered 1.25 to 2.35 micrometers with a resolving power between 35 and 70. The ultraviolet and visible instrument covered 0.26 to 0.65 micrometers with a resolving power between 300 and 850. The other seven LCROSS instruments are named in the abstract but are not the subject of this paper, with one exception: visible camera images are used to set the ejecta cloud diameter and the field-of-view filling factor, and LRO Diviner results are cited as an independent check on the cloud optical depth.

The field of view is a physical footprint, and its size relative to the plume is what governs how the mass numbers should be read. At 8 s after impact the ejecta cloud diameter in the visible camera was about 4.5 km while the spectrometer footprints were about 10 km in diameter. At 20 s the ejecta cloud diameter was about 8.5 km and the footprints were about 9.6 km. The Shepherding Spacecraft was three-axis stabilized and pointed at the impact target to within 0.1 degree.

Post-impact spectra are all ratios against a pre-impact reference, and the averaging bins were chosen from the data rather than fixed in advance. A reference spectrum was built from 180 measurements taken in the roughly 90 s before impact. Each near-infrared scan took about 0.6 s, so averaging across 30 scans covers about 20 s. The averaging periods were guided by discernible periods of distinct near-infrared phenomenology, identified from the peaks and valleys in the total near-infrared radiance, which means the time bins are phenomenology-driven and not uniform. A separate reference for the ultraviolet and visible ratios was composed from the average of the last four scans before impact.

The record has two known gaps and the paper flags both. A gap in the near-infrared data about 1 min before impact corresponds to the nadir near-infrared spectrometer being placed into flash mode, in which only five spectral positions are sampled but at about 72 Hz, and the paper states that those data are not used in the analysis presented here. The ultraviolet and visible spectrometer was reconfigured for long integration times, which produces a sampling pause just before impact. The paper also notes that a high concentration of dust or of near-infrared absorbing species lowered integrated radiance across most of the near-infrared spectrum, an effect it says was seen both experimentally and in the Deep Impact mission.

Species abundances come from a linear mixing model fit over a restricted wavelength window. Quality of fit was determined by the total residual between the measurements and the fit from 1.3 to 2.0 micrometers, the region where water vapor and ice are primarily involved. Absorption cross sections were computed using HITRAN at local thermal equilibrium, room temperature, and 1 atm, convolved to the instrument resolution. A chi-squared minimization was run for three epochs to elucidate the uncertainties against their related confidence intervals.

Every mass in the paper passes through a radiative transfer model with assumed grain properties. The ejecta cloud radiance was modeled with the multistream radiative transfer model DISORT at the appropriate incident and viewing angles. Grain optical properties were calculated using Mie theory and a log-normal particle distribution with area-weighted mean radii between 1 and 35 micrometers and a variance of 1.5, using indices of refraction for silicon dioxide. The mixed ice and dust cloud optical properties were approximated as a linear mix of pure water ice and pure silicon dioxide grains. The cloud was approximated as five layers with the top and bottom layers ice free, and the paper notes that at these low optical depths the result is mostly insensitive to how the ice is arranged among the layers. A grain density of 3000 kg per cubic meter was assumed when calculating total cloud mass. That assumed density is an input, not a measurement, and it scales the dust mass that sits in the denominator of the reported concentration.

The whole observational record analyzed here is four minutes long. The reported time series runs from about 100 s before impact to about 250 s after it, and the persistent post-impact signal is followed for the remaining 4-minute observation duration.

### Key findings

The findings below are ordered by how far each one sits from the instrument, because the paper builds a chain in which each step adds an assumption. The first group is what the detectors recorded. The second is column density inferred from band depth. The third is mass inferred from column density and the field-of-view area. The fourth is the ejecta dust mass inferred from optical depth through a radiative transfer model. The concentration this paper is usually cited for is the ratio of the third quantity to the fourth.

What the instruments recorded is a radiance history, not a composition. A hot vapor cloud at about 1000 K emerged from the crater immediately after impact, followed by low-angle and high-angle ejecta. The spectrometers measured both thermal emission from ejecta at roughly 300 to 1000 K and solar scattering from sunlit debris. The debris cloud reached sunlight about 1 s after impact. Peak brightness in the ultraviolet and visible spectra came approximately 17 s after impact, and Figure 1 places that peak at about 91 W per square meter per steradian. Brightness then waned to a persistent level substantially above the pre-impact background for another 223 s. The near-infrared radiance was one-fifth of the ultraviolet and visible radiance against a modeled ratio of 2.5, and the observed ratio of 5 is read as indicating grains smaller than a few microns. At times later than 30 s the total cloud brightness dropped by a factor of about 3.2 and then stayed fairly constant for another 200 s.

The spectral detections are band assignments in ratioed spectra. Absorption attributed to hydroxyl and to water, both vapor and possibly mineral bound, appears in the 0 to 23 s fit. Features at 1.4, 1.9, and 2.2 micrometers are read as mineral-bound hydroxyl, bound water, and metal-bound hydroxyl, for which the source gives the aluminum-hydroxyl bond at the 2.2 micrometer feature as its example, all present in substantially smaller quantities than gas-phase water. The 1.5 micrometer ice feature and its 2.0 micrometer counterpart in the 23 to 30 s period are what the paper uses to argue that at least some of the ejected water was in the form of ice rather than water adsorbed on grains. The 1.87 micrometer water vapor feature reappeared at later times and persisted for the rest of the 4-minute observation. Hydroxyl emission near 0.308 to 0.310 micrometers was absent before impact within measurement uncertainty, rose to an initial peak about 30 s after impact, and stayed above the pre-impact baseline for the remaining observation, while the off-band comparison wavelength at 0.312 micrometers stayed at baseline within error. That off-band channel is the paper's own internal control on the hydroxyl detection and it behaved as a control should.

Fit quality is stated explicitly and is wavelength dependent. The model fit is good below 2.0 micrometers, where residual errors are typically less than 1.5 percent, and is described as not as good at longer wavelengths where instrument performance is poorest and the blackbody source function varies most. The reduced chi-squared values for the three fits are 1.16, 1.8, and 1.2. For the fit used to derive the volatile abundance table, the notes give a reduced chi-squared of 1.19 with 75 points and 9 free parameters, and independent point errors on the water vapor of plus 5 and minus 5 percent at 1 SD, plus 7 and minus 10 percent at 2 SD, and plus 12 and minus 24 percent at 3 SD. The same note states that the water vapor mass is uncertain to 28 percent at 1 SD, taken as the root mean square of a 5 percent mixing-model point error and a roughly 28 percent radiance uncertainty at the 1.87 micrometer band center.

Column densities convert to masses only by multiplying through the field-of-view area. Over the 123 to 180 s period, the total water vapor column in the field of view was about 5.1 plus or minus 1.4 times 10^19 molecules per square meter at 1 SD. Over that same period the field of view ranged from about 3.8 times 10^7 down to 2 times 10^7 square meters, which the paper converts to a total water vapor mass in the field of view ranging from 74 to 31 kg, and 53 plus or minus 3 kg at the average field of view. Total water ice abundance over the same period ranged from 22 to 9 kg with an average of 16 plus or minus 2 kg. Using the 1.5 micrometer ice feature from the 23 to 30 s period and an assumed 2 micrometer ice particle radius, the water ice grain column number is 6.3 times 10^7 particles per square meter, or about 131 plus or minus 8 kg of water ice. Independently, the maximum hydroxyl band strength at impact plus 180 s, about 8.0 times 10^-3 W per square meter per micrometer per steradian, gives a hydroxyl column of 2.2 times 10^16 molecules per square meter and corresponds to 23 plus or minus 11 kg of water vapor, which the paper reports as consistent with the near-infrared value for about the same period.

The spectral evolution is read as a two-stage plume. The paper interprets Figure 2 as showing an initial release of vapors, including water vapor and hydroxyl, together with small amounts of ice-rich ejecta that passed out of the instrument field of view within about 20 s. That initial plume was followed by the emergence of water ice grains that continued to sublime for the entire 4-minute observation period and possibly continued subliming from the heated surface near the impact site. The supporting observation is the consistent increase in the water vapor absorbance after impact shown in Figure 4.

The hydroxyl timing is used to argue for more than one water source. If an expanding vapor cloud at about 1000 K were the only source of hydroxyl, the paper states that a rapid decline in hydroxyl emission would be expected at times longer than 30 s. Continued high levels after 30 s, and an apparent rise after about 120 s, are read as indicating that additional sources of water vapor and possibly hydroxyl were present. The position and shape of the emission after about 190 s is described as consistent with prompt emission, and that is the same period in which the near-infrared spectra show persistent water vapor. The hydroxyl column estimate itself uses an assumed g factor of 5 times 10^-4 photons per second.

The ejecta dust mass is the denominator of the concentration and it is a model output. Using the highest cloud radiance shortly after impact and a fit with an area-weighted grain radius of 2.5 micrometers, the visible optical depth at 0.5 micrometers is 0.002 to 0.003. For a uniform distribution along the line of sight, that implies a number density of about 1.4 times 10^6 to 2.1 times 10^6 grains per square meter. A uniform distribution of debris across the instrument field of view gives a total ejecta cloud mass in sunlight of about 4300 to 6500 kg. Applying an average area filling factor of 0.58 to account for field-of-view underfilling before 20 s brings the low and high estimates to approximately 2500 kg and 3800 kg, with an average mass of 3150 plus or minus 790 kg. At times later than 30 s the cloud optical depth was about 9.3 times 10^-4, corresponding to a cloud mass of 1850 kg, which the paper reports as consistent with the optical depth LRO Diviner derived for the period about 90 s after impact.

The paper reads these ratios against other reservoirs rather than against the Moon. It states that the hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, sulfur dioxide, and carbon monoxide to water ratios are more commensurate with abundances in molecular hot cores than with those found in comets, in the interstellar medium, or predicted from gas-gas reactions in the protoplanetary disk. From that it infers that the volatile reservoir LCROSS struck may partly have cometary or asteroidal origins but probably also holds molecules formed in situ on cold grain surfaces in the persistently shadowed region.

The comparison against the neutron measurements is a disagreement the paper addresses directly. LEND neutron data is cited as indicating that the floor of Cabeus contains more than 2 weight percent water, which the paper states is about a third the value derived from LCROSS. Four caveats are listed on that comparison.

- The LEND result is strongly model dependent, for instance in its assumptions about desiccated top layer thickness.
- The LCROSS sample depth was possibly deeper than neutron spectroscopy can effectively sample, which the paper puts at deeper than about 0.7 m.
- Persistent conduction of impact heat into the regolith caused a continued release of volatiles.
- The LEND and LPNS instrument footprints were never smaller than 10 km across, so spatial gradients smaller than 10 km are smoothed.

Taken together the paper reads the combination as suggesting some spatial heterogeneity at scales below 10 km. Separately, LAMP on LRO is cited as suggesting that about 140 kg of molecular hydrogen was released at impact, corresponding to an initial regolith concentration of 1.4 percent.

### Limitations

The paper labels its own water masses as lower limits. The stated reason is that the ejecta cloud filled the spectrometer field of view about 20 s after impact, so some water would have been outside the instrument field of view (Colaprete et al., 2010, p. 465). In the opposite direction, the paper states that the early-period mixing ratios are likely to be overestimates during the interval when the ejecta cloud was smaller than the instrument field of view, and it notes that water vapor sublimated from ice grains in sunlight cannot be differentiated from water vapor sublimated from heated surfaces in and near the crater. The two biases run in opposite directions and the paper does not net them.

The scale the concentration applies to is the field of view over a few tens of seconds, not a volume of regolith. The denominator is a dust mass of about 2175 kg on average across the three averaging periods, ranging from 942.5 to 3148 kg by period, and that mass is a radiative transfer output rather than a weighed quantity. No excavation depth, depth interval, or excavated volume is given anywhere in this article. The only related phrase is the abstract's reference to the estimated total excavated mass of regolith that reached sunlight, and the companion crater-formation analysis is a separate paper by Schultz and colleagues that is not part of this work.

Several assumed parameters set the derived masses and none of them are measured here.

- A grain density of 3000 kg per cubic meter, assumed when calculating total cloud mass.
- Indices of refraction for silicon dioxide, standing in for the actual regolith mineralogy.
- An area-weighted grain radius of 2.5 micrometers for the optical depth fit.
- A 2 micrometer ice particle radius for the ice grain column number.
- An average area filling factor of 0.58 for field-of-view underfilling before 20 s.
- A five-layer cloud geometry with the top and bottom layers ice free.

The paper quantifies some of the resulting error: the 1.5 micrometer feature could be modeled to within about 14 percent, taken as the root mean square of 11 percent fitting uncertainty and 9 percent radiance measurement uncertainty, and the ultraviolet and visible continuum fit carries an optical depth uncertainty of about 25 percent, from 5 percent fitting and 24 percent radiance uncertainty.

The volatile abundances in Table 2 carry uncertainties that reach or exceed the central value for four of the nine species. Ethylene is 1.6 with an uncertainty of 1.7, carbon dioxide is 1.1 with an uncertainty of 1.0, methane is 3.3 with an uncertainty of 3.0, and methanol is 7.8 with an uncertainty of 42, all in the same exponent as their row. The paper itself calls this a preliminary analysis and notes the caveat that the abundances are derived from HITRAN cross sections at local thermal equilibrium, room temperature, and 1 atm, while the impact plume contained low-density and rapidly cooling gases, probably far from equilibrium.
