# Six Hypotheses for Accelerating the Lunar Economy

## Citation

Nayak, M. (2024). *Six hypotheses for accelerating the lunar economy* (arXiv:2403.05959). arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2403.05959

Publisher URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05959

## Abstract

A short position paper from DARPA's Strategic Technologies Office. Drawing on work
under the 10-Year Lunar Architecture (LunA-10) study, the author names six technology
areas where revolutionary improvement would, in his assessment, directly accelerate the
fielding of a lunar economy: centralized thermal rejection/generation as a service,
widespread low-altitude orbital prospecting, large silicon wafer manufacture on the
Moon, biomanufacturing for lunar construction, higher refinement rates in low gravity,
and new lunar Position/Navigation/Timing (PNT) concepts. Each hypothesis is stated,
motivated, and followed by a list of open technical questions the author advocates for
future research. No experiments, models, or data are presented; the paper is an agenda.

## Summary

### Background and objective
DARPA supports a model in which NASA, international governments, and commercial
industry can rapidly scale up lunar exploration and commerce, enabled by an integrated,
shared lunar infrastructure framework. The author frames the current paradigm as one
where each lunar lander or activity must organically supply all its own survival
resources (power, communications, data storage), which limits scale-up. In November
2023 DARPA launched the 10-Year Lunar Architecture (LunA-10) study to examine the
business and technical case for non-terrestrial technology concepts that move away from
isolated, self-sufficient systems toward shareable, scalable, resource-driven systems
operating jointly, with an emphasis on monetizable services delivered mass-efficiently.
The paper's stated objective is to identify, from LunA-10 work, six hypotheses where a
revolutionary technical improvement would directly accelerate a lunar economy, and to
recommend these as near-future focus areas for government and commercial stakeholders.

### Methods and scope
The paper is a non-quantitative, single-author perspective piece. It presents no formal
methodology, dataset, model, or experiment. Each of six topics is introduced with a
qualitative rationale, illustrative figures drawn from cited literature, and a bulleted
list of "important technical issues that remain to be understood" that the author
advocates as research directions. Scope is limited to identifying and motivating the six
areas; the paper explicitly leaves technical development to future work by others. It
carries a disclaimer that views are the author's own and not official DARPA, DoD, or US
Government positions, and is marked unclassified and approved for public release.

### Key findings
The paper's content is a set of hypotheses rather than empirical results. Its substantive
claims, stated with figures drawn from the cited literature, are:

- **Topic 1 — Centralized thermal rejection/generation as a service.** Thermal hardware
  can dominate lunar system mass: cited mass fractions reach as high as ~60% for an
  optical-wavelength power-beaming system, ~45% for an ISRU plant, and ~25% for surface
  fission plants. Proton exchange membrane fuel cells output up to 45% heat;
  radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) have 5–20% electrical conversion
  efficiency, so ~300 W of electrical power can imply 4–5 kWt of continuous heat. The
  author hypothesizes that offloading thermal functions to a centralized "thermal hub"
  (analogous to a building HVAC system), billed per kilowatt like a terrestrial utility,
  could eliminate up to ~60% of an individual system's mass. Cost-to-surface is cited at
  roughly $1M–$1.5M per kilogram.
- **Topic 2 — Widespread lunar orbital prospecting.** Commercial resource value on the
  Moon remains uncertain; data are lacking on near-subsurface distribution (defined as
  the top 3–10 m). The author calls for a single-orbiter, very-low-altitude campaign
  (below 15 km) able to maneuver with very little or no propellant while hosting
  spectrometers or other prospecting payloads, and notes potential for 3D
  photogrammetry models of the surface.
- **Topic 3 — Large silicon wafers for lunar microsystems.** Citing a microgravity
  crystal-growth literature review, the author notes crystals grown in microgravity can
  be up to 60% larger, up to 75% higher quality, and up to 80% more uniform than
  Earth-grown, with improvement in ~90% of crystals analyzed. He argues lunar gravity
  (1/6 g) may be an advantageous compromise, enabling manufacture of large (>400 mm)
  silicon wafers and wide/ultra-wide bandgap semiconductors. Silicon crystal growth
  occurs at ~1425 °C, near the operating temperature of some ISRU pilot plants,
  suggesting co-design potential; lunar regolith is ~21% silicon by average composition.
- **Topic 4 — Biomanufacturing for lunar construction.** Microbial biomanufacturing is
  proposed for closed-loop production of bio-enabled structures from regolith, industrial
  fuels and lubricants (with some Earth-sourced starting materials), and even biomining
  of trace rare-earth elements. Emphasis is placed on loop closure for cost and waste
  reduction.
- **Topic 5 — Higher refinement rates in low gravity.** Several valuable elements exist
  only at trace concentrations: the Procellarum KREEP terrane has ~300× more uranium and
  thorium than chondrites but only single-digit ppm (U ~2 ppm; Th ~7–10 ppm).
  PGM-rich asteroids run 100–250 ppm. At 250 ppm, returning ~1 metric ton of refined
  product would require refining ~4,000,000 kg of raw material at the source, taking on
  the order of 10 years through a spacecraft-sized refinery. The author argues that
  refinery input rates well above 3 kg/min/W (per watt of supplied power) would be a
  paradigm shift for elements below ~100 ppm abundance.
- **Topic 6 — New lunar PNT concepts.** The author argues an Earth-like space-based GPS
  is likely not the best solution for a Moon where activity is confined to a few
  resource-rich areas. He calls for miniaturized, very-low size/weight/power (SWaP)
  timing and navigation solutions independent of Earth signals, targeting ~10-meter
  position accuracy when out of line of sight, and synchronization methods among mobile
  users beyond line of sight (e.g., low-frequency ground-wave radio).

### Limitations
By design the paper is a hypothesis-generating agenda, not an analysis. It presents no
original data, models, cost estimates, or validation; quantitative figures are cited from
other sources and used illustratively. Assessments of which improvements would
"accelerate" the lunar economy are the author's judgments, hedged in the text (e.g., "may
be likely to occur"). The document carries an explicit disclaimer that it does not
represent official agency positions. Each topic ends with open questions rather than
answers, and no prioritization, timeline, or budget is offered across the six areas.

### Topic mapping
The paper's own six sections define its subject areas: (1) centralized lunar thermal
management as a utility service; (2) low-altitude orbital resource prospecting and
surveying; (3) in-space/lunar silicon wafer and semiconductor manufacturing; (4) space
biomanufacturing and closed-loop bioregenerative systems; (5) low-gravity ore
beneficiation and refinement throughput; (6) lunar Position, Navigation, and Timing.
Cross-cutting themes include In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU), shared/scalable lunar
infrastructure, mass-efficiency and cost-to-surface economics, and the DARPA LunA-10
study framing. The work is a government-affiliated technology-roadmap perspective piece.
