# Economic and Technical Issues for Lunar Development

## Abstract

An essay/overview arguing that large-scale lunar development will be led by the private sector rather than governments, because governments lack excess funds and public attention spans for space are short. Its central proposal is a "new paradigm": deconstruct the long, expensive lunar-development project into independently financeable units built on **dual-use technologies** that are profitable for terrestrial applications while advancing lunar capability. A coordinating "Lunar Development Corporation" (LDC), operating like a venture-capital holding company over a ~20-year horizon, is proposed to prioritize and assemble these technologies. The paper also surveys launch economics, lunar-resource prospects, reliability-based design, NASA's role, and long-term environmental/ethical and colony-independence issues. Method is qualitative argument; appendices tabulate candidate dual-use technologies.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Benaroya frames lunar development as an "open-ended" project whose vastness makes its benefits hard to itemize and whose long time scale deters both public support and private investors. Unlike closed-ended public works (airports, highways, the 1960s space program) with understood benefits, lunar development cannot be justified as a single investment. The stated objective is to propose a financing/organizing framework — one the author states does not appear to have been suggested by others — that lets investors back individual, near-term-profitable segments while the aggregate carries humanity to a permanent lunar presence.

### Methods and scope

This is a conceptual essay, not an empirical study. It combines a proposed organizational/financing paradigm with narrative discussion of enabling technologies, launch-market economics, resource prospects, reliability-based design reasoning (including simple probabilistic notation), and forward-looking policy/ethics. Nine appendix tables (Appendix I) list candidate dual-use technologies across communications/information, in-situ resource utilization, surface mobility (suits and vehicles), human support, power, structures/materials, science equipment, and operations/maintenance; these are stated to be adapted from the July 1997 NASA Mars-exploration reference-mission report (Hoffman & Kaplan 1997). Supporting references are largely the author's own prior lunar-development papers.

### Key findings

The paper's contributions are the proposed framework and a set of quantitative reference points:

- **Private-sector premise.** Continued large-scale lunar development is argued to depend more on the private sector than on governments, for two reasons: governments are budget-cutting, and public support for space is weak and short-lived.

- **The "new paradigm" — substructuring into financeable units.** The core proposal is to break the long project into smaller, independent, profitable units, most based on **dual-use technologies** (valuable on Earth *and* for the Moon). A significant dollar-percentage of technologies must be dual-use so ventures return reasonable rates while collectively building lunar capability. Named example dual-use arenas include self-repairing systems, materials development/processing, low- and micro-gravity technology, robotic manipulators, instrumentation (material-integrity and fluid-flow monitoring), and micro/nano devices.

- **Lunar Development Corporation (LDC).** A proposed independent, preferably for-profit company acting as a venture-capital holding entity — pulling together management, science/engineering, financial, and legal teams to coordinate ventures, attract capital, prioritize technologies by critical-path and economic-benefit criteria, and ultimately contract the return to the Moon. Time frame: **~20 years** from LDC creation to the end of the first year of lunar colonization (versus a possible but unlikely 10 years under full taxpayer funding). LDC may start privately held and evolve into a public corporation, later adding debt financing alongside equity.

- **Launch economics.** Launch capability is placed at the head of the needed initiatives. Atlas and Delta rockets account for ~90% of US commercial launches, each lifting ~8,000–10,000 lb to geosynchronous orbit; an Atlas launch costs ~$80 million and a Delta ~$60 million, i.e., ~$6,000–$10,000/lb. Near-term (next-decade) costs are expected to roughly halve, but the paper states costs must fall **below ~$1,000/lb** for lunar development to become financially feasible. Worldwide national subsidization of rocket development is noted as favorable.

- **Lunar-resource assessment (skeptical).** Lunar rocks/soils are ~45% chemically bound oxygen by weight, with silicon, iron, calcium, aluminum, magnesium, and titanium extractable as byproducts, plus regolith-implanted helium, hydrogen, nitrogen, and carbon — enabling O₂/H₂ rocket fuels. Helium-3 is called the "brightest spot" as a potential fusion fuel, but the paper stresses fusion reactors are not yet engineered (a guessed ~three decades out, noted before Congress cut Princeton Tokamak funding). Conclusion stated plainly: there are **not enough raw materials on the Moon to justify taxation-funded colonization**; the paradigm shift is the better approach. Mars is said to offer even fewer economically viable rationales.

- **Reliability-based design.** The paper discusses acceptable failure rates, factors of safety, and redundancy. It proposes using a rare catastrophic-meteorite-strike probability as a "base risk" against which other natural and man-made risks (thermal-cycle fatigue, LOX-tank explosion, projectile penetration, human factors) are weighed, defining a minimum design risk R_min = min{R_meteorite, R_thermal-fatigue}. It argues against first-excursion failure (progressive/graceful failure required), for full sensoring/monitoring and some self-correction ("smart structures"), and weighs a two-tier concept: a higher-risk large outpost paired with a smaller high-safety refuge facility to cut cost. Logistics (minimum-time-to-maximum-payload) sets the required self-sufficiency time.

- **NASA's role (critical).** The paper argues NASA has become an impediment to entrepreneurial space development, citing its January 1998 announced-then-retracted plan to drop manned operations except the space station, and characterizing the Shuttle and station as politically driven. It suggests NASA should "move out of the way" for an entrepreneurial space program.

- **Long-term societal and environmental issues.** Assuming a well-developed lunar presence by ~2050 (a colony of 500 to several thousand), the paper argues for safeguarding lunar/planetary "integrity" in advance of explosive development: avoiding strip-mining-style devastation even at higher cost, applying democratic principles, and anticipating that colonies will inevitably seek autonomy and eventually independence (analogized to the American colonies). It lists Earth "mistakes" to avoid (pollution, over-exploitation of resources, degraded man-made environments).

### Limitations

The paper is explicitly a "prelude to a more specific proposal": it states cost estimates are premature, the LDC would be created "in various forms by various groups," and the dual-use technology tables are a preliminary list requiring prioritization. Claims are qualitative and assertion-driven rather than modeled or empirically validated; the reliability section presents notation and reasoning but no worked quantitative results. The resource assessment is a summary judgment, not a new analysis. Portions (NASA critique, colony-independence, environmental ethics) are the author's opinion/advocacy. Launch-cost and helium-3/fusion figures reflect the 1998 vantage point. The dual-use tables are adapted from a NASA Mars study rather than independently derived.
