# New and Revised Statistics for the U.S. Space Economy, 2017-2022

## Citation

Highfill, T., Georgi, P., & Surfield, C. (2024, June 25). New and revised statistics for the U.S.
space economy, 2017–2022. *Survey of Current Business*. U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis.
bea.gov/scb

Publisher URL: bea.gov/scb

## Abstract

BEA release article presenting new and revised space-economy satellite-account estimates for
2017–2022: real and current-dollar GDP (value added), gross output, private employment, and
private compensation, now published with 75-industry detail (up from ~30) and rebased to BEA's
2023 comprehensive National Economic Accounts update. Reports 2022 headline levels and 2017–2022
growth rates by series and industry, price-index movements, a revisions account versus the prior
June 2023 release, an overview of the supply-and-use-table methodology used to delineate the space
economy, and an experimental sub-estimate of space-related nondefense government employment.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The article is BEA's June 25, 2024 release of updated U.S. space economy statistics, authored by
Tina Highfill, Patrick Georgi, and Chris Surfield and published in the *Survey of Current
Business*, described on the page header as "The Journal of the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis."
It presents new 2022 estimates and revised 2017–2021 estimates of the space economy's contribution
to current-dollar and chained-dollar ("real") GDP and gross output by industry, along with private
employment and compensation. The stated occasion for revision is BEA's 2023 comprehensive update
of the National Economic Accounts, which the space economy statistics now incorporate. The article
states this is the first release of the space economy statistics formatted identically to BEA's
official industry statistics, expanding industry detail from just over 30 industries to 75 and
adding two new price-index datasets (for value added and for gross output) at the same 75-industry
level of detail. The report's stated structure is: an overview of major findings, a description of
revisions to the 2017–2021 estimates, a methodology overview, and a discussion of current and
future plans informed by BEA's first Space Economy Measurement Workshop (March 2024).

### Methods and scope

The statistics are constructed from BEA's comprehensive supply and use tables (SUTs) and National
Income and Product Accounts (NIPAs), which the article describes as the standard architecture
underlying U.S. national accounts: SUTs track flows of goods and services purchased by industry,
incomes earned by industry, and the distribution of sales by product, while NIPA data present the
value, composition, and associated employment of U.S. GDP. The Economic Census is stated as the
primary SUT data source, supplemented by data from the U.S. Departments of Agriculture, Education,
and Energy and private organizations. BEA defines the space economy as space-related goods and
services that (1) are used in space or directly support those used in space, (2) require direct
input from space to function or directly support those that do, or (3) are associated with
studying space. Rather than a new survey, the space economy statistics are described as a
rearrangement of existing SUT/NIPA data: qualifying products are selected from BEA's list of nearly
5,000 SUT goods-and-services categories to isolate space-attributable production and spending
already embedded in the accounts (e.g., isolating space-related educational services or
space-related construction spending from the broader educational-services or construction totals).

Space products were identified using prior research and subject-matter input, including the OECD
Handbook on Measuring the Space Economy, a Bureau of Industry and Security assessment of the U.S.
space industry supply chain, private-sector reports (Satellite Industry Association, Space
Foundation), input from U.S. and international space agencies (NASA, the Canadian Space Agency,
the Australian Space Agency), BEA industry-analyst input, and industry expert feedback. Where a
single SUT product category comingles space and nonspace activity (e.g., a single "internet
services" category), external indicator data are used to isolate the space share; revenue or
spending indicators are used where available, with employment and wage data substituted when
revenue/spending data are not available. Table 3 in the source lists the supplementary data
sources by industry group: manufacturing/wholesale trade/retail trade draw on Census Economic
Census product-line data and government budget documents; information draws on BLS Occupational
Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS), FCC data, and SEC data; government draws on public budget
documents and the NSF Survey of Federal Funds for Research and Development; professional and
business services draw on BLS OEWS and both NSF surveys (Federal Funds and Business Enterprise
R&D); construction draws on the Census Value of Construction Put in Place Survey; and other
services (launch, insurance, education, observatories, planetariums) draw on AXA XL and the
National Center for Education Statistics' Integrated Postsecondary Education Data System.

GDP (value added) by industry for the space economy is derived from the ratio of an industry's
space-attributable output to its total output, applying the assumption that the intermediate-
consumption share for space-related output equals the industry's overall intermediate-consumption
share. Private-sector compensation and employment are derived by applying that same output ratio
to each industry's total employment and compensation. The article notes this method surfaces
"secondary production" — space-related output from industries not conventionally considered part
of the space sector, such as oil and gas extraction and mining-support-activities industries,
which the detailed industry tables show contributing $2–10 million annually to space economy GDP
between 2017 and 2022 (R&D performed by mining-industry firms with space applications, not mining
conducted in space). Scope is 2017–2022 only; this release corresponds to the first phase of BEA's
comprehensive industry-statistics update (which covered 2017–2022), with a second phase (2016 and
earlier) still pending and slated to extend the space economy series back to 2012 in a future
release. A separate, non-SUT-based methodology (public government budget documents and datasets)
is used to produce experimental estimates of space-related nondefense government employment,
because BEA's industry data lack the detailed agency-level employment/compensation breakdowns
needed to apply the output-ratio method used for private industry.

### Key findings

**2022 headline levels and growth (Table 1, current 2022 dollars unless noted):**
- GDP (value added): $131,776 million ($131.8 billion), equal to 0.5 percent of total U.S. GDP in
  2022; current-dollar GDP growth of 6.0 percent in 2022; 2017–2022 average annual growth of 1.3
  percent.
- Real GDP, chained (2017) dollars: $113,153 million; 2022 growth of 2.3 percent (versus 1.9
  percent real growth for the overall U.S. economy the same year); 2017–2022 average annual growth
  of −1.7 percent.
- Gross output: $232,061 million ($232.1 billion); 2022 growth of 7.4 percent; 2017–2022 average
  annual growth of 2.0 percent.
- Real gross output, chained (2017) dollars: $201,288 million; 2022 growth of 2.9 percent;
  2017–2022 average annual growth of −0.9 percent.
- Private-sector compensation: $54,453 million ($54.5 billion); 2022 growth of 2.8 percent;
  2017–2022 average annual growth of 1.7 percent.
- Private employment, full- and part-time: 347,000 jobs; 2022 growth of 1.8 percent; 2017–2022
  average annual growth of −1.9 percent.

The article states that 2022 growth outpaced the 2017–2022 average for every series, "reflecting a
rebound from slow growth and declines in many key industries in 2020 and 2021" — i.e., the negative
or below-2022 average annual growth figures reflect a pandemic-era drag. Comparing current-dollar
GDP growth (6.0 percent) to real GDP growth (2.3 percent) in 2022, the article attributes the gap
mainly to inflation/price change rather than volume growth.

**Industry drivers of 2022 growth:** Real GDP growth of 2.3 percent in the space economy was
propelled by growth across all categories of government (7.2 percent), most notably federal
national defense (16.2 percent), and by professional, scientific, and technical services (11.7
percent, double-digit). Both federal national defense and professional/scientific/technical
services are described as heavily R&D-driven. Real gross output grew 2.9 percent in 2022 with
"similar industry trends." Growth was partly offset by contractions in broadcasting and
telecommunications (−3.0 percent), led by declining direct-to-home (DTH) satellite television
revenue and subscribers over the full 2017–2022 period, and in "other transportation equipment"
manufacturing (−7.3 percent in 2022), a category consisting mostly of space vehicles and space
weapons systems that swung from −7.3 percent (2022) to +23.0 percent (2019) across the period.
Government-industry GDP is defined as government employee compensation (federal, state, local)
plus consumption of fixed capital; the article states the majority of government space production
is federal, chiefly NASA and the U.S. Space Force. Table 2 lists the federal departments,
agencies, and federally funded R&D centers with direct space activity feeding the government
estimates, grouped under three category headers: Nondefense (U.S. Department of Commerce, U.S.
Department of Energy, U.S. Department of the Interior, U.S. Department of Transportation, NASA,
the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration, the National Science Foundation, and the Smithsonian Institution — NASA is listed
under this Nondefense category), Defense (the U.S. Air Force, U.S. Army, U.S. Navy, the Defense
Advanced Research Projects Agency, the Defense Information Systems Agency, the Defense Innovation
Unit, the Defense Logistics Agency, the Missile Defense Agency, the Office of the Secretary of
Defense, the Space Development Agency, and the U.S. Space Force), and Federally Funded Research
and Development Centers (Aerospace, Brookhaven National Laboratory, Jet Propulsion Laboratory,
Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Lincoln Laboratory, Los Alamos National Laboratory, the
National Center for Atmospheric Research, and the National Optical Astronomy, Radio Astronomy, and
Solar Observatories).

**Narrower definition (addendum):** An addendum reports space economy values excluding DTH
satellite television, satellite radio, and educational services. Under this narrower definition,
2022 real value-added growth was 3.9 percent (versus 2.3 percent for the standard, broad
definition), and real gross output growth was 5.1 percent (versus 2.9 percent broad). The article
attributes the faster narrow-definition growth mainly to removing DTH satellite television, whose
ongoing decline has an "outsized impact" on the overall space economy.

**Prices:** Value-added price indexes rose in most industries in 2021–2022. Wholesale trade rose
39.7 percent in 2021 (versus 6.8 percent for the total space economy) and 7.9 percent in 2022
(versus 3.6 percent for the total space economy); wholesale trade represents the margins charged by
distributors of space-related items such as GPS transceivers and antennae, and the article notes
this pattern mirrors price increases in the household-appliance and electrical/electronic-goods
merchant-wholesaler industry. Wholesale trade equaled 19 percent of space economy GDP in 2022, so
its price movements are said to significantly affect overall space economy growth. Manufacturing
moved the opposite direction: total manufacturing value-added prices declined 2.4 percent on
average over 2017–2022 (largest single-year decline of −4.4 percent in 2018), driven by falling
prices and quality increases in computer and electronic products (satellite and ground equipment,
GPS equipment, positioning/navigation/timing [PNT] equipment).

**Private employment and compensation (2022):** 347,000 private jobs and $54.5 billion in
compensation. Manufacturing accounted for 31.1 percent of private employment but 43.1 percent of
compensation, reflecting a relatively high average annual compensation of $217,296. Information was
the second-largest industry by employment (21.9 percent) and third-largest by compensation (18.0
percent). Wholesale trade was second-largest by compensation (19.2 percent) and third-largest by
employment (21.6 percent), with average annual compensation of $139,333 versus $128,789 for
information.

**Revisions versus the June 2023 release:** Revisions to 2017–2021 estimates were driven mainly by
BEA's 2023 comprehensive update of the National Economic Accounts, a benchmarking exercise
performed roughly every five years against the U.S. Census Bureau's quinquennial Economic Census.
Wholesale trade value-added levels were revised up $2–4 billion per year; computer and electronic
products manufacturing levels were revised down $3–8 billion per year; both revisions are
attributed to updated Economic Census business revenue data. Wholesale trade's 2017–2021 average
annual growth was revised down from 3.7 percent to 2.0 percent. Computer and electronic products
manufacturing's average annual growth was revised from +1.5 percent to −2.6 percent, described as
fluctuating (not uniformly shifted) across the time series. A second major change was moving the
chained-dollar/price reference year from 2012 to 2017, which the article states makes the real
value-added and gross output figures in this release not directly comparable to earlier releases.
Additional, smaller 2021 updates incorporated new R&D spending data (NSF Business Enterprise
Research and Development Survey and NSF Survey of Federal Funds for Research and Development),
satellite-internet usage data (FCC), and updated federal defense space-weapons-systems and
investment spending data; the article states none of these caused notable revisions to levels or
growth rates for any industry.

**Space-related nondefense government employment (Table I, experimental estimates):** Total
available nondefense federal/state/local space employment rose from 19,017 (2017) to 19,699 (2022),
an average annual growth rate of 0.7 percent. Nondefense federal space total rose from 17,951 to
18,478 over the same span. NASA (excluding aeronautical employment), the largest reporting agency,
rose from 15,728 (2017) to 16,128 (2022) (annual series: 15,728; 15,455; 15,654; 15,871; 15,762;
16,128). NOAA's space total, the second-largest nondefense workforce in 2022, rose from 1,977
(2017) to 2,056 (2022), comprising the National Environmental Satellite, Data, and Information
Service (742→794), the National Ocean Service (547→572), and the National Weather Service
(688→690). Smaller reporting units: U.S. Geological Survey's National Land Imaging/Landsat program,
148 (2017) to 177 (2022); FAA Office of Commercial Space Transportation, 98 (2017) to 117 (2022).
State and local space total rose from 1,066 (2017) to 1,221 (2022), almost entirely estimated
state-university space-related faculty (1,059→1,214), plus the Oklahoma Space Industry Development
Authority (7 in both 2017 and 2022). Not included in this table: U.S. Space Force space-related
defense employment, reported separately at 12,764 for 2022 (Space Force personnel were first broken
out from total Air Force figures starting in 2021). Revisions to the 2017–2021 government
employment figures are attributed mainly to updated National Center for Education Statistics data.

**Current and future plans:** BEA's first Space Economy Measurement Workshop (March 2024) convened
experts from government, private industry, academia, and international organizations (including
OECD, NASA, the U.S. Space Force, the Canadian Space Agency, and the National Space Council). Two
takeaways prioritized for future releases, subject to resources and data availability, were demand
for more timely estimates and demand for state-level estimates. Workshop participants showed
"limited interest" in reorganizing the statistics around activity categories used in private
industry reports (e.g., launch and satellite services), on the reasoning that divergent private
and BEA figures for the same nominal category would likely cause confusion, and that BEA's
industry-level statistics already provide a view not available in private industry reporting.

### Limitations

The article states plainly that classified government space activity performed by national
intelligence personnel is excluded from the government industry statistics for lack of source
data, and that government purchases from private industry (e.g., a Space Force satellite purchase)
appear as production within the private industry that produced the good, not as government
production. The 2012-to-2017 chained-dollar reference-year change means the real value-added and
gross output series in this release are explicitly stated as not directly comparable to prior
releases. Data availability is limited to 2017–2022 in this release; the pre-2017 series (back to
2012) awaits the second phase of BEA's comprehensive update. The space economy's product boundary
is not a fixed, self-evident category: it is built by selecting products from a general-purpose
5,000-category list, comingled product categories require external indicator data (revenue,
spending, or, failing that, employment/wage data) to split space from nonspace shares, and the
article itself offers two coexisting boundary definitions (standard and a narrower one excluding
DTH satellite television, satellite radio, and educational services) rather than a single
authoritative delineation. Most products are assigned to a single "primary" industry for
industry-level GDP/output attribution even though some products (e.g., guided-missile and
space-vehicle equipment) are also produced by other industries; "secondary production" from
non-primary industries is captured only via the underlying product-level data, not the primary
headline industry breakdown. The space-related government employment estimates are explicitly
labeled "experimental" and "prototype," built from a different method (public budget documents)
than the output-ratio method used for private employment/compensation, and are stated to be
incomplete: excluded are state and local spaceports and administration (aside from Oklahoma) and
NSF-funded research and development centers and observatories. U.S. Space Force employment is only
separately reported from total Air Force figures beginning in 2021, limiting earlier-year
comparability for that line.
