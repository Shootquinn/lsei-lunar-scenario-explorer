# Building an Economical and Sustainable Lunar Infrastructure (Lunar COTS)

## Citation

Zuniga, A. F., Turner, M., Rasky, D., Loucks, M., Carrico, J., & Policastri, L. (2017). *Building an economical and sustainable lunar infrastructure to enable lunar industrialization* (AIAA 2017-5148). AIAA SPACE and Astronautics Forum and Exposition, 12-14 September 2017, Orlando, FL. American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics. https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2017-5148

Publisher URL: https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/10.2514/6.2017-5148

## Abstract

A NASA Ames concept-study paper proposing "Lunar COTS" (Commercial Operations and Transport Services), an extension of NASA's Commercial Orbital Transportation Services public/private-partnership model to cis-lunar space and the lunar surface. It defines the concept's goals, a three-phase development approach, and a reference lunar architecture (lander, solar/battery power station, communication tower, navigation, thermal control, rovers), then reports quantitative trajectory and mass analyses for delivering that architecture on commercial launch vehicles. Scope covers acquisition strategy, infrastructure element design, a survey of prior lunar surface missions, and delta-V/payload estimates; findings are from conceptual design plus numerical simulation, not flight hardware.

## Summary

### Background and objective
The paper opens with historical analogies in which government infrastructure investment sparked economic growth in new frontiers (Spanish exploration; the U.S. westward expansion via the Louisiana Purchase, Lewis and Clark, and the transcontinental railroad completed in 1869), contrasted with Antarctica's McMurdo Station as a case where a science-and-environmental-protection focus, a mining ban (1991 Madrid Protocol), lack of competition, and a culture discouraging private investment produced little economic development. The authors argue that if the Moon is developed under Antarctica-like guidelines it will likewise see little economic growth, and that a space-based economy therefore requires deliberate government incentives, policy, partnerships, and investment. The objective is to examine extending NASA's COTS acquisition model to build an economical, evolvable, sustainable lunar infrastructure through public/private partnerships.

### Methods and scope
The study combines acquisition-model analysis, conceptual reference-architecture design, a review of historical lunar surface missions, and quantitative mission analysis. Trajectory delta-V values were estimated analytically (vis-viva/orbital energy conservation and the rocket equation) and confirmed with high-fidelity numerical simulations in STK/Astrogator, including gravitational effects of Earth, Moon, and Sun and LOI gravity losses. Launch-vehicle performance was estimated from publicly available GTO payload figures scaled to trans-lunar trajectories (scale factors 1.0-1.2). Two Design Reference Missions (DRM-1, DRM-2) were constructed for baseline estimates.

### Key findings

**COTS model and precedent.** The COTS program, with the ISS Commercial Resupply Services contracts (SpaceX and Orbital Sciences/Orbital ATK awarded 2008), produced two new launch-vehicle/spacecraft systems servicing ISS since 2012 (Falcon 9/Dragon; Antares/Cygnus). Government funding covered less than half of development (47% for SpaceX, 42% for Orbital). Falcon 9 development cost is cited at about $400M, roughly 10 times less than a projected approximately $4B under traditional cost-plus contracting. Six best-practice principles are listed (shared cost/risk, long-term NASA service commitments, industry targeting non-government markets with NASA as anchor but not sole customer, Funded Space Act Agreements for flexibility, pay-on-performance milestones as off-ramps, and industry retention of IP and ownership).

**Three-phase approach.** Phase 1: partner with industry to develop capabilities (lunar cargo delivery, power stations, communication towers) and assess sites/economics. Phase 2: pilot-scale demonstration of infrastructure services supporting future NASA missions and commercial activities such as lunar mining/resource extraction. Phase 3: NASA awards long-term contracts for infrastructure services and potentially full-scale resource production/delivery. The phased plan provides off-ramps and reassessment between phases.

**Environmental challenge.** The Moon's synodic period of about 29.5 days gives roughly 14.77 days of sunlight then 14.77 days of darkness. Equatorial daytime temperatures reach 127 deg C and night reaches -173 deg C; polar temperatures reach about -258 deg C. Survival of the 14-day lunar night is identified as the greatest design challenge.

**Historical mission review.** There have been 20 successful controlled soft landings. The Soviet Luna program landed eight times (Luna 9 was the first soft landing on another Solar System body, Feb 1966), with sample returns (Luna 16, 20, 24) and the first roving vehicles (Lunokhod 1 and 2 on Luna 17 and 21). NASA's Surveyor program (1966-68) launched 7, with 5 successful landings. Seven of the 20 missions survived one or more lunar nights: four Surveyors (powered by a 0.85 m2 solar array generating up to 85 W into silver-zinc batteries), the two Lunokhod rovers (about 180 W, polonium-210 RHU; Lunokhod 2 traveled up to 39 km before dust on its radiators caused overheating), and Chang'e 3/Yutu (2013; Yutu used a plutonium-238 RHU). Apollo ALSEP packages (missions 12, 14, 15, 16, 17) used plutonium-238 RTGs generating about 70 W and operated until turned off September 30, 1977. The authors note radioisotope units (RHUs/RTGs) add cost, complexity, scarcity, regulation, and safety burdens, making them undesirable for low-cost commercial missions; the reference architecture therefore uses solar power and batteries only.

**Reference architecture design.** The lunar lander uses a LADEE-heritage common bus: 2 m hexagonal aluminum structure, three self-leveling tripod legs, fitting a 4 m fairing; requirements are landing within 100 m of target, a landed dry mass of 900-1000 kg including payload, and payload delivery of 350-450 kg. The power station uses body-mounted and deployable solar panels with lithium-ion batteries; 2-4 m2 of panel area generates roughly 800-1600 W during lunar day, and about 4 m2 normal to the Sun could charge up to 36 battery modules in about 15 hours. Using 24-36 ABSL lithium-ion modules (6-8 year life expectancy, LADEE heritage), about 40-75 W of continuous power would run heaters through the 14-day night; module mass of 150-250 kg is the main payload-mass driver, and the power station is concluded to endure 6-8 years (battery-limited). An 8-10 m telescopic communication tower with an S-band or X-band transponder links rovers (up to 500 m away) and Earth ground stations, enabling rover operation in craters and caves. Rovers are allocated 50 kg each (two per lander), deployed by cable, using inductive charging, hibernation, and payloads up to 10 kg (candidate instruments listed in Table 2: neutron spectrometer, NIRVSS, camera/NIR spectrometer, radiation sensors, magnetometer, seismometer).

**Quantitative mission analysis.** Estimated delta-V values (m/s): LEO-to-GTO 2,426; TLI 3,105; Lunar Orbit Insertion 835; Descent and Landing 1,822; total lunar mission 8,188. Landing-site delta-V varied less than one percent across North Pole, South Pole, Taurus Littrow, and Lacus Mortis. Total-mass and payload estimates to the lunar surface were computed for Atlas 551, Falcon 9 FT, Falcon Heavy, Vulcan ACES, and New Glenn; for example Falcon Heavy gives 8.1-11 metric tons total mass with 1.5-3.9 mt payload. Isp of the lander engines (ranging 285-336 s) was the largest driver of payload mass; the parking-orbit altitude had only a small effect and the trajectory-optimization scale factor affected mass by about 20 percent. The paper recommends higher-Isp lander engines. DRM-1 (medium-class Falcon 9 or Atlas V, delivering 1-2 landers) demonstrates cargo delivery, solar power/storage, communication links, continuous operation through darkness, rover life extension, and imaging; launch-to-landing spans 7.6-14.6 days. DRM-2 (heavy-class Falcon Heavy, Vulcan ACES, or New Glenn) delivers up to 4 landers to multiple sites and adds multi-rover recharging, multi-tower communications, and landing-beacon triangulation.

**Enabling lunar industrialization.** SpaceX operations cost is cited at about $62M per launch, roughly $2720/kg to LEO. The paper surveys commercial deep-space efforts investing their own resources: SpaceX Falcon Heavy (about 5 million pounds liftoff thrust, planned 2018 lunar-tourism circumlunar flight), Blue Origin's proposed Blue Moon cargo lander, NASA's Lunar CATALYST no-funds-exchanged Space Act Agreements with Astrobotic, Masten, and Moon Express, Astrobotic's ULA-launched Peregrine mission planned for 2019, and ULA's Cislunar 1000 vision (1000 people in cislunar space within 30 years) using the reusable ACES stage refueled with lunar-derived LO2/LH2.

### Limitations
The work is a conceptual study, not flight hardware; a specific rover design was declared out of scope. Launch-vehicle performance rests on publicly available GTO figures scaled by assumed factors, introducing uncertainty, and several vehicles cited were then undeveloped. Phase 2 and 3 goals are described as tentative and generic, to be refined by ground truth from Phase 1. Power, mass, and endurance figures are preliminary estimates from analysis rather than tested results, and market/programmatic claims depend on assumed commercial demand and continued partnership funding.
