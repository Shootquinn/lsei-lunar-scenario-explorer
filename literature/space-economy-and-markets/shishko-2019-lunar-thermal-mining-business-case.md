## Abstract

Presents a "lean" Business Case Analysis (BCA) framework and an engineering-based production-rate model for a lunar thermal-mining concept (solar-thermal sublimation of water ice under a mobile tent, in a permanently shadowed region, using reflectors to deliver heat). States the deck's purpose as establishing whether and at what demand level the business case for lunar-sourced propellant closes against Earth-delivered propellant, and where tipping points occur. Opens with a literature review of prior lunar-mining BCAs (Charania and DePasquale 2007; Kornuta et al. 2018; Jones et al. 2019; Pelech, Roesler, and Saydam 2019; Bennett and Dempster 2019), then works through the production-rate model's design vector, a power/illumination sub-model, a partial engineering database, and the BCA spreadsheet's cost, depreciation, and profit-and-loss modules using a single worked example (a Cold Trap/Ice Hauler asset). The deck's own financial model, run with no sales revenue entered, returns a Net Present Value of 0.00 and an undefined Project Internal Rate of Return (#NUM!) at a 12.5 percent discount rate (slide 15), so the deck demonstrates the modeling framework rather than presenting a solved business case.

### Literature review of prior BCAs (slide 2)

The deck opens by stating that previous BCAs of lunar mining concepts "contain multiple assumptions and draw different conclusions" (slide 2, p. 2). The figures below are quoted by Shishko, Stoica, and Mrozinski from these prior authors' own publications; they are not results computed by Shishko et al., and are reported here as attributed quotations, not as this source's own findings.

- Charania and DePasquale (2007, IAC Paper 07-A5.1.03): a financial model that solves for a sales price per kg such that NPV at a 21.7 percent discount rate over 18 years equals zero; uses $80K/kg for equipment capital costs plus $90K/kg for lunar deployment (FY19 dollars); a 20.94 mt mine, including a nuclear power plant, is stated to produce 49.4 mt/year of propellant. Three delivery scenarios (lunar surface, low lunar orbit, geostationary orbit) give sales prices of $35.3K/kg, $176.1K/kg, and greater than $9M/kg respectively (slide 2).
- Kornuta et al. (2018, CSM/ULA, "Commercial Lunar Propellant Architecture"): a financial model computing NPV and IRR; uses $100K/kg equipment capital cost plus $35K/kg lunar deployment; a 30 mt mine is stated to produce 1,640 mt/year of propellant. Seven demand scenarios are modeled, of which two return NPV less than 0 and five return NPV greater than 0; the LEO delivery market is stated as the worst case, with price depending on delivery location (slide 2).
- Jones et al. (2019, AIAA-2019-1372, SciTech Forum): uses kg/(kg/year) and $/kg factors "leveraged from CxP" for surface mining systems and from LSPC for commercial launch vehicles, with a rough-order-of-magnitude figure for SLS that is stated as not revealed. To a cis-lunar aggregation point, the source is quoted as finding "lunar ISRU propellant is 97% more expensive than Earth-based propellant," at $40K versus $78K per kg (slide 2).
- Pelech, Roesler, and Saydam (2019, *Acta Astronautica* 162, pp. 388 to 404): described as avoiding cost and NPV metrics altogether, instead using a Propellant Payback Ratio (PPR) and containing "many useful mining equations." Lunar in-situ ice sublimation is stated to have a PPR less than 1 "even after many years" (slide 2).
- Bennett and Dempster (2019, submitted to *Planetary and Space Science*): uses the same $/kg factors as Kornuta et al.; calculates NPV and IRR relying on a propellant "burn-to-deliver ratio" from the rocket equation. Geosynchronous transfer orbit (GTO) is stated as a more viable market than LEO for impulse delivery, with NPV and IRR much higher (and positive) due to the smaller scale of lunar operations required (slide 2).

### Purpose and framework (slides 3 to 4)

The stated purpose (slide 3) is twofold: (1) establish the case for the proposed ISRU technology against delivering propellant from Earth or another alternative, including at what demand level the business case closes and where tipping points occur; and (2) develop an engineering-based production-rate model for the proposed ISRU technology that enables tradespace exploration, providing quantitative design-parameter estimates for cost models, identifying which design parameters most affect production, and identifying which feasible parameter combinations "make the most sense." A note on the slide states each ISRU technology requires its own custom production-rate model (slide 3).

The "Lean BCA Framework" (slide 4) is presented as a block diagram linking a Profit & Loss module (computing NPV/IRR) and a Balance Sheet module (via Cash Flow) to underlying inputs: Market Parameters and Cost Breakdown Structure feed Revenues and Cost of Goods Sold; Depreciation & Amortization and a Production Model feed Tangible Assets and a Model-Based Asset Cost, together with ConOps (concept of operations) and MELs (Master Equipment Lists).

### Lunar thermal mining concept (slides 5 to 6)

The mining concept operates in permanently shadowed craters (slide 5). The concept of operations (slide 6), credited to Sowers, Dreyer, and Williams (2019, Colorado School of Mines), is a schematic process flow: launch; deploy and set up; (re)position tent and mirrors; solar heating of the surface; collect ice in a cold trap; transport ice to a refinery; purify ice to water; electrolysis; propellant storage and handling. A callout details the tent-move cycle: detach cold traps, jack the tent up onto skids, attach haulers to hauling lugs, drag the tent to a new location, detach haulers, lower the tent to the ground, reposition the rim mirror, reattach cold traps, and begin heating again. The slide notes all operations are tele-operated from Earth.

### Production-rate model, "Threads of Calculation" (slides 7 to 9)

The production model's baseline design vector and computed outputs are laid out as a linked spreadsheet diagram (slide 7). Printed input values include: 3,600 sec/hour, 12 months/year, 0 hours/month monthly downtime, 8,766 hours/year, 12 hours intersite move time, 0.3 m effective regolith depth, 4.00 percent regolith water by weight, 1.50E+03 kg/m^3 regolith density, a 1.60E+06 kg/year water requirement (target), 1.0 m^2 deposition area per cold trap, 2 cold trap/ice haulers per tent, 44 hours site dwell time, 6,000 kg cold-trap/ice-hauler effective capacity, 800 kg/hour cold-trap discharge rate, 2.5 hours one-way hauler travel time, 300 m distance to the production site, 120 m/hour hauler speed over terrain, a 29 m tent diameter, 97.00 percent tent efficiency, 0.2 m^2 leak loss area, and 220 K (-53.15 C) temperature, together with physical constants (molar mass of water 1.801528E-02 kg/mol, R = 8.3145 J/mol*K, pi = 3.14159).

At these baseline values the model computes: 100.00 percent tent availability, 156 annual tent moves, 1.80E+01 kg/m^2 ice sublimated before losses, 1.64E+01 kg/m^2 ice yield after losses, 2.0 m^2 total cold-trap deposition area, 640.70 m^2 effective tent area, 9.995E+04 m^2/year (24.70 acres/year) annual area mined, 1.64E+06 kg/year water production and annual harvest, 238.26535 kg/hour (0.066184818 kg/sec) deposition rate on cold traps, 23.82653 kg/hour (0.006618482 kg/sec) loss rate, 26.43181818 Pa average pressure over dwell time, an 11.552-hour cold-trap/ice-hauler turnaround time, and a 758.16 kg hauler margin at dwell time (slide 7). At this baseline vector, computed annual water production (about 1.64E+06 kg/year) meets the stated 1.60E+06 kg/year water requirement.

The governing equations printed on slide 7 include: deposition loss mass rate as a function of vapor pressure, loss area, and the Hertz-Knudsen sublimation term sqrt(M(H2O)/(2*pi*R*T)); Annual Harvest = (Deposition Rate)(Site Dwell Time)(Annual Moves); Annual Moves = INT[(Hours Per Year x Tent Availability)/(Site Dwell Time + Intersite Move Time)]; Annual Area Mined = (Effective Tent Area)(Annual Moves); Water Production = (Annual Area Mined)(Ice Yield After Losses); Tent Availability = 1 minus [(Monthly Downtime)(Months Per Year)/(Hours Per Year)]; Ice Sublimated Before Losses = (Regolith Percent Water by Weight)(Regolith Density)(Effective Depth); Ice Yield After Losses = Ice Sublimated Before Losses x [1 minus A(loss)/(A(loss)+A(cold trap))]; Effective Tent Area = pi x (Tent Diameter/2)^2 x Tent Efficiency; A(cold traps) = (Cold Traps Per Tent)(Deposition Area Per Cold Trap).

A downtime sensitivity comparison (slide 8), holding months per year, hours per year, intersite move time, and site dwell time fixed, shows: at 0 hours/month monthly downtime, tent availability is 100.00 percent and annual moves are 156/year; at 90 hours/month monthly downtime, tent availability falls to 87.68 percent and annual moves fall to 137/year.

The model is extended with a solar power and illumination sub-model (slide 9): secondary optics transmitter efficiency 97.00 percent, secondary optics effective size 6.00 m^2, average delivered thermal power 1.607 kW, distance from TransFormer (TF) mirror to tent 10,000 m, solar irradiance at 1 AU 1,353.3 W/m^2, solar irradiance at the tent 298.372977 W/m^2, TF mirror diameter 40 m, TF mirror tower height 50 m, number of TransFormers 2, average solar illumination at the TF 92.56 percent, average solar irradiance at the tent 276.1628013 W/m^2, and 0 percent site terrain masking degradation. The printed relation states solar irradiance at the tent as a function of TF mirror diameter, distance from TF to tent, and solar irradiance at 1 AU. A slide note states the design vector totals 22 input variables so far, excluding physical constants.

### Average solar illumination (slide 10)

A chart, credited to Henrickson and Stoica (2017, IEEE Aerospace Conference), plots average annual illumination (0.92 to 0.99 on the y-axis) against reflector height (0 to 500 m on the x-axis) for two TransFormer reflectors located at 89.9029 S 145.2301 W and 89.6876 S 162.8645 W. Plotted values rise monotonically with height (approximately 0.925 at 50 m, 0.941 at 100 m, 0.953 at 150 m, 0.964 at 200 m, 0.972 at 250 m, 0.977 at 300 m, 0.982 at 350 m, 0.986 at 400 m, 0.989 at 450 m, read from the chart). A logistic curve is fit to the data and printed: L = exp(2.26844 + 0.00504*H) / [1 + exp(2.26844 + 0.00504*H)], where H is reflector height in meters. A companion grayscale illumination map of the Shackleton crater region marks the two reflector locations.

The "Lunar Thermal Mining In-Service Elements" table (slide 12) lists Scenario 1's eight equipment items with operating location and required operational/spares quantities: Thermal Tent Mobility/Setup Robotics (PSR, 3 operational, 2 spares); Cold Trap/Ice Hauler (PSR, 2 operational, 1 spare); Solar Reflectors/TransFormers (Crater Rim, 3 operational, 0 spares); ISRU Water Purification and H2-O2 Production (PSR, 2 operational, 0 spares); Liquefaction Unit (PSR, 1 operational, 0 spares); Integrated Cryo-Propellant Hauler w/CFM (PSR, 2 operational, 0 spares); Thermal Tent w/Secondary Optics (PSR, 1 operational, 0 spares); Power Generation Station (PSR, 1 operational, 0 spares).

### Results recapitulation and forward work (slides 16 to 18)

The recapitulation (slide 16) states an engineering-based production-rate model is essential to build a defensible BCA, and reports progress on a thermal-mining production-rate model as a function of the deployed "assets." It notes reliability and maintainability (MTBF, MTTR) matter for estimating production rate and standby-asset counts; robotic travel distance and speed over terrain could add waiting time and reduce production tempo; the Cold Trap/Ice Hauler dry mass needed to be about 15 to 30 percent larger than previously published figures to be internally consistent; location (percent water by weight) and element geometry matter (solar thermal power delivered within the tent depends strongly on distance from the crater-rim-sited reflectors, and on reflector diameter and height above terrain); and that estimating element costs will be "a continuing challenge," since current spacecraft cost models may not be appropriate for surface systems.

"Moving Forward" (slide 17) lists open items: the relationship between regolith/ice thermodynamic properties and ice-deposition rate (via tent pressure build-up) is not yet established, including the relationship between delivered solar thermal power and tent pressure build-up as a function of percent water by weight; thermal diffusivity of regolith at PSR conditions; sublimation, vapor transport, and deposition processes and rates; and effects of ice grain size, porosity, and impurities remain open, with these four items flagged as covered by References 3 to 8 in the backup. The slide also calls for actual lunar demonstrations informed by laboratory simulation, more detail in the operations concept to validate the production-rate model, incorporation of both market (demand) and physical (e.g., water content in regolith) uncertainty, and a new approach to estimating and scaling costs for lunar surface systems, stating "$ per kg is just not sufficient for high confidence."

General Observations (slide 18): the "good news" is that business economists, mining engineers, and space systems engineers "are beginning to have the needed conversations"; the "bad news" is that real BCA models and calculations need to replace the generally "toy models" used in the past, and cost engineers "need to step up to this challenge." The slide states there is no consensus on market demand, no consensus on the capital expenditure needed for a lunar "mine," and no consensus even on the cost of a propellant delivery service from Earth to various cis-lunar nodes, "much less from the lunar surface."

### Backup material: framework spreadsheet structure and definitions (slides 19 to 23)

The backup section (slide 20) lists the Lean Framework's implementing spreadsheet tabs: System MEL (one sheet per system, containing spacecraft/surface-system mass and ConOps); Launch Vehicles and Costs; Detailed In-Service Schedule (optional, quarterly asset placement and launch-service costs); Cost Breakdown Structure; Schedule and Activity Cost (optional, detailed quarterly cost accounting rolling to the COGS sheet); Cost of Goods Sold (COGS); Tangible Assets (asset characteristics, development/production costs and durations, replacement schedules, depreciation type, buy-or-lease, cost spreading across years); Depreciation (straight-line or accelerated, computed via VBA code); Thermal Mining Production Model (determines how many mining surface systems are needed to satisfy annual customer demand); Power and Illumination Model (determines how many power surface systems are needed); Detailed Revenue Forecast (optional, quarterly); Sales (aggregates sales/revenues by revenue stream, allowing year-to-year price changes); Cash Flow (computes cash flow and accounts for sources of capital); Balance Sheet (aggregates assets and liabilities, computes total equity); and Profit and Loss (aggregates revenues, costs, depreciation, and taxes to net profit, and computes NPV and IRR "when feasible").

The reference list (slide 21) cites thirteen works, including Kornuta et al. (2018) with a URL (http://isruinfo.com/docs/Commercial%20Lunar%20Propellant%20Architecture.pdf), Sowers, Dreyer, and Williams (2019, Colorado School of Mines), and a set of ice-sublimation and volatile-transport physics references (Kossacki and Leliwa-Kopystynski 2014; Hedge et al., NASA/TM-2012-217441; Andreas 2007; Piquette, Horanyi, and Stern 2017; Reiss 2018; Frias, Shafirovich, and Van Woerkom 2014) covering the open sublimation/deposition physics flagged on slide 17, plus the TransFormers reflector references (Stoica et al., NIAC Phase I and Phase II final reports; Henrickson and Stoica 2017) and lunar-illumination references (Mazarico et al. 2011; Bryant 2010).

Slides 22 and 23 give the formal definitions of Average Solar Illumination and Average Solar Visibility used elsewhere in the deck: Average Solar Illumination is defined as a time-weighted integral of V(t), the fraction of the solar disk visible at a location, with a threshold parameter deciding which visibility levels count; Average Solar Visibility is the analogous integral using a step (0 or 1) indicator rather than the continuous visibility fraction, so that Average Solar Visibility is stated to not equal Average Solar Illumination in general. Both slides show a schematic V(t) sketch (axis marks at 1.0, 0.5, 0.0) with intervals labeled "solar disk not visible"; no plotted numeric data appears on either slide.

### Key findings

- Purpose (slide 3): establish the case for lunar ISRU propellant against Earth-delivered alternatives, determine the demand level at which the business case closes, identify tipping points, and build an engineering-based production-rate model for tradespace exploration.
- The literature-review figures on slide 2 (the 20.94 mt mine producing 49.4 mt/year of propellant; the 30 mt mine producing 1,640 mt/year; the 97 percent Earth-propellant cost premium; and all sales-price and NPV/IRR figures on that slide) are quotations of prior authors' results (Charania and DePasquale 2007; Kornuta et al. 2018; Jones et al. 2019; Pelech, Roesler, and Saydam 2019; Bennett and Dempster 2019), not Shishko, Stoica, and Mrozinski's own findings.
- The production-rate model's baseline design vector (slide 7) computes annual water production of about 1.64E+06 kg/year, meeting the modeled requirement of 1.60E+06 kg/year, with 156 annual tent moves at 0 hours/month downtime.
- Downtime sensitivity (slide 8): raising monthly tent downtime from 0 to 90 hours/month lowers tent availability from 100.00 percent to 87.68 percent and lowers annual tent moves from 156 to 137.
- The power/illumination sub-model (slide 9) brings the full design vector to 22 input variables (excluding physical constants) and computes an average delivered thermal power of 1.607 kW at the tent from two TransFormer reflectors 10,000 m away.
- The average-solar-illumination fit for two TransFormer reflectors near Shackleton crater (slide 10, credited to Henrickson and Stoica 2017) follows a logistic curve in reflector height H: L = exp(2.26844 + 0.00504*H) / [1 + exp(2.26844 + 0.00504*H)], rising from about 0.925 at 50 m height to about 0.989 at 450 m.
- The single worked cost example, the Cold Trap/Ice Hauler (slide 13), totals $370.75M in development cost and $55.61M in production cost (FY19 dollars), computed from a printed cost-estimating relationship C = 1.1847 * exp(-0.26 + 0.585*ln(M) + 2.6*Percent New Design + 0.231*Location) and Quickcost v.6.0 (March 2016) for a 1.05 mt dry mass, 50 percent new-design asset.
- The deck's own Profit and Loss and NPV modules (slide 15), run with the Target set to Lunar and no sales revenue entered, return Net Present Value 0.00 and Project Internal Rate of Return #NUM! at a 12.5 percent discount rate; only depreciation and amortization are populated, driving all profit lines negative every year.
- The recapitulation (slide 16) states the Cold Trap/Ice Hauler dry mass needed to be about 15 to 30 percent larger than previously published values to remain internally consistent within the model, and that current spacecraft cost models may not be appropriate for lunar surface systems.
- Forward work items (slide 17) include establishing the relationship between regolith/ice thermodynamics and tent pressure build-up, needing actual lunar demonstrations informed by laboratory simulation, and needing "a new approach to estimating and scaling costs" since "$ per kg is just not sufficient for high confidence."
- General observations (slide 18) state there is no consensus on market demand, no consensus on lunar-mine capital expenditure, and no consensus even on the cost of Earth-to-cis-lunar propellant delivery, let alone lunar-surface delivery.

### Limitations

This is a 23-slide conference presentation, not a peer-reviewed paper; content is necessarily compressed to slide-level bullets, tables, and diagrams, several of which (the production-model "threads of calculation," the engineering database, the tangible-assets cost table, the depreciation table, and the profit-and-loss/NPV table) exist only as spreadsheet screenshots and required transcription from the slide images rather than the extracted text layer. The deck explicitly states its cost information is "of a budgetary and planning nature... for informational purposes only" and does not constitute a JPL/Caltech commitment (slide 1).

The deck's central financial result, an NPV of 0.00 and an undefined IRR, is an artifact of no sales revenue having been entered into the demonstration model (slide 15), not a computed finding about lunar thermal mining's profitability; the source does not present a solved business case with a positive or negative NPV for the concept. Only two of the ten listed tangible assets (Cold Trap/Ice Hauler and the Earth-based Mission Control Center) carry populated cost/depreciation figures in the worked example (slides 13 to 14); the other eight assets are listed with dry mass and new-design percentage but without development costs filled in, so the illustrative run is a partial, not a full ten-asset, build-up. The source itself flags as unresolved (slide 17) the physical relationship between delivered thermal power and tent pressure build-up, regolith thermal diffusivity at PSR conditions, and sublimation/vapor-transport/deposition rates and their dependence on ice grain size, porosity, and impurities.
