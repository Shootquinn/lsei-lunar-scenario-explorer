## Abstract

An empirical, cluster-analysis-based study of Generation MZ (Millennials and Generation Z)
respondents' interest in, motivations for, and stated willingness to pay for three forms of space
tourism (spaceflight, terrestrial site-specific tourism, virtual tourism), alongside their
sustainability perceptions of each form. Based on 2,027 survey responses collected from young
adults in North-West Italy in January 2020, a hierarchical cluster analysis identifies seven
segments differing in interest, budget, willingness to pay, and views on which form of space
tourism is most environmentally, economically, and socially sustainable.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper frames commercial space tourism as moving from a "distant dream" toward practical
reality, noting the 2020 first private crewed launch and citing existing price points: Dennis
Tito's reported 20 million USD flight as the first space tourist, a NASA-advertised 35,000 USD
per astronaut for a single ISS night, and a Virgin Galactic SpaceShipTwo suborbital seat price
of 200,000 USD in the Introduction (a figure the paper's own Table 1 later states, without
reconciling the difference, as approximately 250,000 USD, with more than 600 individuals
reported to have paid a deposit for a seat). The authors note the literature on demand, attitudes,
and motivations for space tourism is comparatively scarce and set two aims: (1) empirically
examine Generation MZ's interest in space tourism and willingness to pay for it, and (2)
investigate their perception of associated environmental issues. Space tourism is defined
(citing Von der Dunk, 2011) as human space travel for recreational purposes and is described as
falling into three categories: spaceflight (atmospheric, suborbital, orbital, and future
lunar/Mars trips), terrestrial site-specific tourism (planetariums, observatories,
space-themed attractions), and virtual/cyber space tourism (devices and virtual reality
experiences). A literature table (Table 2, p.33) summarizes prior demand studies the authors use
to motivate their own work, including Futron Corporation (2002, 2006: 80% of an affluent US
sample interested in space travel, 18% willing to undertake orbital flight, forecasts of 15,000
then revised to 10,000 annual suborbital travelers by 2021), Webber and Reifert (2006: "fair
price" for suborbital set at 50,000 USD or below and for orbital at 1 million USD or below among
an adventure-traveler sample), Crouch et al. (2009: high sensitivity of potential travelers to
price among high-income Australians), Reddy et al. (2012: 54% willing to experience space travel
among a UK sample), the European Commission's 2014 Eurobarometer, Chang (2017), Laing and Frost
(2019), and Toivonen (2020). The paper also references Crouch's (2001) review of 1990s demand
studies, noting that Japanese studies from that period found respondents willing to spend "one
year's pay or more" on space tourism, though the authors characterize the era's demand estimates
overall as overoptimistic relative to how the market actually developed.

### Methods and scope

The study is an empirical, quantitative survey conducted among Generation MZ (defined as those
born after the 1980s, split here into Millennials aged 25-40 and Generation Z aged 18-24) in
North-West Italy, an area chosen for its concentration of aerospace-sector companies. Instrument
development proceeded in three stages: an initial six-person focus group (19-27 years old, three
male and three female) to shape the first questionnaire draft and confirm that all three forms of
space tourism should be included; a second seven-person review group (27-41 years old, four male
and three female, three currently working in the aerospace sector and four with aerospace
backgrounds) that refined question wording and structure; and a 20-respondent pre-test among young
people of varied gender, education, and socio-economic background that caught wording errors and
prompted additional response options (for example, seeing Earth from space, health hazards). The
final questionnaire had three closed-ended parts, each using 7-point Likert scales: (1)
demographic and social characteristics (gender, age, municipality, education, university
affiliation, average tourism spending, and socio-economic/environmental attitudes); (2) perceived
opportunity, interest, and motivations regarding space tourism; and (3) perceived environmental,
social, and economic sustainability of the three space tourism forms. The questionnaire was
distributed in January 2020 to the Generation MZ contacts involved in its development, who then
forwarded the survey link to others of the same generation (a snowball approach); 2,027 responses
were collected by month end. The paper's own abstract states a cluster analysis of "2,207
respondents," a figure that does not match the Methods section's stated sample of 2,027, the
Section 3.2 breakdown (51.01% female, 48.09% male; 81.20% aged 18-24, 18.80% aged 25-40), or the
seven cluster sizes in Table 4, which sum to 2,027. This appears to be a transposition typo in the
abstract as printed; this summary uses the Methods/Table-4-consistent figure of 2,027 throughout
and flags the discrepancy rather than resolving it. Analytically, raw Likert-scale items were
first reduced to synthetic qualitative variables via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and
Hierarchical Clustering Analysis (HCA), retaining components explaining at least 75% of variance;
these covered reasons for interest (four motivation groups), perceived sustainability (nine
variables across environmental, economic, and social dimensions and three tourism forms),
personal values (three groups), and travel-choice motivation (three groups). These variables, plus
spaceflight-specific interest, sustainability perception, and willingness-to-pay bands, were then
entered into a Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA), followed by a Hierarchical Cluster on
Principal Components (HCPC) procedure, producing a dendrogram cut into seven clusters (Figure 1,
p.36). All multivariate analysis was conducted in R using the FactoMineR package.

### Key findings

Table 3 (p.37) reports the full sample's distribution across the derived variables. On stated
willingness to pay for a spaceflight specifically: 19.80% (402 of 2,027) would not pay anything;
3.20% (65) would pay up to 500 euro; 14.80% (301) would pay 500-1,500 euro; 30.20% (612) would pay
1,500-5,000 euro; 18.90% (382) would pay 5,000-10,000 euro; and 13.10% (265) would pay more than
10,000 euro. On space-related motivation categories: 48.89% (991) were classified as interested in
all space experiences ("Chosen"), 31.28% (634) as interested but driven by peripheral factors such
as trip risk or operator nationality ("Driven"), 12.53% (254) as uninterested due to fear, and
7.30% (148) as uninterested because they judge space tourism unsustainable. Overall, the authors
report that more than 80% of respondents were interested in space tourism in some form (the five
interested clusters together total 1,625 of 2,027, about 80.2 percent, derived here from Table 4).
The seven hierarchical clusters (Table 4, p.38-39), with willingness-to-pay and typical vacation
budget bands as stated in the text: "Economic sustainable flight" (65 respondents, 3.21%), very
interested in all space experiences but unwilling to pay more than 500 euro, consistent with their
own reported vacation spending of no more than 500 euro; sees spaceflight as most environmentally
and economically sustainable; characterized by fear of the unknown and welfare orientation.
"Social sustainable flight" (343 respondents, 16.92%), interested in spaceflight for reasons tied
to trip logistics (risk, operator nationality, duration, aircraft type) rather than the experience
itself, willing to pay more than 10,000 euro despite typical vacation spending no higher than
1,500 euro, viewing flight as most socially sustainable, and mainly male. "Environmental
sustainable flight" (301 respondents, 14.85%), very interested in the experience itself, willing
to pay 500-1,500 euro against a typical vacation budget below 500 euro, viewing flight as most
environmentally sustainable, and mainly female. "Economic virtual experience" (382 respondents,
18.85%), interested in spaceflight for reasons beyond the direct experience, willing to spend up
to 10,000 euro against a usual vacation budget of 1,500 euro or more, viewing virtual space
tourism as most economically sustainable, and mainly male Generation Z. "Neutral" (534
respondents, 26.34%), interested for varied reasons including both the experience and logistical
factors, willing to pay 1,500-5,000 euro against a vacation budget under 1,500 euro, mainly
Generation Z, with no distinctive sustainability preference. "Fear" (254 respondents, 12.53%), not
interested in spaceflight primarily due to fear, willingness to pay near zero, viewing virtual
tourism as most environmentally sustainable, mainly female Millennials. "Unsustainable" (148
respondents, 7.30%), not interested because space tourism is judged unsustainable across
dimensions despite valuing technological innovation generally, not willing to pay for a
spaceflight, viewing virtual tourism as sustainable on economic, environmental, and social
grounds, mainly female Millennials. The two uninterested clusters ("Fear" and "Unsustainable")
are both described as predominantly female and Millennial, which the authors relate to Reddy et
al.'s (2012) prior finding that women reported less interest in space travel than men. The
Discussion adds an alternate cut of willingness to pay by degree of interest: among respondents
with an interest in space activities, 18% would spend no more than 1,500 euro, 37% would spend up
to 10,000 euro or more, and 26% with only a generic interest would spend no more than 5,000 euro;
the authors note stated budgets are still small relative to current spaceflight ticket prices but
are three to ten times (or more) larger than respondents' typical vacation spending. Separately
from the survey, Table 1 (p.32) catalogs then-current commercial vehicle offerings as reported in
industry sources: Virgin Galactic's suborbital rocket plane (flight above 80 km, six seats, about
90 minutes total flight time including about 4 minutes of microgravity, commercial service
announced for 2022, more than 600 people reported to have paid a deposit for a seat priced around
250,000 USD); Blue Origin's suborbital rocket and capsule (flight above 100 km, six passengers,
about 10 minutes of suborbital flight and a few minutes of weightlessness, limited public pricing
information, a 28 million USD winning bid for the first crewed New Shepard suborbital seat on July
20, 2021); and SpaceX's orbital capsule (up to seven people to the International Space Station,
plans for the all-civilian Inspiration4 orbital mission in late September 2021, three to four days
duration).

### Limitations

The authors themselves state the principal limitation is geographic and demographic scope: the
sample was drawn only from North-West Italy and only from Generation MZ, and they call for
extending the study to other target groups and regions for comparison. The willingness-to-pay
figures throughout are stated, hypothetical intentions collected via a self-administered online
survey distributed through a snowball/forwarding method rather than a probability sample, and the
paper does not report a response rate or discuss possible self-selection into the survey. As
noted above, the abstract's stated sample size (2,207) conflicts with the Methods section, the
demographic breakdown, and the summed cluster sizes (all consistent at 2,027); this appears to be
a typographical error in the source and is flagged rather than resolved. The paper is oriented
throughout toward attitudes, motivations, and sustainability perceptions rather than toward
market sizing: it reports no revenue forecast, no addressable-market estimate, and no extrapolation
of its Italian Generation MZ sample to a national or global population of potential travelers. Its
willingness-to-pay bands are stated in euro ranges for a "spaceflight" experience without
distinguishing suborbital from orbital pricing within the survey instrument itself (that
distinction appears only in the literature-review Table 2 and the vehicle-catalog Table 1, both
drawn from prior studies and industry reporting rather than from this paper's own respondents).
The Introduction's 200,000 USD Virgin Galactic seat price and Table 1's approximately 250,000 USD
figure are both printed in the source without being reconciled with each other.

### Topic mapping

FA7. Source list entry 10. Sub-Q 1. This paper is an attitudinal and motivational survey study,
not a hard market-sizing or revenue-forecasting exercise: it reports a cluster analysis of 2,027
young Italian respondents' self-reported interest in, and willingness to pay for, spaceflight,
terrestrial, and virtual space tourism, together with their sustainability perceptions of each
form, but it does not model an addressable market size, a national or global traveler count, or a
revenue projection. Its usable content for FA7 is price-adjacent rather than a sizing input: stated
willingness-to-pay bands for a spaceflight experience among this sample (19.80% would pay nothing,
rising through bands to 13.10% willing to pay more than 10,000 euro), cluster-level combinations of
interest, budget, and sustainability perception, and a secondary catalog (Table 1) of then-current
industry list prices (Virgin Galactic around 250,000 USD per suborbital seat with more than 600
deposits reported, Blue Origin's 28 million USD auctioned seat, SpaceX's orbital Inspiration4
flight). Because the surveyed population and prices are specific to one Italian generational
cohort's stated intentions rather than derived from ticket sales, bookings, or a formal demand
model, this source supports FA7's characterization of space tourism as a real but narrow,
hard-to-size, prestige/experience demand rather than a source of trillion-scale, durable external
demand figures.
