## Abstract

First in a planned series extending the author's 1972/1974 lunar-industrialization argument. It lays out a "selenosphere system strategy" — a framework for gradually building lunar industrial capability organized around lunar surface industrial zones for extraction and processing, integrated with a circumlunar zero-g processing capability, serving markets in geolunar space. Central claims: lunar oxygen is the most promising initial economic "drawing card"; controlled fusion is the key enabling energy source and is easier to engineer in space; and lunar cycle (materials) management is a fundamental requirement for economic viability. The paper also treats legal/treaty questions and the socio-psychological case for lunar (versus orbital) settlement. Method is conceptual analysis with illustrative engineering estimates; transportation detail is deferred to later papers.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper continues the "open-world" geolunar industrial argument Ehricke first made in 1972, now reframed explicitly in socio-economic terms. Its purpose is to define an approach for the gradual, flexible evolution of lunar industrial capability, driven first by the economic needs of a growing Earth-serving exo-industry and later by "sociospheric" (settlement) incentives. The premise is that productive industrial — and eventually urban — development at the lunar *surface* is the preferred path for complex extraterrestrial advance, both technologically and socially. This is the first of several papers; transportation alternatives, buildup scenarios, and detailed extraction/manufacturing are explicitly deferred to sequels.

### Methods and scope

Conceptual/strategic analysis supported by figures, classification tables, and order-of-magnitude estimates. The paper organizes the problem as a "selenosphere systems strategy" with distinct sides: **INVESTMENT** (habitation, exoponics/food, transportation, industrial plants), **OPERATING COST** (logistics, personnel-vs-automation trade-off, rent, tax structure, investment time frame), **CONTROLS** (legal/treaty constraints, vacuum maintenance, product quality), **PROBLEMS** (ownership/management, disaster/rescue, dispersed metallogenic provinces), **COMMERCIAL INCENTIVES/MARKETING**, **INDUSTRIAL INCENTIVES**, and **SOCIOSPHERIC INCENTIVES**. Lunar-sample data are those from the Lunar Science Conferences (Apollo 11–15). Legal analysis draws on U.N. Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space working papers (U.S. 1972, U.S.S.R. 1971, Bulgaria 1973) and the 1967 Outer Space Treaty.

### Key findings

The contributions are framework definitions, classifications, and quantified illustrations:

- **Three foundations for lunar development.** (1) Safe/adequate living conditions (habitation plus food, including growing food in fertilized lunar ground — "exoponics"); (2) economic transportation between lunar surface and circumlunar orbit (CLO), and between CLO and circumterrestrial orbits; (3) an initial economic "drawing card."

- **Lunar oxygen as the initial "drawing card."** Oxygen is highlighted because it needs no further processing once extracted and has high transport value in geolunar space. Illustrative logistics: delivering 10,000 metric tons of material to geosynchronous orbit (GSO) using O₂/H₂ orbital transfer vehicles requires ~29,000 tons of liquid oxygen (plus ~6,000 tons H₂) at near-Earth orbit; supplying that LOX from Earth costs ~11–15 tons of LOX/H₂ burned per ton delivered to NEO, a difference of ~370,000 tons of propellant per 10,000 tons of GSO cargo. Lunar LOX (LLOX) is therefore an attractive substitute. Chemical O₂/H₂ transport is argued to require far fewer ships than electric propulsion for equal NEO–GSO transfer capacity per unit time (an electric OTV would need 30–50× the capacity to break even), though electric propulsion is more favorable on the lower-gravity GSO–CLO leg.

- **Controlled fusion as the top-ranked energy source.** Fusion (steady or pulsed) is argued to be the key to lunar industrial economics and diversity: it yields the highest processing temperatures, is compact/light per unit energy, and consumes very little mass (~1 gram per 27,000 kWh). Crucially, space is presented as a *more favorable* environment for fusion — a magnetically confined plasma needs ~10⁻⁶ torr surrounding vacuum, while lunar vacuum is 10⁻⁷ torr or better, allowing larger reactor chambers and lower plasma/magnetic pressures. Large-scale lunar resource extraction (even of oxygen) is estimated to be ~20 years away, so early priority on plasma/steady-state fusion R&D is urged.

- **"Demandite" and lunar abundance.** Adopting Goeller and Weinberg's (1976) "demandite" (a hypothetical material reflecting total US non-renewable-resource consumption), the paper tabulates demandite composition against terrestrial supply periods and a lunar abundance index. A general correlation is noted: where terrestrial reserves of a material are limited, its lunar abundance is usually low too. A "lunar demandite" must instead reflect operational demands of the lunar environment and a market in which lunar resources replace only part of the richer terrestrial spectrum, adding value where lunar conditions (vacuum, low gravity) give an advantage. Table 2 classifies lunar elements by relative potential utility (with neutron/proton/liquid definitions and Apollo-site occurrence).

- **Lunar industrial zones (LIZ) and ballistic collection.** Metallogenic provinces appear widely dispersed; rather than many single-product settlements (uneconomic due to duplication and market sensitivity), the paper favors ballistic (electromagnetically accelerated) transport of "lunar crude" to central extraction facilities. Illustrative near-side, near-equatorial zones: a Tranquillity LIZ (titanium-rich) within a ~300 km radius, and a Procellarum LIZ (~300 and ~600 km) rich in Na, K, Mn, Cr, P.

- **Cycle management and hydrogen cycle.** Economic viability is argued to depend on "cycle management" — closed materials loops. Detailed examples include a hydrogen cycle (H₂ reduces oxidized minerals, producing water that is electrolyzed to recover H₂ and O₂), plus interlocking O₂, carbon, and magnesium sub-cycles (e.g., Mg reducing TiO₂ and BeO, Si and Mg as reducing agents, Na₂O₂ absorbing CO₂ to release oxygen for life support). Carbon and hydrogen are noted as elements that must initially be imported from Earth.

- **Legal / treaty analysis.** The paper reviews the unresolved Moon-treaty debate (differing interpretations of Articles I and II of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty). It critiques the "common heritage of mankind" concept, drawing an analogy to the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas, and argues that celestial bodies are humanity's "province" (rightful field of activity) but should not be declared terrestrial *property*. It cites the author's own 1957 "three fundamental laws of astronautics." Environmental-contamination provisions in the draft treaties are noted, with a caution that lunar and terrestrial environments differ profoundly and the Moon has a far lower industrial sensitivity threshold.

- **Sociospheric case for the Moon over orbital colonies.** The Moon is argued to be a superior, more "forgiving" proving ground than orbital space for studying the technological, biological, cultural, social, and psychological matrix required before large off-Earth settlements are viable — because it offers stepwise growth ("a colony in the raw... supplied free of charge"), an open landscape reducing the psychological burden of enclosure, low-gravity construction advantages, and "amphibious" movement between 0.16 g surface and orbital weightlessness.

### Limitations

By design the paper is the first installment and repeatedly defers substance: transportation alternatives, buildup scenarios, low-grade-ore plasma exploitation, and detailed manufacturing technologies are all promised in later papers. Quantitative claims are illustrative order-of-magnitude estimates, not derived from new data. Key economic premises depend on future, then-nonexistent technology — economical controlled/pulsed fusion, ballistic electromagnetic lunar transport, and low-cost chemical transport that "almost eliminates" the lunar gravity well. The "lunar demandite" is acknowledged as only a small step, since real relative consumption rates over long periods are unknown. Legal conclusions are the author's advocacy positions within an unsettled treaty debate. Sample-based abundance claims rest on the limited Apollo 11–15 dataset, and the author notes the Apollo sites may not be the most suitable locations, requiring much further prospecting.
