# The Space Economy in Figures: Responding to Global Challenges

## Citation

OECD. (2023). *The space economy in figures: Responding to global challenges*. OECD
Publishing, Paris. https://doi.org/10.1787/fa5494aa-en

Publisher URL: none in source

## Abstract

Second edition (following a 2019 first edition) of the OECD Space Forum's flagship statistical
report on the global space sector, produced by the Directorate for Science, Technology and
Innovation. Compiles indicators on government space budgets, launch activity, satellite
populations, private investment, patents, scientific output, and space-related official
development assistance (ODA), and assesses the sector's contribution to climate, connectivity
and other global challenges. Four thematic chapters cover innovation/funding trends, earth
observation and data applications, competition for orbital resources, and sustainability
threats (debris, workforce, environmental footprint), followed by structured country profiles
for the ten members of the OECD Space Forum. Draws on OECD-compiled administrative data,
patent and bibliometric databases, national space agency/industry surveys, and third-party
satellite and launch registries; text-only synthesis, largely non-experimental.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The report updates the OECD's 2019 *Space Economy in Figures* against what the Foreword
describes as a substantially changed context: the COVID-19 pandemic, Russia's invasion of
Ukraine and the associated energy crisis, layered onto pre-existing global challenges (climate
change, biodiversity loss, pollution, unequal internet access). It was drafted by Marit
Undseth and Claire Jolly of the OECD Space Forum, with contributions from junior researchers
on Chapter 4 and the country profiles, and reviewed by steering-group members from the
national space agencies of Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Korea, the Netherlands, Norway,
Switzerland, the United Kingdom, the United States, and the European Space Agency. The stated
objective is to document how the space sector supports responses to global challenges through
four lenses: (1) overarching innovation and funding trends and recent-crisis resilience; (2)
space as a data provider, particularly earth observation; (3) growing competition for orbital
access and resources; and (4) vulnerabilities that threaten the sector's own sustainability,
plus a statistical guide to the space programmes of the OECD Space Forum's ten member
countries. A "Revised version" of the PDF was issued in September 2024, per the copyright
page, though the substantive content used here reflects the report as published in 2023.

### Methods and scope

The report is a synthesis of secondary and administrative data rather than an original
empirical study. Sources include: OECD calculations from national government budget
submissions (institutional space budgets, 2015-22, deflated to constant currencies and
converted to USD); the Union of Concerned Scientists (UCS) Satellite Database (1 January 2023
version) and US Space Force space-track.org for satellite and debris counts; the Committee on
Earth Observation Satellites (CEOS) database for earth observation missions; the OECD STI
Micro-data Lab Intellectual Property Database for space-related patent applications (IP5
patent families, by priority date and applicant location, 2006-10 versus 2016-20); Elsevier's
Scopus Custom Data for bibliometric indicators across four space-related journal categories
(aerospace engineering, astronomy, atmospheric science, space and planetary science); the
OECD Development Assistance Committee's Creditor Reporting System (CRS) database, from which
more than 2,200 space-related ODA projects were manually identified over 2000/2002-2021; and
national space industry/workforce surveys for Canada, Korea, the United Kingdom, France,
Germany, Italy and Japan. Definitions of "upstream," "downstream" and "space-derived"
segments follow the OECD Handbook on Measuring the Space Economy, 2nd edition (2022). The
ten country profiles (Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Korea, the Netherlands, Norway,
Switzerland, the United Kingdom, the United States) use a common template: fast facts,
budget trends and main programmes, top patent applicants, ODA commitments, and scientific
output/excellence indicators, each keyed mainly to 2021-22 data.

### Key findings

**Global framing (Executive summary).** As of the report's baseline, 32% of the world's
population does not use the internet, with high-speed fixed broadband unavailable in remote
and sparsely populated regions, including in some OECD countries. Space-based systems already
support more than half of the most frequently designated critical infrastructures in OECD
countries (transport, energy, food supply, law enforcement) and provide more than half of the
essential climate variables used to monitor climate change. In 2022, newly launched satellites
detected more than 1,000 human-induced methane super-emitter events. Space-related ODA
totalled more than 700 million constant USD over 2000-21, with commitments rising recently.
OECD government space budgets reached an estimated USD 75 billion in 2022, or 0.1% of OECD
GDP (a conservative estimate including civilian and military spending where available). Almost
100 countries have placed a satellite in orbit since 1957; 23 countries are pursuing national
launcher projects and 11 are developing spaceports. There were about 6,700 operational
satellites in orbit by end-2022, twice the 2020 total, with more than two-thirds run by
commercial operators. Satellite broadband remained a small share of connectivity (0.2 fixed
broadband subscriptions per 100 inhabitants in the OECD area). About 25,000 debris objects are
identifiable and tracked, against an untracked population estimated in the hundreds of
millions. Women's share of the space-industry workforce was reported at 29% in Canada, 15% in
Korea and 24% in the United Kingdom.

**Chapter 1 — Space technologies coming of age.** Space technologies support more than half of
the 16 most frequently designated critical-infrastructure sectors across 32 OECD countries
surveyed (Table 1.1); Belgium, France, Spain and the United Kingdom designate the space sector
itself as critical. Annual launch activity, essentially flat at 80-110 objects per year around
2000, roughly doubled in 2013 and has exceeded 1,000 objects per year since 2019. Of active
satellites, 78.6% were commercially operated, 10.2% by non-military government and 8.8% by
military actors (active satellites represent only about 25% of unclassified objects tracked by
the US Space Force). Estimates of the global space economy, excluding government procurement,
range from about USD 200 billion to USD 350 billion depending on definition and method, with
the upper range including GNSS-enabled location-based service revenues. Historical growth has
been modest and uneven: BryceTech data for the US Satellite Industry Association show
2008-21 compound annual growth of 7% for PNT ground equipment (the strongest segment) against
0.8% for space manufacturing, 1.3% for satellite services and 1.5% for launch; the US Bureau of
Economic Analysis's Space Economy Satellite Account shows 1.6% average annual growth for
2012-19, below overall US GDP growth. The report explicitly judges a previously projected "USD
1 trillion economy by 2040" (cited to investment-bank forecasts) as not supported by this
historical record. Government space budgets averaged 0.10% of OECD GDP in 2022, down from
0.12% in 2008; in the United States, FY2023 congressional allocations gave the three-year-old
Space Force USD 26.3 billion against USD 25.4 billion for NASA, and ESA's science programme's
share of its multi-annual budget fell from 24% (2002-06) to 19% (2023-27). Private space
investment reached an all-time high of about USD 15.4 billion globally in 2021 (BryceTech)
before dropping sharply in 2022; the US National Venture Capital Association reported the
funding supply/demand ratio flipping from 1.5-to-1 (supply exceeding demand) in early 2022 to
2-to-1 (demand exceeding supply) by year end. Emergent "in-orbit economy" activities remain
small: asteroid-mining start-ups attracted more than USD 50 million in the 2010s before that
niche's investment collapsed in 2019.

**Chapter 2 — Space as a provider of critical data and applications.** More than 1,000
operational earth observation and earth-science satellites were in orbit by late 2022 (mostly
low-earth orbit), split by operator type as 589 commercial, 320 civilian government, 242
military/dual-military, 30 "other" and 12 public-private partnership missions (Figure 2.1).
More than half of earth observation satellites are now commercially operated (574 satellites),
two-thirds of them US-operated and mostly nanosatellites, led by US firms Planet and Spire.
China accounted for about 36% of civilian government-led earth observation missions in 2022.
CEOS records about 180 operational civilian environment/climate missions with another 158 in
planning; multilateral missions collect atmospheric data most often (97% of observations),
followed by land (76%), ocean (26%), snow/ice (26%) and gravity/magnetic fields (12%). NASA's
Earth System Observatory is a USD 2.5 billion, five-mission programme; the European Commission
renewed Copernicus funding at EUR 5.6 billion. Weather satellite coordination (WMO/WIGOS)
covered 19 geostationary and 15 low-earth-orbit satellites in early 2023, feeding more than 15
gigabytes of data daily to operational weather centres; a cited data-denial study found that
withholding satellite observations degrades day-5 forecast skill by about two days in the
southern hemisphere versus 0.5 days in the northern hemisphere. The International Charter for
Space and Major Disasters, launched in 2000, had been activated more than 750 times for 130
countries using 270 satellites, with more than 20 supporting organisations by 2023. On
greenhouse gases, 16 dedicated tracking missions were in orbit in 2022 with another 16 in
development; two commercial missions (Canada's GHGSat, the US Orbital Sidekick's Aurora) were
operating, with four more in development. In 2022, satellites detected more than 1,000
human-caused methane super-emitter events, over half from oil and gas fields, 105 from coal
mines and 340 from waste sites such as landfills. CEOS reports 61% of active-mission and 57%
of decommissioned-mission earth observation data as open access. Cited economic-value studies
include: satellite-derived US air-pollution monitoring estimated to save roughly 2,700 lives
annually; EU poor-air-quality warnings estimated to avoid EUR 8.3-21 million in hospital costs
by 2035; Copernicus ex ante benefits estimated at EUR 4.3 billion annually by 2025 rising to
EUR 8.3 billion by 2035 (low estimate); combined lifetime benefits of ESA's Aeolus-1/Aeolus-2
wind missions estimated to exceed EUR 10 billion; UK earth observation value estimated at GBP
966 million annually (2020), about 90% from meteorological applications; 20-year European
socio-economic benefits of satellite meteorology estimated at EUR 16-61 billion; US Landsat
programme valued via willingness-to-pay at USD 3.45 billion; and China's Public Weather
Service valued at CNY 46.5 billion (0.22% of GDP) in 2006. Broader open-government-data
research cited by the OECD put the aggregate economic impact of public sector information at
roughly 1.1% of cumulated GDP in 2008; a Canadian study found open geospatial data added CAD
695 million to GDP. Copernicus recorded 185.7 million user downloads and 80 pebibytes of data
in 2021, with commercial users accounting for 67% of downloads. A Danish farm survey found
precision-agriculture technology used on 37% of farms (77% of agricultural area) versus
satellite/drone imagery used on just 8% of farms (26% of area), though rising since 2018.

**Chapter 3 — Managing a growing space economy.** Almost 100 countries had operated a
satellite by late 2023, including 12 new lower-middle-income and two new low-income countries
since 2012. A "firsts in lunar exploration" table (Table 3.1) traces successive national
milestones: the United States and USSR/Russia in the 1960s-70s; Japan's 1990 orbiter (the
first success by a third country); Europe (2003), China (2007 orbiter, 2013 lander, 2020
robotic sample return) and India (2008 orbiter, 2023 first landing near the lunar south pole);
Luxembourg's 2014 flyby (via a Chinese spacecraft); Israel's unsuccessful 2019 attempt (the
first privately funded lunar lander mission); and 2022 landings/attempts by newcomers Italy,
Korea and the United Arab Emirates. International co-authorship in space/planetary science
publications reached 56% in the OECD area in 2021 (67% for the European Union) and grew from
21% to 30% (2007-21) among BRIICS economies. Space-related patent applications filed at the
USPTO reached about 600 in 2019, following a sustained increase over the prior 10-12 years;
the "cosmonautics" (spacecraft/launch technology) share of applications grew from 19-26%
(2006-10) to 38-42% (2016-20), while satellite-navigation's share fell from 33% to 12.6% over
the same comparison. In the 2016-20 period, US regions held 9 of the top 20 patent-filing
regions worldwide, China held 4, and France and Germany together held 4 (down from 8 in
2006-10). Launch activity in 2022 set records: the United States carried out 76 successful
launches (61 by SpaceX, 80% of the US total), China 62 (up from a prior record of 48 in 2021),
and Russia 21; New Zealand carried out 9, Europe 6 (with one Vega-C failure), India 5, and
Iran, Japan and Korea one each (Korea's Nuri reached orbit on its second attempt after a 2021
failure). Ariane 5 flew its final mission in July 2023 after 27 years; Ariane 6's inaugural
flight was expected in 2024. Between 2018 and mid-2023, 11 economies demonstrated orbital
launch capability — six with domestic-only launches (Iran, Israel, North Korea, Korea, Japan,
Russia) and five with both domestic and international customers (China, India, Europe via
French Guiana, New Zealand, the United States); the United States alone had 13 spaceports/
launch sites across nine states, including three for exclusive SpaceX/Blue Origin use. About
19 dedicated small-satellite launchers had flown at least once by 2023, with more than 60
further projects in development through 2030 (19 of them in Western Europe: France 5, Germany
4, Italy 1, Norway 1, Spain 2, United Kingdom 5). Medium/heavy and super heavy-lift launcher
capacities are tabulated (e.g., Falcon Heavy 38,000-45,000kg, Vulcan Centaur 25,000kg, Ariane 6
21,650kg, Long March 5/5B 25,000kg; NASA's SLS 95,000-130,000kg by block, SpaceX Starship a
targeted 150,000kg). By end-2022, the United States held 67.4% of the 6,700 operational
satellites (4,529), followed by China (8.8%, 592), the United Kingdom (8.4%, 563), Russia
(2.6%, 177), Japan (1.3%, 90), ESA (1.0%, 64) and India (0.9%, 62); upper-middle-income
economies (mainly China and Russia) held 13% and low/lower-middle-income economies just 1.9%
(100 satellites). SpaceX and OneWeb together accounted for 74% of all operational commercial
satellites at end-2022. On spectrum/orbit governance, the 2019 World Radiocommunication
Conference introduced deployment milestones for constellations (10% of authorised satellites
within two years, 50% within five, 100% within seven) to curb "warehousing," and geostationary
slot spacing was earlier reduced from three to two degrees of longitude, raising available
slots from about 120 to 180. On space resources, by 2022 the United States, Luxembourg, the
United Arab Emirates and Japan had each enacted national laws on resources extracted from
celestial bodies; in November 2022 Japan granted ispace its first commercial licence under the
country's Space Resources Act, permitting the sale of lunar regolith to NASA via an "in-place"
ownership transfer. The Artemis Accords, launched in October 2020 and grounded in the 1967
Outer Space Treaty, had 29 signatories by October 2023 (not including China or Russia); the
1979 Moon Agreement, by contrast, counts India as the only major spacefaring country to have
signed without ratifying it.

**Chapter 4 — Growth and sustainability of the space economy under threat.** Upstream space
manufacturing and launch remain heavily government-dependent: public-sector sales/grants
accounted for 40% of upstream revenue in Canada (2021), 69% of private-sector domestic
upstream revenue in Korea, about 70% of the upstream segment in Europe, and 67% of domestic
revenue in Japan (mainly upstream); 16% of US commercial respondents to a 2014 industrial-base
survey called themselves "dependent" on US government programmes. Externally funded R&D
accounted for 24% of business R&D spending in Canada (2021) and 51% in the United Kingdom
(2020). SMEs form the bulk of firms (94% of commercial actors in Canada, 92% in Korea) but the
top 10 manufacturers captured 85% of European industry revenue (2020) and 84% of Canadian
revenue (2019). Reported downstream-segment employment shares of the total space workforce
were 47% in Canada (2019), roughly 50-53% in Korea (2021) and 67% in the United Kingdom
(2020); Europe's earth observation workforce was estimated at about 24,000 persons (2020).
Education levels are high (77% of UK space employees held a bachelor's degree or higher in
2020; 67% in Canada in 2021; 67% of Europe's upstream workforce had 3+ years of university
education). Skills shortages are widespread: an Australian gap analysis found shortages in all
but 9 of 319 identified space-industry skills, with 86 flagged as requiring particular
attention. Workforce ageing is uneven across countries: 36% of the US space workforce was 50
or older in a 2013 survey, and Europe's 2021 workforce skewed to the 49-58 age bracket, while
Korea's 50-plus share was under 11% in 2019. On gender, women's share of total staff at
selected national space organisations (2019-23 data) ranged from 46% (South Africa's SANSA)
and 45% (Canada's CSA) down to 20% (Japan's JAXA, India's ISRO) and 14-16% (Korea's KARI and
related institutes), with shares roughly halving again when restricted to non-administrative,
non-clerical (science/engineering) roles. Women held about 23% of Europe's upstream-segment
employment in 2021 (stable over the decade) and 34% of US aerospace manufacturing employment
in 2023 (up from 19.5% in 2017); the share of women among US aerospace-engineering graduate
students rose from 14% (2010) to about 19% (2021). A 2020 UK Space Census found only 47% of
female respondents (versus 79% of male respondents) felt "always" welcome in the sector, and
38% of female (44% of male) respondents of colour reported the same. On infrastructure
threats: a 2006 solar radio burst disrupted satellite-ground communications and GPS signals
for about ten minutes; a 2022 coronal mass ejection deorbited 40 of 49 newly launched Starlink
satellites; the 1989 geomagnetic storm that struck Quebec left six million people without
power for nine hours; forecast accuracy for coronal mass ejections is limited to 6-8 hours for
timing and 15-30 minutes for magnetic-field orientation, while solar flares and particle
ejections cannot be forecast at all given their light-speed travel. On debris, the US Space
Force tracked more than 25,000 debris objects (mostly 10cm or larger) as of March 2022against
an untracked population estimated in the hundreds of millions; rocket bodies make up about 10%
of tracked objects but roughly 40% of tracked mass. One cited study (Vittori et al., 2022)
estimates the monetary losses of a full-blown Kessler Syndrome cascade at approximately USD
191.3 billion. Orbital clearance compliance (25-year post-mission deorbit guideline) for
satellites above 650km remains low, though improving with mega-constellation operator
performance. On light and radio pollution: existing satellites and debris already raise
night-sky brightness by at least 10% at some sites, crossing the conventional "light polluted"
threshold; simulations for the USD 500 million Vera C. Rubin Observatory suggest 30-40% of
exposures during the first and last hours of the night could be compromised; and continued
interference from the Iridium satellite constellation has blocked a protected radio-astronomy
band (1610.6-1613.8 MHz, used to observe hydroxide molecules) for 22 years. On environmental
footprint, a cited lifecycle-assessment review (Maury et al., 2020) finds the launcher itself
accounts for 99% of a space mission's mass and 50-70% of its global-warming potential, and
that the launch event covers nearly 100% of a mission's ozone-depletion potential; modelled
scenarios to 2050 (Table 4.3) show ozone-depletion impact reaching up to roughly 1,900% of
current annual global anthropogenic impact under a high-growth scenario (versus under 6% under
a low-growth scenario), reflecting the small current baseline rather than an absolute
prediction; and one input-output estimate finds the combined carbon footprint of ground- and
space-based astronomical research comparable to a small European country's annual emissions
(subject to about 80% methodological uncertainty, per the authors). ESA's "Green Agenda"
targets a 46% cut in facility electricity/gas/fuel consumption by 2030 and a full shift to
renewable energy.

ODA commitments (2002-21), among other indicators. Canada: first satellite Alouette 1 (1962);

billion and 32,200-person workforce; third-largest global patent applicant at 12% share; USD

lunar orbiter reached the Moon in 2022, with a regional Korea Positioning System planned by the
2030s. The Netherlands: first satellite ANS (1974); 15 satellites; 2022 budget USD 163.1
million (EUR 155 million), 0.016% of GDP, declining 0.5%/year since 2015; 2018 revenue USD 966
million and 6,000-FTE workforce (40% at ESTEC); patent share roughly 1%, stable; USD 41.8
million ODA, mainly via the Geodata for Agriculture and Water (G4AW) programme. Norway: first
satellite Thor 1 (1992, acquired on-orbit); 9 satellites; spaceport opened 2023; 2022 budget
USD 177 million (NOK 1.7 billion), 0.031% of GDP, growing 3.5%/year since 2015; 2021 revenue

country donors over 2002-21. Switzerland: first satellite SwissCube-1 (2009); 15 satellites;
2022 budget USD 288 million (CHF 276 million), 0.036% of GDP, growing 1.5%/year since 2015;
1,500-person workforce; 0.4% patent share, a notable decline from 2006-10; drafting its first
national Space Act; a Swiss firm won ESA's first in-orbit debris-removal service contract
(launch planned 2026). United Kingdom: first satellite Ariel (1962); first orbital launch 1971

account); 36% global patent share (up 8 percentage points from 2006-10); USD 75 million ODA,
including the NASA/USAID SERVIR programme (since 2004) operating in more than 50 countries.

### Limitations

The report is a compilation and synthesis of third-party and administrative data rather than
original primary research, and it flags its own definitional and comparability problems
throughout: government space-budget totals are described as "conservative estimates" that do
not uniformly include classified or military spending; national industry-survey revenue and
employment figures are explicitly stated as "not always directly comparable" across countries
because of differing industry scope (e.g., inclusion of satellite television) and survey
coverage; and global space-economy size estimates (USD 200-350 billion) are presented as
sensitive to definitional choices. Several cited figures carry their own caveats flagged in
the source: environmental lifecycle and carbon-footprint estimates for the space sector are
described as subject to large uncertainty (as much as 80% in one cited input-output study) due
to sparse observational data on satellite/rocket composition; launch price comparisons are
undermined by non-disclosure of many (especially military) launch contracts and heavy vehicle
customisation; and the 2050 environmental-scenario projections (Table 4.3) are scenario-based
extrapolations, not forecasts. Author-observed inconsistencies include an apparent internal
discrepancy in France's reported institutional-budget share of GDP (0.097% in the summary
table versus "0.97%" in the accompanying narrative text). Bar-chart figures throughout the
report (e.g., per-country government budget shares in Figure 1.7, satellite-class breakdowns
in Figure 1.4) provide comparative visual detail — such as a country-by-country ranking of
2022 versus 2008 government space budgets as a share of GDP — beyond what is stated in
running text or captions; this summary relies on the printed prose, captions and tabulated
data and does not attempt to read exact plotted values off charts. Several key statistics
(e.g., orbital-launch price trends, satellite-class population counts) are presented only
graphically without corresponding text figures.

### Topic mapping

FA4, FA7. Source list entry 14. Sub-Q 2. The source addresses: definitions and measurement
frameworks for the global space economy (upstream, downstream, space-derived segments); trends in
government space budgets and public/private investment; satellite launch activity, launcher
capacity and spaceport development; the deployment and use of earth observation and other
satellite data for climate monitoring, disaster response, agriculture, and official development
assistance; the economic valuation of satellite data applications; competition for orbital slots,
radio spectrum and lunar/space resources, including relevant international legal instruments (the
1967 Outer Space Treaty, the 1979 Moon Agreement, the 2020 Artemis Accords); space-industry
workforce composition, skills shortages and gender representation; space debris, the Kessler
Syndrome, natural and human-made threats to space infrastructure (space weather, jamming,
cyberattacks), and the environmental footprint of launch and satellite operations (light
pollution, radio interference, atmospheric emissions); and structured national statistical
profiles for the space programmes of Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Korea, the Netherlands,
Norway, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and the United States.

## FIGURE-ONLY DATA NEEDED

None identified as strictly necessary. All headline statistics requested for this summary
(global space-economy revenue range, government budgets by country, launch/satellite counts,
private investment, climate/connectivity contributions, Chapter 3 competition/resource
material, Chapter 4 vulnerabilities, and country-profile figures) were available in running
text, figure captions, or tables. Two chart types carry additional comparative detail not
transcribed here because no headline figure depended on it: Figure 1.7 ("Selected government
space budget estimates," p.28), which plots a country-by-country ranking of government space
budgets as a share of GDP for 2022 versus 2008 across roughly 25 economies (OECD-average
figures for these two years are stated in text and used above, but individual-country bar
values beyond the ten profiled countries are chart-only); and Figure 1.4 ("Operational
satellites by satellite class," p.25), which plots satellite counts by mass class and orbit
without corresponding running-text totals.
