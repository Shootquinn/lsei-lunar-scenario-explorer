# Technology Assessment: Neutron Detectors, Alternatives to Using Helium-3

## Citation

U.S. Government Accountability Office. (2011). Technology assessment: Neutron detectors:
Alternatives to using helium-3 (GAO-11-753). Report to congressional requesters, dated September
29, 2011 (cover date September 2011). Center for Science, Technology, and Engineering, and
Natural Resources and Environment team. Signed by Timothy M. Persons, Chief Scientist and
Director, Center for Science, Technology, and Engineering, and Gene Aloise, Director, Natural
Resources and Environment. GAO product page: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-11-753

Publisher URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-11-753

## Abstract

A congressionally requested GAO technology assessment evaluating whether boron-10 and lithium-6
based neutron detector technologies can replace helium-3 in large-area science detectors and
homeland-security radiation portal monitors, prompted by a helium-3 supply shortage the U.S.
government discovered in 2008. Serves here strictly as the demand-side companion to the
helium-3 supply-side source (Olson 2021, Source list entry 5); read only for its market, supply,
demand, and price content, not its detector-physics comparisons.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The report states that helium-3 became a favored neutron-detection material beginning in the
1980s and was considered a gold standard conversion material for detecting neutrons. Today's
U.S. supply of helium-3 is described as coming entirely from the radioactive decay of tritium
held in the National Nuclear Security Administration's (NNSA) tritium stockpile, maintained to
support the nuclear weapons program; tritium decays into helium-3 at a stated annual rate of 5.5
percent. The document states the United States ceased tritium production in 1988. As NNSA
maintains the tritium stockpile, it removes accumulated helium-3 because the gas can diminish a
weapon's effectiveness; in the past this helium-3 was treated as a waste product and vented to
the atmosphere. From about 1980 through 1995, and again from 2003 through 2008, NNSA and
predecessor agencies instead supplied helium-3 to the Department of Energy's (DOE) Isotope
Program for sale; the report notes the Isotope Program did not sell helium-3 from about 1995
through 2001 because it was being stockpiled for a since-discontinued tritium production project,
during which time Russia was the primary source of commercially available helium-3.

The report states that in 2008 the U.S. government abruptly learned it faced a severe helium-3
shortage, caused by the combination of a declining tritium stockpile (and therefore declining
helium-3 byproduct) since the end of the Cold War, together with rising helium-3 demand over the
prior decade, driven primarily by increased use in neutron detectors for research and security
applications. The stated purpose of this report is narrower than the shortage itself: at
congressional request, GAO assessed (1) what alternative, non-helium-3 neutron detector
technologies are currently available and how effective they are, and (2) the status of
alternative detector technologies still under development for future availability. The report
does not attempt an economic or market analysis of helium-3 in its own right; it treats the
supply and demand figures as background context establishing why alternatives are needed. GAO
notes a companion report, cited in this document as GAO-11-472, "Managing Critical Isotopes:
Weaknesses in DOE's Management of Helium-3 Delayed the Federal Response to a Critical Supply
Shortage" (May 12, 2011), as the source for some supply and price details reported here.

### Methods and scope

GAO reviewed program documents from Commerce, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), the
Department of Defense (DOD), and DOE; test and evaluation reports from detector manufacturers and
from DOE's Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL);
and interviewed agency officials and detector vendors. With assistance from the National Academy
of Sciences, GAO assembled and consulted a group of eight outside experts in technology
development, nuclear physics and engineering, and neutron detector applications, who also
reviewed a draft of the report. Fieldwork ran from July 2010 to September 2011. The bulk of the
document (sections 2 through 4 and the appendices) is a technical and programmatic comparison of
three currently available alternative detector technologies, boron-10 lined proportional
detectors, boron trifluoride (BF3) gas proportional detectors, and lithium-6 scintillators, plus
more than 30 additional research and development programs, assessed against NASA's nine-point
technology readiness level (TRL) scale. That detector-physics and TRL-assessment material is
outside the scope of this summary. The supply, demand, and price figures relevant to helium-3 as
a market appear concentrated in the report's background section (2.3 and 2.4) and its two demand
charts (Figures 2.5 and 2.6), which the report attributes to "GAO analysis of information from
the interagency policy committee" formed to manage the shortage, rather than to independent GAO
market research.

### Key findings

**Uses of helium-3 and their approximate consumption.** The report identifies five categories of
helium-3 use, with the two largest explicitly named as creating "the greatest demand" for the
gas:

- Large-area detectors for scientific research (materials research in medicine, energy, and
  transportation), at facilities such as DOE's Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) at ORNL. These
  detectors have a surface area of about 15 to 40 square meters and can each require hundreds to
  thousands of liters of helium-3; ORNL officials stated SNS can contain up to 25 such detectors,
  with a planned 2018 expansion requiring about 25 more.
- Radiation portal monitors (RPMs) for homeland security, screening vehicles and cargo at ports
  and borders for nuclear material. Each RPM's neutron detector has a surface area of about 0.7
  square meters and requires about 44 liters of helium-3. As of the report, DHS had deployed about
  1,400 domestic RPMs (with about 700 more planned through 2015), DOE's Second Line of Defense
  program had deployed about 2,000 RPMs overseas (with about 2,900 more planned through 2018), and
  DOD's Guardian program had deployed 24 RPMs using helium-3 domestically and overseas with plans
  for 12 more using an alternative technology.
- Smaller handheld and backpack security detectors, each using a small quantity of helium-3, with
  a total projected annual demand across the U.S. government of about 7,000 liters from 2011 to
  2015.
- Oil and gas well logging, using neutron detectors in exploratory drill shafts to assess the
  likelihood of oil or gas; annual demand for this application is estimated at about 1,000 liters.
- Industrial moisture gauges measuring moisture content in rock and soil, for example to support
  road construction; annual demand is estimated at about 500 liters.

Two non-detector uses are also named: magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), where a patient inhales
helium-3 gas during a scan of the pulmonary structure to study lung disorders such as chronic
obstructive pulmonary disease, and scientific research involving ultra-low-temperature
refrigeration systems. Fusion energy is not mentioned anywhere in this document as a use, market,
or driver of helium-3 demand; the report's entire demand accounting is built from the five
detector and industrial categories above plus the MRI and cryogenics uses, with no reference to
fusion fuel, fusion research, or fusion power at all.

**Production and supply quantities.** Naturally occurring helium-3 is stated to make up only a
few parts per million of natural helium gas (the remainder being helium-4), and extraction of
helium-3 from natural gas deposits is stated to not be commercially pursued because the
concentrations are too low to be economically viable. All of the U.S. supply is therefore
described as coming from tritium decay. NNSA is stated to estimate it will make available about
8,000 to 10,000 liters of helium-3 per year for distribution, which the report states is "less
than the demand created by neutron detectors and other applications." Figure 2.5 plots this flat
8,000 to 10,000 liter annual production line against two projected total demand curves, one as
projected in 2009 (before steps were taken to reduce demand) and one as projected in 2011 (after
the policy committee's allocation measures), for fiscal years 2011 through 2018; the underlying
numeric values of the demand curves themselves did not extract cleanly from the source PDF's
chart, only the flat production line and the surrounding narrative describing projected demand as
still exceeding production even after the 2011 reduction. Separately, the text states that as of
the report, an inventory of roughly 31,000 liters of helium-3 remained available to help close the
gap between demand and current annual production. Figure 2.6 further breaks 2011-projected demand
into four categories (security applications excluding RPMs but including portable detectors;
science applications including large-area and smaller detectors; other research and industrial
applications including oil and gas exploration; and other U.S. government-funded applications
including NASA-associated missions), again against the flat 8,000 to 10,000 liter production
line; this chart explicitly excludes RPM demand, because the policy committee had already
eliminated helium-3 allocations for domestic RPMs (see policy response below), so RPM demand is
not part of the figure 2.6 breakdown at all.

**Price.** The report states that before the shortage, the Isotope Program's minimum sale price
for helium-3 (set to recover extraction and administrative costs) typically ranged from $40 to
$85 per liter, a level the report characterizes as low or competitive compared to detector
alternatives. A footnote citing the companion report GAO-11-472 states that in 2011, helium-3
bought from the U.S. government cost $600 to $1,000 per liter, a roughly ten-fold increase
attributable to the shortage and the resulting allocation and pricing response (this comparison
across the two cited figures is derived here; the report does not itself state the multiple).

**Policy response: allocate and reduce demand, not grow supply.** In 2009, the National Security
Staff established an interagency policy committee of officials from Commerce, DHS, DOE, and the
Department of State to address the shortage. This committee established criteria and a process
for allocating the limited helium-3 supply to government and non-government customers, with
applications having no helium-3 alternative receiving highest priority, followed by foreign
nuclear-detection programs, followed by programs with substantial sunk costs. Critically for the
demand question, the committee determined that alternatives to helium-3 would suffice for RPMs in
security applications, and on that basis it eliminated helium-3 allocations for domestic RPM
deployments beginning in fiscal year 2010. The stated purpose of developing boron-10 lined
proportional detectors, boron trifluoride proportional detectors, and lithium-6 scintillators
throughout the report is explicitly to reduce demand for helium-3, not to expand or serve any new
market; the report states DHS considers both boron-10 and lithium-6 to be in sufficient supply for
future RPM deployments, and that DOE officials stated the U.S. stockpile of lithium-6 is
sufficient to meet detector demand, with lithium-6 reserved by NNSA for detector use. Both
boron-10 and lithium-6 are described as export-controlled materials. Separately, a 2010
international collaboration of 10 research facilities (including ORNL's SNS, NIST, Japan's
J-PARC, Germany's Julich Centre for Neutron Science, and Switzerland's Paul Scherrer Institute)
agreed to jointly develop and test alternative large-area detector technologies on a multi-year
plan, again framed as reducing helium-3 demand for science facilities. Four federal agencies
(Commerce, DNDO, DOD, and DOE) are stated to be coordinating more than 30 research and
development projects toward additional alternative neutron detector technologies, with about $16
million in fiscal year 2009 funding and about $20 million in fiscal year 2010 funding awarded to
industry, academic, and national laboratory projects. Nowhere in this policy discussion, or
anywhere else in the document, is expanding helium-3 production, developing new supply sources,
or growing any helium-3 market presented as a policy option; every described action either
reallocates a fixed, shrinking supply or works to substitute non-helium-3 materials to shrink
demand.

### Limitations

This is fundamentally a detector-technology readiness assessment, not a market or economic study
of helium-3; the supply, demand, and price figures reported above are background context the
report attributes to an interagency policy committee's analysis or to a separately cited GAO
report (GAO-11-472), not to independent primary research conducted for this document. The
numeric values underlying the demand curves in Figures 2.5 and 2.6 did not extract as legible
axis data from the source PDF, only the flat 8,000 to 10,000 liter annual production reference
line, the approximately 31,000 liter remaining inventory figure stated in text, and the category
labels; the shape and magnitude of projected demand relative to that production line could not be
independently confirmed beyond the qualitative statement that projected demand exceeds
production. Figure 2.6's demand breakdown explicitly excludes RPM demand once domestic RPM
allocations were eliminated in fiscal year 2010, so the four demand categories shown are not a
complete picture of total helium-3 demand as of 2011. All data in this report are circa 2011: the
demand and production projections run only through fiscal year 2018, the price figures are
current only "in 2011," and the report reflects fieldwork conducted from July 2010 to September
2011. No later update, revision, or reconciliation of these 2011 projections against actual
outcomes is present in this document. The report does not address helium-3 as a fusion fuel or
discuss fusion energy in any capacity; its complete accounting of helium-3 demand is limited to
detector applications (large-area science detectors, radiation portal monitors, handheld and
backpack security detectors), industrial well-logging and moisture-gauge use, MRI, and low
temperature research.
