## Abstract (transcribed)

Dwarf planet Ceres is a compelling target for future exploration because it hosts at least regional brine reservoirs and potentially ongoing geological activity. As the most water-rich body in the inner solar system, it is representative of a population of planetesimals that were likely a significant source of volatiles and organics to the inner solar system. The authors describe possible medium-class (~$1 billion) mission concepts that would determine both Ceres' origin and its current habitability potential. Habitability is addressed through a combination of geological, geophysical, and compositional investigations by (i) searching from orbit for evidence of past and ongoing geological activity near landforms interpreted as brine-driven volcanic structures, and (ii) probing the brine distribution below one of these regions with in situ electromagnetic sounding. Two approaches were considered for compositional measurements: (1) in situ exploration at two sites, and (2) sample return from a single site. Both concepts targeted material at Occator crater (~20 Ma; a site rich in evaporites evolved from recently erupted brine sourced from >35 km deep). The authors conclude that a sample return architecture from these young evaporite deposits offers greater science return—enabling high-resolution analysis of organic matter trapped in salt minerals and isotopes of refractory elements—for a similar cost and less science risk than in situ analyses. Six science objectives and two implementation concepts are described.

## Summary

### Background and objective
Ceres is the largest main-belt object and the most water-rich (~25 wt.%) body in the inner solar system. Dawn-mission evidence (recent/ongoing geologic activity, liquid below an ice-rich crust, surface organic matter, superchondritic regolith carbon, an exosphere, and brine-driven surface exposures at Occator crater) qualifies Ceres as an "ocean world." The study defines two overarching goals: (1) assess Ceres' current habitability as a test case for volatile-rich bodies over time, and (2) determine Ceres' origin and the relationship of its volatiles/organics to other inner-solar-system bodies. These map to six science objectives (O1–O6).

### Methods and scope
This is a NASA pre-decadal medium-class mission concept study (a "PMCS"-type point design taken to concept maturity level 4), not an observational or experimental research paper. Methods consist of: definition of a science traceability matrix (objectives → measurements → instrument requirements → functional requirements); a set of key engineering/science trades (landing site; sample return vs. in situ; single spacecraft vs. orbiter+lander; sample acquisition via legs vs. deployable arm); a strawman instrument payload; mission/trajectory design; sample curation and contamination-control planning; and comparative science-return and cost assessment of two architectures (single-site sample return vs. two-site in situ). Companion papers are cited for flight system (Brophy et al. 2022), landing-site selection (Scully et al. 2021), and planetary protection (Castillo-Rogez et al. 2021).

### Key findings (as stated)
- **Preferred architecture:** A single-site sample return from the Occator crater / Vinalia Faculae evaporites provides better science at lower science risk for approximately the same Phase A–D development cost as the two-site in situ option. Mission class is medium (~$1 billion).
- **Six science objectives:** O1 test extrusion from a brine-rich mantle; O2 test whether endogenic activity is ongoing at Occator; O3 determine depth of liquid water below Occator; O4 characterize Ceres' deep brine environment; O5 characterize evolution of organic matter in long-lived brines; O6 determine Ceres' accretional environment.
- **Target site:** Vinalia Faculae, on the floor of Occator crater (a <2 Ma feature within the ~20 Ma crater), where freshly exposed evaporites sample brine sourced from >35 km depth; the region extends ~10 km with many candidate 100 m-diameter landing sites (>90% of Vinalia Faculae have slopes <10°).
- **Deep brine:** Dawn found sodium carbonate plus smaller fractions of ammonium chlorides and hydrohalite; inferred alkaline source, temperature >245 K (~ -28 °C; text also cites early-ocean estimates of <50 °C, alkaline pH, log pH2 > -5). Depth of the current brine layer is poorly constrained.
- **Sample mass / handling requirement:** ~100 g of nonvolatile material (container sized for up to 200 g with 100% margin); returned and maintained cold ( -20 °C actively early in cruise, then passively cooled to < -35 °C) to prevent aqueous alteration and salt deliquescence; internal pressure requirement <3×10^-5 Pa; ISO 5 (class 100) organically clean cold curation. 25% of the sample used for preliminary examination over a 2 yr period, 75% retained for long-term curation.
- **Instrument-performance rationale:** State-of-the-art in situ instruments (e.g., ICEE-2 Compact Integrated Raman Spectrometer, ~10 μm spatial resolution; next-gen ORCA mass spectrometer) were judged unable to detect organics trapped in salt grains at ppb / <10 nm scales, and cannot measure key isotope systems (e.g., 17O/16O, ε50Ti, ε54Cr). Earth laboratories offer 1–several orders-of-magnitude better spatial resolution and detection limits, driving the sample-return conclusion.
- **Propulsion / trajectory:** Round-trip ΔV ~14 km/s achievable with solar electric propulsion (SEP; Dawn provided 11.5 km/s), plus ~600 m/s of monopropellant hydrazine for landing/takeoff. Launch on a Falcon Heavy-class vehicle (5 m fairing, C3 ~13.4 km²/s²) in any year 2030–2037. Total flight time ~13 yr (~6.7 yr outbound, ~500 day stay at Ceres, ~4.7 yr return); includes a Mars gravity assist. Surface gravity ~2.8% of Earth's (no anchoring needed; ~930 N downward force).
- **Sample return capsule (SRC):** Based on Stardust/OSIRIS-REx heritage; passive spin-stabilized capsule; parachute landing at Utah Test and Training Range (UTTR); reentry within Stardust SRC stress/thermal capability; SRC volume 0.032 m³; low-leakage seal (10^-10 Pa m³/s helium, attributed to Honeybee Robotics) plus argon cover gas and an ammonia (compressed-gas expansion) cooling system.
- **In situ payload (both concepts share some elements):** narrow-angle camera (two color filters ~450 nm blue, ~850 nm red; iFOV 10 μrad; <5 m/pixel for O1/O2, <30 cm/pixel for landing-site hazard mapping); Ceres Magnetotelluric Sounder (magnetometer + four deployed electrodes, ~identical to SwRI Lunar Magnetotelluric Sounder selected for CLPS 19D; 2.5 m mast, 100 m electrode deployment / 200 m baseline, sounds to >50 km; ~27 hr integration); infrared point spectrometer (2–4 μm, 10 nm resolution, ~2.6 kg, ~7.8 W, JPL CubeSat-class, cryocooled focal plane <100 K, characterizes hydration of sampled material in situ).

### Limitations (as stated by the authors)
- Explicitly pre-decisional, for planning/costing only; not an official NASA or COSPAR position.
- The closed trades are stated to not be the only viable solutions; other teams evaluating the same trades could reach different conclusions.
- Depth/extent of the deep brine layer is poorly constrained by current data.
- In situ organic detection carries the risk of nondetection because trace organics may fall below flight-instrument detection limits (the central risk driving the sample-return recommendation).
- Contamination cannot be entirely avoided in sample return; the plan relies on witness plates, isotopic/molecular fingerprinting, and salt-grain protection to mitigate.
- No missed-thrust analysis was performed for the low-thrust trajectory; a 90% IPS duty cycle was assumed.
- Planetary protection categorization (outbound "IVb-like"; return "restricted Category V") is preliminary and requires formal NASA/COSPAR assessment.

### PROSPECTING / SAMPLING-INSTRUMENT CONTENT (flagged as likely main relevance)
This paper contains substantial **in situ instrument and sample-acquisition** engineering detail relevant to airless-body prospecting hardware. Co-author Kris Zacny (Honeybee Robotics) contributed the sampling system, and the document describes it concretely:
- **Pneumatic sampler:** The sample acquisition system uses **PlanetVac** pneumatic technology (cited to Zacny et al. 2019, 2020), in which compressed gas (N2 from a cylinder) lifts loose surface material and transports it through a tube to the sample container in the SRC. Text says it is at TRL 6 and is derived from PlanetVac systems for the JAXA MMX mission (NASA sampler contribution) and the Dragonfly mission. (In Tables 5, the sampler is also labeled "PneumaVac / Honeybee Robotics (HBR)".)
- **Configuration:** One sampling system in each of the three lander legs (redundancy); sampler heads integrated into the footpads; ejectable contamination covers over the collection heads; debris-deflection shields; pneumatic transfer tubes; and an SRC pneumatic-tube separation mechanism. Sampling verified optically.
- **Augmentation:** A **drill or mechanical break-up device** was considered to augment the pneumatic sampler for robustness across a range of ground strengths; the considered system was demonstrated to **drill to 2 cm depth** (deep drilling not required, since space weathering affects only the top ~mm–cm and pristine material is shallow).
- **Sample containment / sealing:** Low-leakage-rate seal (10^-10 Pa m³/s helium) attributed to Honeybee Robotics; sealing system is TRL 6 heritage from the CAESAR New Frontiers comet-sample-return concept (Zacny 2016); argon cover gas; ammonia compressed-gas-expansion cooling.
- **Other flight instruments described** (spectrometers / sounders): infrared point spectrometer (2–4 μm), Ceres Magnetotelluric Sounder (magnetometer + electrodes), narrow-angle imaging camera. A wide range of *ground-laboratory* analytical techniques is also enumerated (FTIR, XRD, INAA, ICP-MS, EMPA-WDS, FIB-TEM, SIMS/nano-SIMS, Raman, TEM, XANES, L2MS, UPLC-FD/QToF-MS, EA-GC-MS, GC/C/IRMS, pico-CSIA), but these are Earth-based, not flight prospecting instruments.
