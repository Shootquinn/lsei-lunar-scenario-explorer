# Energy for the Selenosphere (Lunar Evaluation, Part II)

## Citation

## Abstract
Second installment of a series on lunar industrialization and settlement. Assesses which energy technology is most economically promising as groundwork for later energy discussion. Treats three consumer sectors — habitats, transportation, production — and two source families — nuclear and solar — in variously processed forms. Quantifies power demands for each sector, compares solar-electric and nuclear-electric supply on a mass and cost basis, and argues that above roughly 50 kWe with around-the-clock operation, nuclear power is the superior lunar source. Descriptive engineering-economic study; figures drawn from the paper's own tables and charts.

## Summary

### Background and objective
The paper opens from the premise that as the technospheric and sociospheric components of the "selenosphere" grow (referencing Part I), energy demand rises correspondingly, and that low energy cost together with low transportation cost are the prerequisites for both technospheric growth and sociospheric development. It names the four principal energy consumers, paralleling Earth: habitats, transportation, industrial operations, and (later) lunar agriculture, termed "exoponics." The stated objective is to assess the economically most promising energy technology as the basis for subsequent energy discussions, examining how the choice of energy-processing modes and supply depends on the development of these components.

### Methods and scope
The treatment is a descriptive engineering-economic assessment. It walks sector by sector (habitats, transportation, production, then supply) and compares candidate primary sources and power-conversion units (PCUs) using projected 1990–2000 state-of-the-art performance data collected in tables. Transportation energy is derived from propellant-recovery bookkeeping for an enclosed launch track (ELT) and from prior mission-profile results. Production energy is estimated from terrestrial industrial electricity-consumption data scaled down to projected lunar output. Supply options are compared on a weight-per-power basis (kWe/kp) and on delivered cost of electricity, folding in Earth-to-Moon transportation cost. The analysis also incorporates the possibility of using radioisotopes recovered from terrestrial nuclear waste deposited on the Moon. Numerous figures (habitats, ELT, ballistic crude delivery, transportation cost charts, element-abundance chart, industrial-process levels, mobile industrial vehicle, weight characteristics of power systems) support the text.

### Key findings

Habitats. Initial habitats carry solar panels and O2/H2 fuel cells. Power per person scales from about 2 kWe/p for small crews of tens of persons up to about 10 kWe/p once selenic agriculture is added. Once lunar population grows beyond roughly 30–40, exclusive reliance on solar energy becomes insufficient because of night-time power needs and the associated storage burden, at which point continuously operating nuclear power is described as economically superior. Habitat requirements are placed in the range of hundreds of kWe to a few MWe for crews of 30–150.

Transportation and propellant recovery. Transportation evolves toward minimal gas release into the lunar environment (to protect the industrially valuable high vacuum and for economic reasons). An enclosed launch track can trap the H2O exhaust; hydrogen — which must be imported from Earth during buildup — is recovered by electrolysis. At 100% conversion efficiency, electrolytically separating 1 t H2 from 16 t O2 in 24 hr is stated to require 39,690 kWhe, i.e. about 1.65 MWe continuously. Overall ascent hydrogen consumption to a 60 km circumlunar orbit (CLO) is about 0.082 t H2 per t payload, of which about 0.075 t/t is spent in the ELT; at full recovery this means 1 t H2 recovered per 13.3 t payload, doubling to per ~26 t under oxygen-rich propulsion. To sustain 35 t payload per 24-hr day, between 2.64 and 1.32 t H2 must be recovered electrolytically per day; at a conservative 78% annual utilization (286 d/yr), 10,000 t is delivered to CLO annually. Recovering 5.3–2.6 t H2 daily over 286 days to deliver 10,000 t net payload requires a continuous output of 10–5 MWe. An outlying electroballistic supplier of lunar crude 17–300 km from the central processing facility (CPF) needs a plant of 5–10 MWe. If solar energy were the primary source, generation capacity would have to at least double and the excess be stored.

Transportation cost. Using a chemical Earth-to-orbit vehicle (ETOV), a nuclear geolunar interorbital vehicle (IOV) and a chemical Lunar Shuttle, the Earth-surface-to-lunar-surface delivery cost is $8640/kg when propellant delivery into near-Earth orbit (NEO) costs $1000/kg (near the then-NASA-projected Shuttle figure), or $2500/kg when ETO delivery costs $100/kg; these fall by 25% and 32% respectively when the Lunar Shuttle runs on inexpensive lunar liquid oxygen (LLOX). With LLOX used at all levels except Earth surface, costs drop to 8–26% of the baseline. For round-trip Earth–Moon–NEO cargo/personnel profiles the mean cost is stated as just over $1000/kg down to just over $100/kg. The Lunar Slide Lander (LSL) reduces descent oxygen consumption to about 7%, nearly doubling annual net payload to CLO for a given launch rate. ETO transportation cost remains the decisive factor whenever any flight-cost item (e.g. LH2) must be supplied from Earth, motivating cost-effective extraction of lunar hydrogen.

Production. An element-abundance-and-use chart correlates lunar elements with their applications. Ilmenite and plagioclase are the most abundant basaltic minerals; ilmenite is 32 mole% oxygen versus 12 mole% for plagioclase, favoring mare locations for oxygen yield. Suggested CPF siting: equatorial or near-equatorial mare sites with nearby moderate highlands — western Oceanus Procellarum, eastern Oceanus Procellarum west of Sinus Medii, and southern Mare Tranquilitatis — with electroballistic feeding of highlands plagioclase needing only about 250 m/s. Projected electric-energy consumption figures (kWhe/t) are tabulated: electric-furnace ferroalloys ~5000–4500, electric-furnace steel ~600–500, aluminum from alumina ~16,400–15,800, magnesium ~16,200–15,600, titanium ~40,000 (sponge), and overall solar-cell production ~38,154. A solar-thermal Brayton-powered mobile Lunar Industrial Vehicle (LIV) with a 150 Hpe drive and 200 kWe for electrolytic furnaces would produce 13–25 kg/hr of various metals, i.e. 4.2–8.1 t over 327 hr of sunshine, its 38 m concentrator generating 1240 kWt. Scaling from U.S. Census 1972 data (primary aluminum, copper, steel/iron plus electrical/electronics manufacturing consumed 18.46% of national industrial energy), the paper places a reasonable horizon figure for the first CPF buildup phase at 200–400 MWe-yr, with per-sector power for solar-cell manufacture reaching some 55 MWe and iron extraction as low as 2 MWe.

Supply comparison and nuclear waste. All solar-electric options are strongly depreciated by the need for night-time storage; solar collectors for process heat fare better (collector area only ~10% of that for photovoltaic conversion, with demonstrated mirror temperatures of at least 1800 K). Among solar-electric PCUs the Brayton cycle is judged most promising. Radioisotopes from terrestrial nuclear waste could be available if extraterrestrial disposal of long-lived waste were decided in the early 1980s and a lunar nuclear waste depot established; disposal-mission cost tables give values around 0.1–1.0 ¢/kWhe. Depositing the LWR waste from 1 TWt-yr reactor output (≈315 GWe-yr) at the lunar surface takes 244 Shuttle missions by single-launch mode or 35 by multiple-launch mode. The waste radioisotope mix performs relatively poorly and is proposed for habitat heat or low-level thermoelectric power; the preferred spacecraft isotope 238Pu (86.4 yr half-life, ~6% decline in 10 yr) is produced more abundantly in breeder than in LWR waste.

Nuclear superiority. For power levels above about 50 kWe and continuous operation, nuclear power (excepting waste-derived radioisotopes) is stated to be superior to all solar alternatives: less costly, unaffected by day/night temperature cycling, corpuscular radiation and micrometeorites, readily shielded by lunar topography, long operating life (≥7 yr), and requiring no energy storage. Even doubling projected photovoltaic kWe/kp by 1995 would leave it inferior to nuclear systems around 50 kWe. A thermoelectric (TE) system with a 1500 kWt reactor delivering at least 100 kWe weighs about 1.6 t and over a 7-year life delivers about 6×10^6 kWhe (3750 kWhe/kg), needing ~190 kg of new fuel after 7 years. Assuming a 220 kg terrestrial supply requirement, the energy/supply ratio is 24,270 kWhe/kg; at $8640/kg supply cost the electricity cost is about $0.356/kWhe. Reactors of 50 kWe are projected demonstrable in orbit by the mid-1980s and multi-hundred kWe units in the 1990s. TE conversion is favored for simplicity, reliability, low maintenance, and manufacturability on the Moon. The cheapest heat source in both cost and transportation terms is stated to be heat released by nuclear detonations, analyzed elsewhere.

### Limitations
The paper is explicitly a projection using assumed 1990–2000 state-of-the-art performance data, and the author notes the values are indicative. Local selenographic abundances are said to vary considerably from the mean values shown, and best sites remain to be determined by future orbital prospecting. Transportation cost figures are described as approximate direct flight cost only, excluding amortization of lunar facilities construction and costs tied to vehicles, production installations, and orbital/lunar operations. The radioisotope-waste option is contingent on policy decisions not yet made. A lunar product market analysis is deferred to a separate paper, and the nuclear-detonation heat method is analyzed elsewhere.

### Topic mapping
- Lunar surface power systems: nuclear (thermoelectric, Brayton cycle, gas core, nuclear detonation) versus solar (photovoltaic, thermoelectric, collector/Brayton, laser power satellites at libration points)
- Energy demand by sector: habitats and selenic agriculture (exoponics), transportation, industrial production
- Propellant recovery and transportation: enclosed launch track (ELT), electrolysis of trapped H2O, lunar liquid oxygen (LLOX), Lunar Slide Lander (LSL), Earth-to-Moon transportation cost
- Lunar resource processing: element abundances, ilmenite/plagioclase, electrolytic and centrifugal metal separation, mobile Lunar Industrial Vehicle, strip mining, projected industrial energy consumption
- Nuclear waste utilization: extraterrestrial disposal, lunar nuclear waste depot, radioisotope thermoelectric power, 238Pu
- CPF siting on the Moon: mare versus highlands, western/eastern Oceanus Procellarum, Mare Tranquilitatis
