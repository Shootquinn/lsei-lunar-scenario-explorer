# A Framework for the Economic Evaluation of Lunar Mining Projects

## Citation

Smith-Vaniz, G., Stähler, S. C., & Kehl, F. (2026). A framework for the economic evaluation of
lunar mining projects. *Space and Planetary Resources, 2*, Article 3.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s44461-026-00008-9

Publisher URL: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44461-026-00008-9

## Abstract

Proposes a diagnostic economic-structuring framework for lunar mining. Adopts discounted cash
flow (DCF) analysis not to value a present-day project but to disaggregate the net present value
(NPV) formulation into constituent variables (resource grade, recovery factor, price, sold
quantity, operating and transport costs, maintenance and energy costs, and four discount-rate
components) and to map each variable onto the domain that must be constrained before it can be
bounded: geological characterization, market formation, technology maturation, or operational
stability. From this mapping the paper derives a logically ordered dependency chain, or "critical
path," running from resource characterization through market development and technology
maturation to operational stability, and argues that departing from this sequence risks
misaligned assumptions, inefficient technology development, and capital misallocation.
Illustrates the framework with three resource case studies (helium-3, oxygen from regolith, water
ice), each occupying a different position along the critical path depending on whether its
geological or market uncertainty is more binding, and closes with policy recommendations for
prospecting campaigns, market-signal formation, and shared technology infrastructure. Method is
conceptual and analytical (equation disaggregation plus literature synthesis), not empirical or
site-specific; no original geological, market, or technical data are generated or analyzed.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper situates lunar mining within long-standing visions of a future space economy in which
scientific work, exploration, and commercial activity reinforce one another. It states that while
recent advances have brought aspects of these visions closer to technical feasibility, the
economic plausibility of space resource extraction, and lunar mining specifically, "remains
widely questioned," citing Lindbergh (2024). Extraterrestrial mining as a concept is traced back
over a century (Serviss, 1898), and the authors argue that today's obstacle is not a lack of
technical imagination but the absence of a coherent framework for evaluating progress toward
eventual economic viability. Skepticism toward lunar mining is attributed not only to technical
uncertainty but to the perceived prematurity of economic analysis itself.

The paper's diagnosis of the field is explicit: progress remains fragmented, "with limited
consensus on development priorities" or on the sequence of actions required to reach economic
viability. In the absence of an articulated economic objective, the authors argue, the wide range
of scientific investigations, technology demonstrations, and architecture-specific concepts
pursued in the field cannot be organized within a common developmental logic, and there is no
consistent basis for judging how individual activities contribute to long-term maturity, how they
should be sequenced, or which uncertainties they are meant to resolve. The paper contrasts this
with terrestrial capital-intensive mining, where projects become institutionally financeable only
once they satisfy formal DCF-based economic decision criteria; for lunar mining, such criteria
cannot yet be evaluated because of pervasive uncertainty. Rather than treating this as a reason
to defer economic analysis, the paper treats the present inability to compute a defensible NPV as
"a diagnostic opportunity": the parameters required for DCF evaluation reveal which uncertainties
must be resolved for the sector to mature.

The stated objective is to articulate such a framework and apply it to lunar resource
development. The paper positions itself as complementary to prior architecture-specific
techno-economic studies, including asteroid-mining simulation (Hein et al., 2020), lunar
propellant production modeling (Kornuta et al., 2019), resource-royalty and governance proposals
(McKeown et al., 2023), and, closest in spirit, McKeown et al.'s (2024) work defining
risk-adjusted investment "hurdle rates" that space ventures must satisfy to attract capital.
Where that hurdle-rate work addresses the financial threshold itself, this paper states its aim
as addressing "the logical sequence of informational anchors required to satisfy such economic
requirements," i.e., what must be known, and in what order, before hurdle-rate or NPV criteria
can be meaningfully evaluated for a specific lunar project.

### Methods and scope

The paper is conceptual and analytical rather than empirical. It adopts the standard
mining-economics NPV formulation, NPV = sum over t=0 to N of (R_t - C_t)/(1+r)^t - C_i, where R_t
is revenue in year t, C_t is operating cost in year t, r is the discount rate, N is the economic
project lifetime, and C_i is the initial capital investment. It then disaggregates R_t, C_t, and
r into constituent sub-variables (resource grade, recovery factor, unit price, excavated and
recovered quantities, unit operating and transport costs, maintenance and energy costs, and four
additive discount-rate components) and maps each sub-variable onto the domain of knowledge that
must mature before it can be empirically bounded: resource characterization, market development,
technology performance, operational scalability, or financing conditions. The framework is not
applied to compute an actual valuation for any real project; it is used diagnostically, to
identify which variables can currently be constrained and which remain speculative.

The body of the paper is organized into four expository sections that mirror this disaggregation:
"Economic evaluation of mining projects" (introducing DCF and NPV as the reference framework and
end-state decision gate), "Resource understanding" (geological uncertainty, prospecting, and
resource modeling), "Market and policy" (demand formation and policy transparency), and
"Technology" (technological uncertainty, technology maturation, techno-economic feasibility
analysis, and the operational phase). These are synthesized into a "Strategic roadmap" section
presenting a critical path and a set of policy recommendations, followed by three "Illustrative
pathways" case studies (helium-3, oxygen from regolith, water ice) that apply the roadmap logic
qualitatively to specific resources. A summary table (Table 1) maps each section to the specific
NPV variables it is said to constrain. The paper draws on roughly sixty prior sources spanning
geology, techno-economic modeling, governance, mining economics, and industry/agency program
documentation, plus one numerical sensitivity illustration constructed by the authors; it does
not present new geological, market, or technical data of its own for any specific lunar deposit
or project.

### Key findings

**DCF/NPV as an end-state decision gate, not a valuation tool.** The paper adopts DCF because, in
terrestrial capital-intensive mining, projects "become financeable only when expected discounted
cash flows justify total capital outlay, yielding NPV>0." NPV is retained as the central
criterion over alternatives such as internal rate of return (IRR) or payback period because it
represents absolute value creation and functions as "the primary economic gate for financeable
mining projects." The paper states explicitly that "DCF is not used to perform a project
valuation, nor to solve for NPV" in this study, because the empirical information needed to
populate a defensible model does not yet exist for lunar mining; instead, the inability to
evaluate NPV is treated as diagnostic of present sector immaturity.

**Reserve/resource classification is directly engaged, via McKelvey (1974) and CIM standards,
though not by the names JORC, NI 43-101, or CRIRSCO.** The paper explicitly imports the
terrestrial mining distinction between "resources" (total identified deposits) and "reserves"
(the subset that can be profitably extracted), attributing this distinction to Vincent McKelvey
(1974) and reproducing it as Figure 1, "Distinction between resource and reserve." It states that
on Earth, "DCF analysis, and the associated concepts such as NPV, thus provides a quantitative
framework for evaluating and enabling" the transition from resource to reserve, and that advances
in technology, cost reductions, or favorable price shifts can convert resources into reserves.
The paper then reproduces a second figure, Figure 2, "Resource and reserve categorization,"
explicitly "Adapted from CIM definition standards for mineral resources & mineral reserves,"
citing the Canadian Institute of Mining, Metallurgy and Petroleum (2014). Using this
categorization, the paper states that current lunar data (from Clementine, Lunar Prospector,
Chandrayaan-1's M3, SELENE/Kaguya, SMART-1, and LRO) support only "inferred resources," not
"proven reserves," and that advancing from the former to the latter requires detailed geological
characterization, without which "estimates of economic viability remain speculative and
disconnected from operational reality." The paper's reference list additionally includes the
Canadian Institute of Mining, Metallurgy and Petroleum's "CIM Best Practice Guidelines for
Estimation of Mineral Resources and Mineral Reserves" (2019) and the "CIMVAL Code for the
Valuation of Mineral Properties" (2019), both cited in-text under the shortened form "Canadian
Institute 2019" in connection with DCF being "the standard for evaluating a project's financial
viability" and with the use of "stochastic risk modeling and detailed sensitivity studies" once
site-specific techno-economic evaluation becomes possible. The paper does not name JORC, NI
43-101, SAMREC, SME, or the CRIRSCO framework anywhere in the visible text; its explicit
terrestrial reserve-classification anchor is the McKelvey (1974) resource/reserve diagram
together with CIM's Canadian definition standards and valuation code.

**Parametric disaggregation of the NPV formula.** Revenue at time t is defined as R_t = Q_r,t ·
p_t, where Q_r,t [kg] is recovered resource quantity sold and p_t [$/kg] is unit price; recovered
quantity is further defined as Q_r,t = G · F · Q_e,t, where G is resource grade, F is the
recovery factor, and Q_e,t is total excavated mass. Operating cost is defined as C_t = Q_e,t ·
(c_opex,t + c_transport,t) + C_m,t + C_energy,t, separating unit excavation/processing cost, unit
transport cost, maintenance cost, and energy cost as distinct categories, each tied to a
different source of uncertainty (excavation efficiency and throughput scaling; surface mobility,
ascent, and in-space transfer; system reliability and redundancy; local resource availability and
duty cycle, respectively). The discount rate is decomposed as r = r_base + r_tech + r_market +
r_policy, i.e., a baseline return requirement plus additive risk premia for technology, market,
and policy/regulatory uncertainty; the authors describe this decomposition as an interpretive
device rather than a claim about how discount rates are set in practice. Combining all terms
yields an "abstracted representation" of NPV (their Eq. 6) that the paper states would require
"substantially greater certainty across all parameters than is currently available for lunar
mining" to apply defensibly; its stated role is "diagnostic," to show which uncertainties must be
reduced, and in which domains, before viability can be assessed.

**Numeric sensitivity illustration.** To demonstrate the compounding effect of discounting, the
paper works a hypothetical example: an upfront investment of $1,000 million and annual net cash
flows of $200 million over ten years. At a discount rate of 15%, the resulting NPV is
approximately $3.8 million; raising the discount rate to 16% yields an NPV of approximately
-$33.4 million. The paper uses this to argue that "uncertainty-driven increases in required
returns can eliminate apparent economic viability," underscoring the importance of reducing
informational uncertainty rather than relying on nominal performance assumptions. No other
project-specific dollar valuations, IRR figures, cost-per-kilogram estimates, or production
thresholds are given for any actual or proposed lunar mining project.

**Geological/prospecting stage.** The paper identifies geological uncertainty as "one of the most
immediate sources of uncertainty in space resource economics," noting that direct lunar
measurements remain sparse and that whether a target resource is chemically bonded, physically
bound within regolith, concentrated in pure aggregates, or dispersed determines both extraction
method and full cost structure. It distinguishes "exploration" (localized scientific or
technology-demonstration goals) from systematic "prospecting" (deliberate, repeated regional data
acquisition at operational scale), arguing that current and planned missions (Clementine, Lunar
Prospector, Chandrayaan-1/M3, SELENE, SMART-1, LRO, JAXA's LUPEX, NASA's VIPER) remain
"fundamentally exploratory" and do not provide the geographic breadth or density of data needed
to move from inferred resources to proven reserves. It recommends mass-produced fleets of
identical prospecting systems over bespoke one-off rovers to reduce per-unit cost, cites the
International Lunar Resource Prospecting Campaign (ILRPC) as an existing coordination effort, and
proposes hybrid public funding models such as government data-purchase agreements, drawing
analogies to NOAA's commercial data-purchase program and NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services
(CLPS) program. It further argues that resource models (three-dimensional extrapolation from
sparse point data, potentially using geostatistics, inverse modeling, or machine learning) are as
important as the raw data itself, since methodology-dependent interpretation shapes both
resource-estimate reliability and which data prospecting missions should prioritize collecting.
From an economic standpoint, the section's purpose is stated as constraining resource grade (G),
recoverable quantities (Q_e,t, Q_r,t), and, by extension, achievable recovery factor (F) and unit
operating costs; "until these parameters are bounded, downstream economic evaluation remains
structurally unconstrained."

**Market and policy stage.** The paper describes a "chicken-or-the-egg conundrum" for in-situ
resource utilization (ISRU): projects depend on market demand to develop, yet local resource use
is often deemed necessary for that market to develop in the first place. It contrasts a
technology-first (supply-first) pathway, which it characterizes as long-timeline, uncertain, and
best suited to publicly supported or strategically funded programs rather than commercial
investment, with a demand-first approach that it recommends: prioritizing activities with real,
persistent paying demand (returning rare resources to Earth, in-space/low-gravity manufacturing,
satellite refueling) so that "supporting activities will follow naturally" once demand grows,
rather than assuming "if we build it, they will come." On policy, the paper argues that
government procurement stability (citing CLPS as an example) directly lowers policy-risk premium
(r_policy), and that early transparency about expected resource types, quantities, and price
ranges reduces market-risk premium (r_market) and helps mining ventures attract capital. This
stage's stated economic purpose is to constrain price (p_t) and demand quantity (Q), and, through
those, the market and policy components of the discount rate.

**Technology stage.** The paper argues real extraction/processing technology cannot be finalized
until geological and market parameters are sufficiently understood, since system design
implicitly assumes values for grade, recovery targets, throughput, acceptable unit costs, and
energy demand; if later data invalidate those assumptions, systems risk being "inefficient,
mis-scaled, or economically misaligned despite technical success." It introduces a standard
learning-curve relation, C_n = C_0 · n^(-b), where C_n is the cost of the n-th kilogram produced,
C_0 the initial unit cost, and b the learning rate, citing the launch industry's historical cost
reduction as an illustrative precedent. It defines resource rent as R_r = V - c_opex,t [$/kg],
where V is resource value per unit mass, with R_r > 0 marking "the onset of profitable
operations" and the point at which a deposit moves from the "resource" to the "reserve" category
in the paper's earlier classification scheme. The paper proposes a shared lunar-base concept in
which co-located companies and organizations pool infrastructure (communications, power, thermal
management, mobility, transport) in a "plug-and-play" model to reduce individual demonstrator
costs, drawing an analogy to the Applied Physics Laboratory's Lunar Surface Innovation Initiative
(Abraham et al., 2025).

**Techno-economic feasibility analysis as "the definitive financial decision gate."** Once
resource characterization, market demand, and technical cost/risk factors are each sufficiently
demonstrated, the paper states the framework transitions from a generalized diagnostic tool to
site-specific, high-fidelity economic evaluation using stochastic risk modeling and sensitivity
studies. This stage is explicitly named the point at which developers can determine whether a
project meets the positive-NPV threshold required to secure larger, "possibly even
institutional," funding, and is described as the mechanism that transitions a project "from a
venture-supported demonstration to a bankable, scaled operation." The paper draws on Terry
McNulty's (1998) research on mine ramp-up performance to argue that less mature processes exhibit
slower ramp-up as a percentage of design capacity, illustrating this with the terrestrial
high-pressure acid leaching (HPAL) nickel/cobalt case, in which "prolonged ramp-up periods,
unforeseen technical hurdles, and cost overruns" eroded investor confidence despite technical
viability, as an economic cautionary example directly applicable to lunar operations.

**The critical path and its policy recommendations.** The paper's central structural claim is a
logically ordered dependency chain rather than a numbered tier system: geological/resource
characterization must precede market formation, which must precede technology maturation and
finalization, which must precede stable, scaled operations; the transition from development to
scaling is itself defined as "an economic decision gate." Activities may proceed in parallel, but
the paper argues that bypassing upstream informational "anchors" (e.g., designing extraction
systems before resource and market parameters are known) increases the risk of misaligned
assumptions, inefficient development pathways, and capital misallocation; following the sequence
is framed as "an efficiency condition," not a normative prescription. The paper closes with
grouped policy recommendations: (1) resource characterization and mapping, including funded lunar
prospecting campaigns, standardized resource-data reporting formats, and development of
lunar-specific geostatistical models; (2) supporting market development, including regular
government-published resource-demand outlooks and advanced market commitments/offtake agreements
at predefined price ranges as credible demand signals; and (3) technology and infrastructure
development, including milestone-based funding mechanisms analogous to CLPS, the Human Landing
System (HLS), and SBIR/STTR programs, shared lunar services infrastructure (communications, PNT,
power, data handling), and interoperable interface standards introduced gradually rather than
prematurely.

**Capital, investment, and financing.** Beyond the general architecture of milestone-based public
funding, data-purchase agreements, and shared infrastructure described above, the paper states
that to bridge early-stage losses, companies "may need continued government support or other
well-resourced patrons," and recommends auxiliary revenue streams such as licensing technologies
and selling scientific data to supplement income during early phases. It frames the
techno-economic feasibility/decision-gate stage as the mechanism for "unlocking the capital
required for industry-wide maturation" by grounding investment cases in demonstrated performance
data rather than speculative projections. No specific capital-requirement figures, funding-round
sizes, or financing-cost estimates are given for lunar mining beyond the abstracted $1,000
million/$200 million sensitivity illustration described above, which is explicitly hypothetical
and not tied to any named project.

**Legal and property-rights preconditions.** The paper explicitly states that the legal and
societal context of space resource extraction, including "property rights, benefit-sharing, and
international governance," is "not addressed in this study," describing this as "an additional
dimension" and stating that "an economic mandate alone is insufficient" — legitimacy, it argues,
also requires clear legal frameworks and social support. It flags these questions as unresolved
and as an area for future research rather than substantively engaging with them.

**Illustrative case studies.** Helium-3: described as a market-first pathway because He-3 already
has an established terrestrial market (quantum computing, national security, medical imaging),
reducing uncertainty in price and quantity variables; the paper reports that Interlune (Seattle)
has secured advance-type agreements with the U.S. Department of Homeland Security and Maybell
Quantum (Denver), though no contract values are given, and that Interlune plans a lunar payload
to characterize He-3 concentration while testing extraction steps. Despite market certainty, the
paper states geological uncertainty remains high because current resource models rely on
"localized Apollo-era samples," leaving the geological informational anchor unestablished; the
paper places He-3 at the prospecting/geological-characterization stage of its critical path.
Oxygen from regolith: described as the inverse case, where geological/compositional data are
comparatively well understood but demand (price and volume, driven by life-support and propellant
needs tied to the pace of crewed exploration) remains speculative; the paper cites the ROXY
process and a "ROXY Pilot Plant" economic analysis (Birch et al., 2026) as maturing extraction
capability ahead of confirmed demand, and states this sector must progress via off-take
agreements or defined mission manifests to reach the financial decision gate. Water ice:
described as occupying "the most nascent stage of the dependency chain," with both geological
characterization (concentration, physical form, and vertical distribution within permanently
shadowed regions) and market demand still speculative, despite orbital remote-sensing and
impact-data inferences of its existence; the paper cites ULA's conceptual orbital-propellant
pricing framework (Bennett et al., 2016) as an early but still-speculative demand signal, and
places water ice firmly in the prospecting phase of the critical path, arguing site-specific
characterization must resolve first before extraction technology or economic evaluation can
proceed.

**Fragmentation diagnosis and its stated implication.** The paper's framing conclusion is that
the uncertainties facing the sector are "structured, not arbitrary," and must be resolved in a
deliberate sequence for coherent planning and investment to proceed. It argues that deliberately
advancing along the identified critical path could, "within decades," unlock a "thriving cislunar
economy," while departures from the dependency sequence are repeatedly characterized as
increasing (not merely correlating with) the risk of misaligned assumptions, inefficient
technology development, and capital misallocation.

### Limitations

The paper is explicit that it is diagnostic and conceptual rather than a valuation exercise: the
empirical inputs a defensible DCF model would require (resource grade, extractable quantity,
operating costs, up-front costs) "cannot be defined with any confidence" for lunar mining at
present. The discount-rate decomposition into base, technology, market, and policy premia is
presented by the authors as "an analytical device rather than a claim about how discount rates
are computed in practice," and they note that real risk premia are set through "subjective
judgment, market conventions, and institutional constraints" that may not map cleanly onto the
four categories used. The cost-structure disaggregation is deliberately left coarse (e.g.,
excavation and processing costs are retained as one combined category rather than split further)
because, in the authors' words, "additional detail would be architecture-specific and reliant on
assumptions that cannot yet be constrained empirically." The paper explicitly excludes legal,
property-rights, benefit-sharing, and governance considerations from its scope, describing them
as "beyond the scope of the present work" despite calling them an "essential complement" to the
economic and technical analysis. The three illustrative case studies (helium-3, oxygen, water
ice) are qualitative applications of the roadmap's logic rather than independent techno-economic
evaluations with new data, and rely on existing, often sparse, sources — the He-3 discussion, for
instance, leans on geographically limited, decades-old Apollo-era sample data. No datasets were
generated or analyzed for the study, per its own data-availability statement, and the outlook
section identifies several areas the paper itself does not resolve, including
prospecting-campaign logistics and funding mechanisms, market evolution over time, system-level
engineering design informed by both geology and demand, and the legal/societal dimension noted
above.
