## Abstract

An IDA Science and Technology Policy Institute (STPI) report prepared for NASA, assessing
civil and commercial demand for lunar and cislunar goods and services through 2040 with a
focus on non-NASA commercial demand. Study performed September 2019–March 2020. The team
built a database of lunar companies, conducted interviews, derived cost models for major
lunar technology systems, and screened more than a dozen candidate markets for economic
viability against government budget projections and terrestrial cost comparisons. Central
result: private demand alone is insufficient to sustain a "pure-play" lunar company; the
two principal demand drivers are government expenditure on Moon activities (which creates
derived demand) and transportation costs. Only a handful of household/business markets —
lunar tourism, moon rocks, lunar-manufactured artifacts, burials, and advertising —
appear economically viable in the timeframe.

## Summary

### Background and objective
NASA anticipated a return to the Moon as the preeminent goal of U.S. human spaceflight,
with initial crewed landing in the 2020s followed by a sustainable surface base. Some in
industry argued this could seed a commercially oriented lunar economy (mining, tourism,
science, other activities) funded by governments, individuals, and businesses; if such
demand materialized, calls on NASA to underwrite lunar activity would fall. NASA therefore
asked STPI to examine the present contours and future scale of demand drivers for lunar
and cislunar activity through 2040, emphasizing non-NASA commercial demand. The report
assesses demand from civil and commercial sources, identifies goods and services that
might generate enough non-government revenue to be commercially viable, and flags those
that would require continued government support. National-security demand was noted but
not evaluated, owing to the lack of unclassified information.

### Methods and scope
The study combined four elements. (1) A database of non-governmental organizations
involved in creating a lunar economy, characterized by focus, country, maturity, and
sector (transportation, structures/habitats, ISRU, communications, supply chain, PNT,
private goods, science/data collection). (2) Interviews with companies and stakeholders.
(3) A set of parametric cost models for major lunar technology systems — transportation,
power (solar and nuclear), resource extraction (volatiles and metals), habitats, and
spacesuits — that estimate cost as a function of quantity supplied, capturing how higher
production lowers fixed cost per unit. (4) Market screening: the customer base was split
into governments/philanthropists versus households/businesses; for each candidate good or
service the team estimated provision cost on the Moon and compared it to demand, terrestrial
production cost, and (for consumer items) willingness/ability to pay. If a product could be
made on Earth more cheaply, it was judged not viable. Timeframe: through 2040. Study window:
September 2019–March 2020.

### Key findings
**Government demand.** The estimated budget available for government-funded human lunar
missions is about $63 billion over 17 years (2024–2040), treated as the ceiling on funds
flowing to the private sector for human exploration. Against an estimated per-mission cost
of $2.6 billion (low) to $4.9 billion (high), this budget supports at least one crewed
mission per year under the low-cost scenario but no more than seven missions over a decade
under the high-cost scenario. With one small science mission costing about the same as the
~$240 million assumed available annually for lunar science, roughly one science mission
per year appears feasible.

**Household markets found viable (episodically for some).** Drawing on the report's
summary table of private lunar markets:
- **Lunar tourism — flyby:** not viable using SLS/Orion (~$1.2B/customer, zero demand);
  viable using Starship at ~$35M/customer, 20+ customers, ~$700M annual revenue.
- **Lunar tourism — landing:** not viable via SLS/Orion (~$1.2B/customer); viable via
  Starship at ~$75M/customer (including lander, rover, habitat), ~20 customers, ~$1.5B
  annual revenue, assuming customers pay up to 10% of net worth.
- **Lunar trinkets / dust jewelry:** $11,400–$194,000 per kg; 0.1–7.5 kg/yr;
  ~$21,500–$1.6M revenue — viable episodically.
- **Moon rocks for sale:** ~$7,400/kg; ~1,180 kg/yr; ~$8.75M revenue — viable episodically.
- **Artifacts manufactured on the Moon:** ~$619/artifact; ~625 kg; ~$6.2–9.3M revenue —
  viable.
- **Burials on the Moon:** ~$1.2M/kg; 2–9,487 kg/yr (higher end deemed unrealistic);
  ~$14,800–$2.4M revenue — viable.

**Business markets — mostly not viable.** Advertising is viable (~$1M cost; 5–22 corporate
sponsors; ~$5–22M revenue). The rest fail: virtual reality ($6M just to transport
equipment vs. $300,000 terrestrially); platinum-group metals for Earth sale (excavation
cost >$100,000/kg exceeds the price of the most expensive metals); helium-3 for Earth sale
(ROI could be ~50 years at expected demand/prices; underdeveloped technology); lunar solar
power beamed to Earth (best case ~twice the cost of terrestrial power); manufacturing in
lunar vacuum (creating vacuum in Earth orbit is cheaper); hazardous-waste disposal
(~$2,800/kg vs. ~$0.77/kg on Earth); and superconducting/server computing on the Moon
(power alone ~200× Earth cost).

**Derived demand / ISRU volatiles.** Water for surface operations costs >$5,000/kg to
produce (25 MT/yr case, with low-volume costs unmodeled). LOX:LH2 propellant for cislunar
customers ranges ~$9,000/kg (high-cost, low-volume) down to ~$1,000/kg (low-cost,
high-volume) on the lunar surface; delivered cost to cislunar space is higher. The report
finds lunar propellant is unlikely to capture the LEO-to-GEO market (about half of in-space
propellant demand) and may not be viable if Starship meets its cost and performance targets
— i.e., it could remain cheaper to ship propellant from Earth.

**Overall.** Two principal demand drivers emerge: (1) government expenditure on
Moon-related activity, which triggers derived demand for transportation, capsules, landers,
and other goods/services; and (2) transportation costs, which set the cost of producing
and moving goods on the Moon and of shipping from Earth. Most companies tie their business
case to space agencies (especially NASA and Artemis); the database showed the United States
leading in company count, concentrated in transportation and structures/habitats where
stable long-term government contracts are likeliest. Private demand is judged insufficient
to sustain a pure-play lunar company; such firms would need to exploit proven terrestrial
or near-Earth markets. The report notes government can still cut costs and accelerate
schedules by using commercial acquisition practices to procure lunar services.

### Limitations
The analysis is bounded to unclassified information and explicitly did not evaluate
national-security demand. Estimates depend on cost models and assumptions (notably Starship
achieving its stated cost/performance targets, willingness-to-pay assumptions such as
tourists paying up to 10% of net worth, and government budget projections) whose
realization is uncertain. Several markets were screened out on cost-comparison grounds
without detailed low-volume production modeling (e.g., low-volume water production costs
were not modeled). Demand figures for nascent consumer markets (moon rocks, burials) carry
wide ranges, and the higher end of some (e.g., burials) is flagged as unrealistic. Findings
are projections to 2040 from a 2019–2020 study window and are sensitive to launch-cost
trajectories. Views are STPI's and not official NASA or NSF positions.

### Topic mapping
The report's own chapter structure defines its subject areas: (1) Introduction — background,
study questions, assumptions, analytic approach, data sources; (2) Potential markets for
lunar goods and services — final demand (governments/philanthropists; households/businesses)
and derived demand; (3) Non-governmental organizations creating a lunar economy —
descriptive statistics by focus, country, maturity, and sector; (4) Cost models —
transportation, solar and nuclear surface power, resource extraction (volatiles, metals),
habitats, and spacesuits; (5) Demand from governments — human exploration and science, with
cost and expenditure estimates; (6) Demand from households and businesses — tourism, moon
rocks, lunar-manufactured mementos, memorials, advertising, VR, precious-metal mining,
helium-3 mining, space solar power, lunar-vacuum manufacturing, hazardous-waste disposal,
and superconducting supercomputing; (7) Summary. Appendices cover the company database,
questionnaire, interviewee list, transportation cost breakdowns, lunar resources, and
tourism cost curves. Cross-cutting themes: space economics and market viability, ISRU,
launch/transportation cost, derived versus final demand, and government versus commercial
funding of lunar activity.
