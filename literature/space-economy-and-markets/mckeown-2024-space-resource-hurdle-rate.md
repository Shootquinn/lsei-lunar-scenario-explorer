# What Is an Appropriate Investment Hurdle Rate for Commercial Space Resource Development Projects?

## Citation

McKeown, B., Coulton, J., Saydam, S., & Dempster, A. G. (2024). What is an appropriate
investment hurdle rate for commercial space resource development projects? *Space Policy, 70*,
101630. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spacepol.2024.101630

Publisher URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0265964624000213

## Abstract

Practitioner-oriented economics article deriving a hurdle-rate range for commercial space
resource (lunar/asteroid/Mars in-situ resource) development-stage projects, in the absence of
an operating industry from which to observe actual practice. Uses two complementary methods:
(1) a survey of hurdle-rate and discount-rate proxies (expected project IRR, corporate CFROI,
VC/PE target IRR) reported in the terrestrial mining, oil & gas, aerospace and venture-capital
sectors, treated as analogues; and (2) an original "Risk Build Up Method" (RBUM) that scores
project-specific commercial risks against a fixed weighting scheme and converts the resulting
score into a risk premium added to an assumed 7% baseline WACC. Concludes that a hurdle rate
around 25% is an appropriate starting point for development-stage commercial space resource
projects, conditional on a suitable legal/regulatory regime being in place, and proposes a
standardised 10% discount rate for project valuation and reporting purposes, modelled on US SEC
oil & gas convention. Draws entirely on secondary/public data (FactSet, Damodaran's online
datasets, mining feasibility studies, company investor presentations, VC/PE literature);
presents no primary financial data collection and no formal econometric test.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The authors (Australian Centre for Space Engineering Research, UNSW Business School, and UNSW
School of Minerals and Energy Resources Engineering) note that hurdle rates -- the minimum
acceptable rate of return required for an investment to be considered worthwhile -- are a
standard input into project economic evaluation but have been applied with little consistency
across the various commercial space resource project proposals published to date (lunar ice
mining, asteroid metals, Mars ISRU, etc.). Table 1 of the paper catalogues eleven prior
published economic assessments of space resource concepts (Bennett et al. 2022, McKeown et al.
2022, Bennett et al. 2020, Sowers 2020, Sommariva et al. 2020, Kornuta 2019, Jones et al. 2019,
Pelech et al. 2019, Shishko et al. 2017, Andrews et al. 2015, Craig et al. 2014, Zacharias et
al. 2011, Charania et al. 2007, Blair et al. 2002, Sonter 2001, Oxnevad 1991) and finds
discount rates or hurdle rates used ranging from 8% to 30%+ across these, most given with
little or no stated justification (e.g. Kornuta 2019 uses a 10% discount rate with "no
justification"; Sonter 2001 states "IRRs in excess of 30% will be needed to attract capital").
Because a commercial space resources industry does not yet exist, there is no observable
"common practice" hurdle rate to borrow, and companies/investors treat their actual hurdle
rates as confidential, so the authors state the objective as filling this gap with two
practical, non-arbitrary methods for deriving a defensible hurdle-rate range, aimed at project
developers, investors and government bodies (they cite as a live application the COPUOS legal
subcommittee's ongoing work on space resource benefit-sharing models, which could require
monetary modelling assumptions). The paper explicitly distinguishes the hurdle rate (the
required rate of return used to accept/reject a project via IRR comparison) from the discount
rate (used to compute NPV), noting the two are often conflated in practice and that the hurdle
rate used for an investment decision will typically exceed the discount rate used for
valuation, particularly where the discount rate approximates WACC.

The paper frames the object of study specifically as space resource projects at the
"development financing stage" -- i.e., projects that have completed appraisal and are raising
capital for construction -- rather than earlier exploration/appraisal stages, on the grounds
that (i) this is the stage at which most published space resource project economics are
pitched, (ii) it is where terrestrial mining capital requirements peak, and (iii) earlier-stage
projects are often evaluated on upside potential rather than DCF. A hypothetical "space
resources development curve" (Fig. 1, modelled on the terrestrial mine development curve) is
presented showing risk falling and funding needs changing across exploration, appraisal,
development/financing, construction and production phases, with the development-financing stage
marked as the paper's focus.

### Methods and scope

Part A benchmarks hurdle rates via analogue industries. The mining industry is proposed as the
closest overall analogue (including the nascent Deep Sea Mining, DSM, sector regulated by the
International Seabed Authority under UNCLOS), triangulated against the oil & gas industry, the
aerospace industry, and the venture capital/private equity (VC/PE) sector. Because direct
hurdle-rate disclosures are scarce, the authors use two proxies: expected project IRR (from 25
Junior Miner feasibility studies sourced via the SEDAR disclosure database for projects that
subsequently secured development financing or were acquired and developed, plus 29 disclosed
Major Miner greenfield/brownfield project IRRs from investor presentations) and 5-year average
corporate CFROI (Cashflow Return on Investment, a company-level proxy for project IRR) for 29
mining, 20 oil & gas and 12 aerospace companies sourced from the FactSet database, benchmarked
against 5-year average industry WACC sourced from Aswath Damodaran's published dataset.
Discount rates actually used in the 25 Junior Miner feasibility studies were separately
collated. For oil & gas, the authors rely on analyst/financing-house references to target
equity returns for Proven Undeveloped (PUD) reserve acquisitions (Raymond James, Kayne
Anderson, BMO). For aerospace, they identify a single public data point: a 2017 Goldman Sachs
research report giving an expected IRR range for a representative $250M satellite. For VC/PE,
they synthesise target-IRR-by-investment-stage data from multiple literature sources
(Damodaran, Swildens/Industry Ventures, a February 2023 practitioner conversation with a mining
private equity firm, and the Tauri Group's space-investment dataset) and net-IRR-to-LP survey
data from Gompers et al.'s survey of US PE funds.

Part B develops the RBUM, an original project-level framework applied hypothetically (not to a
real project) in five steps: (i) estimate a baseline company/project WACC using the standard
formula WACC = wd*kd*(1-t) + we*ke, where wd/we are debt/equity weights, kd is cost of debt, t
is the tax rate and ke is cost of equity (noting ke could be derived via CAPM, ke = Rf +
beta*(Rm-Rf), for large liquid companies, though this is not directly available for a
non-existent industry); (ii) identify a fixed set of nine generic commercial risk factors and
assign each a subjective "Risk Weighting" reflecting relative importance; (iii) score each risk
factor 1-10 ("Risk Rating") for a specific hypothetical project against a qualitative
low/medium/high rubric; (iv) multiply weighting by rating and sum to a project "Risk Score,"
then convert that score to a "Risk Premium" percentage via a lookup table; (v) add the Risk
Premium to the baseline WACC to obtain the project hurdle rate. Two fully worked hypothetical
lunar resource projects (Project A, a low-risk project run by a large established company in a
stable legal regime, akin to a Major Miner; Project B, a higher-risk project run by a small
single-asset company under legal/regulatory uncertainty, akin to a Junior Miner) are scored
through the full framework in the main text and in an appendix.

The overall scope is a desk-based synthesis of secondary and proprietary-database sources
(FactSet, Damodaran's online WACC/CFROI archive, mining feasibility studies, SEC filings,
investor presentations, industry surveys) rather than an original survey of space-resource
investors or a formal statistical model; no primary interviews with space resource companies or
investors are reported (one informal February 2023 conversation with a mining private equity
practitioner is cited as a footnoted source for VC/PE mining-stage IRR figures).

### Key findings

**Headline hurdle-rate recommendation.** The article concludes that a hurdle rate "in the range
of 25%" is an appropriate starting point for evaluating commercial space resource
development-stage projects, conditional on a suitable legal/regulatory regime being in place,
with the range expected to evolve (and potentially narrow or shift) as an actual industry
emerges and matures. Projects earlier in the development cycle (exploration, appraisal) are
stated to warrant higher hurdle rates than the development-financing-stage figure. The paper
separately proposes a standardised 10% discount rate for project valuation/NPV purposes across
a prospective space resources industry, explicitly modelled on the US SEC's mandated 10%
discount rate for oil & gas reserve reporting (the "PV-10" convention), intended to enable
consistent project-to-project and cross-industry comparison rather than to represent any single
project's actual required return.

**Comparator benchmarks from Part A.** Expected project IRRs for the 25 Junior Miner
feasibility studies reviewed range from 15% to 80%, with a median of 28% and a mean of 33%;
100% of these projects had expected IRR above 15%, ~90% above 20%, and ~70% above 25%. The 29
disclosed Major Miner project IRRs range from 15% to 80% with one qualified exception (BHP's
Jansen Stage 1 potash project, IRR 12-14%, justified by BHP on the basis of a ~100-year mine
life and higher-IRR later stages); 97% of Major Miner projects exceed 15% IRR and 66% exceed
20%. Public references indicate Major Miners quote hurdle rates of 15-20% for new project
investment. Discount rates disclosed in the same 25 Junior Miner feasibility studies range from
5% to 10%, median 8% -- notably far below the IRR figures, which the authors read as evidence
that a predetermined hurdle rate, not a WACC-derived discount rate, is the more prevalent basis
for investment decisions in this sector. Corporate 5-year average CFROI versus 5-year average
WACC (Table 6): Metals & Mining CFROI 20.33% against WACC 6.93% (13.40-point premium); Oil &
Gas CFROI 18.67% against WACC 7.05% (11.62-point premium); Aerospace CFROI 15.01% against WACC
6.78% (8.23-point premium) -- all three showing CFROI persistently well above WACC, and the
extractive industries showing a materially larger premium than aerospace. For US oil & gas
independents financing PUD/probable-reserve acquisitions, cited target equity returns are
20-25% (Raymond James), >20% to >30% (Kayne Anderson), and 20-25% (BMO). Deep Sea Mining: the
International Seabed Authority's own fiscal-regime modelling assumes an acceptable project IRR
of 17-18%, which the authors flag as low relative to the 15-30% extractive-industry cluster and
note has not evidently been validated with industry; two actual DSM projects show higher
figures -- Nautilus Minerals' 2018 Solwara-1 Preliminary Economic Assessment indicated an
expected IRR of 28% (though outside the ISA's jurisdiction), and DeepGreen/The Metals Company's
2021 NORI-D Initial Assessment (filed with the US SEC) indicated an expected IRR of 29% using a
9% discount rate. Aerospace: the single identified reference is a 2017 Goldman Sachs report
giving an IRR range of 8-12% for a representative $250M satellite (undisclosed use), which the
authors interpret as more characteristic of mature infrastructure-asset returns than
resource-project returns, attributing the lower figure partly to satellite launch/deployment
insurance (typically ~10% of programme cost) reducing risk, and flag the finding as resting on
a single data point. VC/PE (Table 7) target IRRs by stage, synthesised across four literature
sources: Startup/Seed (mapped to the Exploration stage of the space development curve) --
Damodaran >30%, Swildens 50-70%, a Sentient Equity Partners mining-PE practitioner estimate of
"around 50%," Tauri Group-derived 26-58%; Early Stage Growth (Exploration/Appraisal) -- 40-60%
and 26-58% across sources; Late Stage Growth (Appraisal) -- 35-50% and 38%;
Development/Acquisition/IPO (mapped to the Development stage, the paper's focus) -- Damodaran
>20%, Swildens 25-35%, the mining-PE estimate "20%+", Tauri-derived 25-44%. A Gompers et al.
survey of US PE funds' net IRRs marketed to Limited Partner investors found a 15% to >30% range
with a 20-25% median; a KPMG (2016) study of four large PE firms (Apollo, Blackstone, Carlyle,
KKR) found gross-to-net IRR spreads averaging 9 percentage points, implying materially higher
underlying gross target IRRs. The Reserve Bank of Australia is cited reporting that average
non-mining Australian corporate hurdle rates have run 12-15% over a considerable period,
replicated in other OECD countries, which the authors suggest may approximate aerospace-sector
practice given aerospace's comparatively lower CFROI premium. Across all comparator sectors,
the paper's synthesis judgement (Fig. 6) is that hurdle rates for the extractive resource
industries (mining including DSM, oil & gas) cluster at roughly 15-30%, with 15% read as a
plausible practical lower bound and mining-finance commentary (citing Seeger 2019) suggesting a
20% minimum with 25%+ often required in practice for Junior Miner-financed projects;
aerospace/satellite returns are read as an outlier reflecting mature-technology,
infrastructure-like risk rather than resource-project risk.

**RBUM method components and worked results.** Baseline WACC: since no space resource company
exists from which to observe an actual cost of equity, the paper averages the 5-year average
WACC figures for mining, oil & gas and aerospace from Table 6 (6.93%, 7.05%, 6.78%), giving
6.92%, rounded up to 7.0% as the RBUM baseline. Risk taxonomy and weightings (Table 8, weights
fixed and summing to 50 across nine factors): Legal/Regulatory risk carries the highest
weighting, 9, described as "often a major commercial risk for resource projects. Potentially
the biggest risk factor for space resources currently"; Geology, weighting 8 ("grade
variability...often a key risk for terrestrial resource projects"); Market/Price, weighting 7;
Technical (new/complex technology, flight heritage), weighting 6; Mobilisation and Access
(launch, landing, site access; partly insurable), weighting 5; Infrastructure, weighting 4;
Construction & Operations, weighting 4; Environmental/Social/Governance (ESG), weighting 4,
flagged as of unclear applicability to a space context; Financial (capital structure/sponsor
strength), weighting 3, the lowest. Each factor is scored 1-10 per project against a
qualitative Low(1-3)/Medium(4-7)/High(8-10) rubric (Table 9) tailored with space-specific
descriptors (e.g., Technical "High" includes "new technology with no flight heritage";
Mobilisation/Access "High" includes difficult access "into deep PSR," a permanently shadowed
region). Risk Score-to-Risk Premium conversion (Table 10) is a stepped, increasing-gradient
(convex) curve, not linear, running from 13% at Risk Score 50-100 up to 46% at Risk Score
450-500, with the midpoint of the possible score range (50-500) deliberately calibrated to
produce a 25% premium-implied hurdle rate on the 7% WACC baseline, so as to tie the RBUM back
to the Part A benchmark range -- the authors state this calibration explicitly. Worked example
results (Table 11/12): Project A (low risk, established sponsor, stable legal regime) scores
Legal/Regulatory 3 (score 27), Geology 6 (48), Price/Demand 5 (35), Technical 6 (36),
Mobilisation/Access 6 (30), Infrastructure 6 (24), Construction/Operations 5 (20), ESG 7 (28),
Financial 5 (15), for a total Risk Score of 263, converting to an 18% Risk Premium and, added
to the 7% WACC, a 25% hurdle rate. Project B (higher risk, single-asset junior-type sponsor,
legal/regulatory uncertainty) scores Legal/Regulatory 9 (81), Geology 8 (64), Price/Demand 7
(49), Technical 9 (54), Mobilisation/Access 8 (40), Infrastructure 9 (36),
Construction/Operations 8 (32), ESG 6 (24), Financial 8 (24), for a total Risk Score of 404,
converting to a 36% Risk Premium and a 43% hurdle rate. The paper additionally illustrates a
"Project Stage Multiplier" applied to Project A to approximate earlier-stage risk: Development
stage (base case) 25%; Appraisal stage, multiplier 1.5x, implied hurdle rate 38%;
Exploration/Early Stage Appraisal, multiplier 2x, implied hurdle rate 50%.

**Legal/regulatory risk treatment.** The paper treats legal/regulatory risk as analytically
distinct from the other eight RBUM factors, describing it in a footnote to Table 8 as arguably
"binary -- either suitable legislation/regulation is in place and the project can get financed,
or it isn't, and the project would struggle to raise development financing," and states this
could be argued to mean legal/regulatory risk "sits above" a continuous risk-premium framework
altogether rather than being incrementally priced within it. The authors nonetheless include it
in the RBUM "for the sake of completeness," assigning it the joint-highest fixed weighting (9,
tied for top rank in relative importance though not in the example scores) to reflect its
perceived importance to a prospective space resources industry. Critically, every headline
quantitative result in the paper -- the ~25% central range from Part A, and both RBUM worked
hurdle rates of 25% and 43% -- is stated as conditional on "assuming a suitable
legal/regulatory regime is in place"; the paper does not attempt to price the scenario in which
no such regime exists, nor does it quantify a premium for current legal uncertainty as such,
beyond noting in the Project B narrative that its "no clear legal/regulatory framework in
place" scoring is "reflective of current situation" for real-world space resource activity
generally.

**Stated financeability conclusion.** The paper's own conclusion is that private commercial
capital alone may struggle to fund space resource development projects, at least in a nascent
industry's early years, given the combination of terrestrial-resource-project risk and
space-operating risk without necessarily the open-ended value upside typical of VC investee
companies (because a resource asset base depletes over project life, unlike a typical VC growth
company). It states plainly that VC-style hurdle rates (per Table 7, ranging up to 70% at the
seed stage) are likely "too burdensome" if applied to a finite-life resource project using
standard VC exit-value/terminal-value modelling assumptions, since terminal-value assumptions
common in VC valuation are not generally applicable to a depleting mining-type asset. The paper
states it is "unlikely" that space resource investors would accept a hurdle rate lower than, or
even equal to, terrestrial resources industry return requirements, and considers it more likely
such requirements would be higher, "possibly significantly higher" -- a view counterbalanced by
the observation that very high hurdle rates can bias investment away from long-life projects
generally. The paper concludes some form of government support (cited forms: direct financial
contribution, debt guarantees, guaranteed fixed-price offtake, guaranteed minimum return) is
likely necessary, at least in an industry's early stages, citing prior authors proposing
public-private partnership structures for space resource projects. On insurance as an
alternative risk-mitigation mechanism, the paper concludes insurance could plausibly cover
launch/flight-mission risk but is "unlikely" to be able to mitigate ongoing geological or
technological risk to a commercially acceptable level, noting terrestrial resource projects do
not currently use insurance for these risk categories either, and that even where available
such coverage could be "prohibitively expensive."

### Limitations

The authors state throughout that determining an appropriate hurdle rate is inherently a
subjective, judgement-dependent exercise, not a mechanically derivable figure; this applies
both to the RBUM risk weightings/ratings (explicitly called subjective and context-dependent,
drawn from the authors' own experience and selected literature) and to the Risk
Score-to-Risk-Premium conversion curve, whose convex functional form is an assumed choice among
several possible approaches (linear vs. increasing-gradient) rather than empirically estimated,
and whose scale midpoint was deliberately calibrated to reproduce the Part A 25% benchmark
rather than derived independently -- meaning Part B does not constitute fully independent
corroboration of Part A's headline figure. No commercial space resources company currently
exists, so RBUM's baseline WACC (7%) is itself a proxy (the simple average of three unrelated
terrestrial/aerospace industry WACCs) rather than an observed or modelled cost of capital for
the industry in question, and the two RBUM worked examples are hypothetical illustrations, not
real financed projects. Direct hurdle-rate disclosure is scarce across all comparator
industries because companies and investors treat such figures as confidential, forcing reliance
throughout on proxy metrics (expected project IRR, corporate CFROI) whose relationship to an
actual minimum acceptable return is inferential rather than definitional. The Junior Miner
feasibility-study sample (25 projects) was selected specifically because those projects had
already secured development financing or been sold as viable projects, which the authors' own
selection criteria make an ex post, successfully-financed sample rather than a random draw of
all evaluated mining projects; comparable disclosure was described as unavailable for Major
Miners' individual project economics (only 29 Major Miner project IRRs, and no Major Miner
hurdle rates directly, were available, via investor presentations rather than systematic
filings). The aerospace-industry IRR estimate rests on a single data point (one 2017 Goldman
Sachs report on one representative satellite), a limitation the authors themselves flag
explicitly. The authors note no correlation was found between the discount rates used in
project valuations and either expected project IRRs or corporate CFROI within the same
industries, leaving the relationship between discount-rate and hurdle-rate practice in these
sectors unresolved rather than modelled. The DSM/ISA project-IRR assumption (17-18%) is
characterised by the authors as possibly not having been consulted on with industry, and
therefore of uncertain acceptability. The paper explicitly does not resolve the tension it
identifies between setting hurdle rates high enough to compensate for perceived risk and
setting them low enough not to render long-life, capital-intensive projects categorically
unfinanceable, stating only that "a balance needs to be struck." Finally, the RBUM as presented
is described by the authors as a simplified first attempt focused solely on the
development-financing stage, omitting further elaborations they flag as available in principle
(e.g., a "Project Stage Multiplier" for earlier-stage projects is illustrated only as an
example, not fully specified or validated) and explicitly expected to require recalibration
against real project experience "should such an industry eventuate."

### Topic mapping

FA4, FA5. The article fills FA4 Gap 1 (bankability -- connecting legal title to financeability): it
is a project-finance-literature source, external to space law proper, that states numerically
what return an investor or lender would require before funding lunar/asteroid resource
extraction, and states explicitly that its headline hurdle-rate figures assume "a suitable
legal/regulatory regime is in place" without pricing the alternative. It substantively engages
mining-finance reserve-classification material -- footnote 4 sets out the JORC Code's
exploration-results/inferred/indicated/measured-resource and proved/probable-reserve categories
and the oil & gas industry's parallel proven/probable/possible reserve and Proven
Undeveloped/Proven Developed Producing classifications, using this comparison to justify
treating Junior Miner feasibility-study projects and US oil & gas PUD-reserve acquisitions as
stage-comparable benchmarks for the space-resource development-financing stage -- so per the
FA4 gap note this source cross-lists to FA5. Relevant FA4 sub-questions: 2 (directed credit and
patient capital -- the paper's own conclusion that private capital alone may be insufficient
without some form of government support such as guarantees, offtake or PPP structures bears on
what a lunar-equivalent patient-capital instrument would need to do) and 3 (property rights and
law -- the paper treats legal/regulatory risk as the potentially highest-weighted and possibly
"binary" risk factor in its own taxonomy, and conditions every quantitative conclusion on that
risk being resolved, without itself resolving or pricing it).
