## Abstract

Presents Aqua Factorem, a mechanical beneficiation approach to lunar polar water-ice extraction that substitutes pneumatic, magnetic, and electrostatic particle separation for thermal vaporization. Argues from geological reasoning and prior experimental/modeling evidence that lunar ice exists as fine, loose grains mixed into regolith fines rather than as solid ice-cemented ground, making it separable by grinding and beneficiation rather than requiring heat. Reports benchtop viability testing of each separation stage, a "Minimum Viable Product" (MVP) architecture sized to refuel one 2.5-ton-class satellite boost every four months, and full mass, power, and transportation-architecture roll-ups for that MVP. States a 98.3 percent reduction in mining power demand and roughly a twentyfold reduction in delivered hardware mass and initial capital cost relative to a cited thermal-extraction baseline architecture.

### Background and objective

The report frames thermal ice extraction, the dominant approach in prior lunar ISRU architecture studies, as having two structural problems: the energy budget is dominated by heating the non-ice ("lithic") fraction of regolith, and water vapor driven by heat moves away from the heat source rather than toward a collector, so most vaporized ice re-freezes elsewhere in the soil rather than being captured (p. 1). The PI states involvement in two prior architecture studies that demonstrated these challenges (p. 1, citing Austin et al. 2020 and related NIAC/ISRU work).

The stated objective is a "Minimum Viable Product" (MVP) mining method: a startup-scale architecture that can begin commercial operations and generate revenue at small size while incrementally scaling up as risk is retired, rather than a dead-end small-scale technology (p. 1). Aqua Factorem is presented as this MVP architecture, built around the premise that billions of years of micrometeoroid bombardment has already broken lunar ice into fine grains mixed with lithic fines, so ice can be beneficiated (physically separated) like any other mineral rather than vaporized (pp. 1 to 2).

Three architecture options for arranging power, beneficiation, and transport relative to the permanently shadowed region (PSR) boundary are presented (Figures 1 to 3, pp. 3 to 4): an all-mobile rover-based system recharging outside the PSR; a partial in-PSR system using superconducting cable to bring solar power to a water-cleanup plant located partway into the PSR; and a stationary in-PSR beneficiator powered by superconducting cable, separate from the mining rover.

### Methods and scope

The physical-state argument (section 1.3, pp. 5 to 9) draws on four lines of evidence to argue against solid ice-cemented regolith: the absence of a known lunar mechanism for bulk freezing of liquid water into soil; experimental and modeling evidence (Cole et al.; Hurley et al.) that micrometeoroid impact gardening is a net comminuting rather than net bonding process; the physical impossibility of liquid-water agglutinates given flash-evaporation rates in vacuum (calculated flash evaporation rate approximately 2.7 g/s at 19 K and 100 mbar, p. 7); and the fallback position that even weakly cemented ice-lithic grains can be liberated by light grinding, a standard terrestrial-mining step requiring far less energy than vaporization (pp. 7 to 8).

The extraction method (section 1.4, pp. 9 to 15, Figure 5) is a process chain: excavation with rock rejection (a "grizzly" screen) and gravel separation, light grinding to liberate ice fragments from lithic fragments along grain boundaries, then a Beneficiation System combining pneumatic separation (particles sorted by the product of density and diameter, exploiting the roughly 3x density contrast between rock and ice to give ice particles about 9x the inertia of same-diameter rock particles, p. 14), magnetic separation (removing magnetic lithic material, discarding up to 80 percent of the lithic fraction to slag before electrostatic separation, p. 15), and electrostatic/tribocharging separation (citing prior NASA work achieving 50 to 60 percent ilmenite concentration in one pass and 90 percent in two passes for a related mineral-beneficiation application, and noting ice is expected to experience 100 to 10,000 times the acceleration of similarly sized silicate grains in an electric field, p. 15).

Section 2.0 (pp. 15 to 43) reports viability testing and modeling for each separation stage using lunar soil simulants (Table 1, p. 16) and models of lunar soil and ice particle-size distributions (section 2.2), covering grinding, de-dusting and pneumatic separation, in-line and granular magnetic separation, in-line and granular electrostatic separation (including experiments with actual ice, section 2.9.1), pneumatic size sorting, and vibrational separation, closing with a summary (section 2.11, p. 43) stating that all beneficiation stages meet MVP performance requirements at low power and are scalable, and that the system was found to be relatively insensitive to the ice particle-size distribution assumed in the modeling.

Section 3.0 (pp. 43 to 62) is an architecture analysis covering four segments: mining (extraction and beneficiation), surface power, water processing (chemical cleanup and electrolysis), and transportation/utilization. The transportation analysis (section 3.2, pp. 44 to 54) models a reusable Lander-Tug pair that refuels on the lunar surface and boosts geostationary (GEO) communications satellites from geostationary transfer orbit (GTO) using mined propellant, benchmarked against satellite launch-mass data from the Union of Concerned Scientists Satellite Database (p. 44) and delta-v budgets derived from NASA GMAT trajectory simulation plus GEO-operator "rule of thumb" estimates (p. 48). Mass and power roll-ups for the mining/beneficiation/water-processing segment (section 3.7, Tables 21, 22, Appendix B Tables B.1, B.2) were computed in a spreadsheet model to support trade studies (p. 60).

### Key findings

- The MVP architecture is sized to boost one 2.5-ton-class GEO satellite every four months, requiring 27,900 kg of water per year, produced from 558,000 kg per year of regolith excavated in the PSR (Table B.1, Appendix p. 6 / PDF p. 72), consistent with the report's stated 2 to 5 wt% ice concentration basis.
- The Aqua Factorem beneficiation step is stated to remove 0.75 (75 percent) of excavated mass as waste before the concentrated regolith is carried forward, leaving 160,425 kg per year of concentrated regolith (Table B.1). Comparing the input and output masses directly (558,000 kg minus 160,425 kg = 397,575 kg removed) gives an implied mass removal of about 71.2 percent rather than 75 percent; both the "0.75" removal factor and the 160,425 kg concentrated-regolith figure are reported here exactly as printed in the source, and the report does not itself flag or reconcile the difference between the two figures.
- Total MVP energy demand is 1,193.55 kWh/day, 35,806.46 kWh/month, and 429,677.55 kWh/year (Table B.2, Appendix pp. 6 to 8 / PDF pp. 72 to 74), which the source converts to a continuous power requirement of 49.7 kW (rounded to 50 kW elsewhere, e.g., section 3.7, p. 60).
- Water processing (melting, heating, reverse-osmosis/ion-exchange pumping, electrolysis, and cryogenic liquefaction) is the dominant load at 704.59 kWh/day, about 59 percent of the daily total (Table B.2). Its components: electrolysis 335.58 kWh/day (the single largest line item), heating water from 0 to 100 C 115.05 kWh/day, melting ice 93.18 kWh/day, reverse-osmosis/ion-exchange pumping 120.00 kWh/day, LH2 liquefaction 28.39 kWh/day, and LO2 liquefaction 7.74 kWh/day plus 4.66 kWh/day for excess LO2 production.
- The mechanical/beneficiation equipment subtotal is 488.96 kWh/day (Table B.2): the Aqua Factorem beneficiator itself at 360.00 kWh/day (estimated by the source as equivalent to three water pumps), the crusher at 120.00 kWh/day (estimated as equivalent to one water pump), and each active excavator or transporter unit at 1.791 kWh/day (several listed excavator positions in the table are inactive at 0 kWh/day in this configuration).
- The source's own framing of this energy split is that the mechanical beneficiation chain performed inside the PSR is the minority of total energy demand, while the majority (the water-processing chain) is relocated to sunlight outside the PSR, which the source presents as the numeric basis for its claim of moving nearly all energy demand out of the PSR (Table B.2 closing discussion).
- Mass roll-ups (Tables 21 and 22, p. 60): elements located inside the PSR total 225.00 kg (two excavators at 25.00 kg each, beneficiation system 100.00 kg, crusher 50.00 kg, transporter 25.00 kg); elements outside the PSR (sunlit) total 2,157.50 kg, dominated by the power/thermal/control system at 1,500.00 kg and storage vessels at 550.00 kg, with water-processing subsystem hardware (melter, degasification x2, reverse osmosis, ion exchange resin, catalytic carbon filter, electrolysis, liquefaction) each in the 3.5 to 9 kg range.
- Total Surface Segment mass is reported as 2,382.50 kg (about 2.4 t) and total surface power as 50 kW, which the source states is a 98.3 percent power reduction from its thermal-mining baseline (p. 60). The whole architecture delivered to the Moon (Surface Segment plus a 1,322 kg dry-mass Lander and Tug pair) totals about 3,822 kg, at an estimated build cost of about $213M (section 7.0 conclusion, p. 63; also section 3.1, pp. 43 to 44).
- The report benchmarks this MVP against a cited baseline architecture (Kornuta et al. 2018/2019) requiring $4B initial investment, 30,000 kg of assets, and a 2.8 MW power system to produce 2,450,000 kg of propellant per year at $500/kg on the lunar surface. The MVP is characterized as roughly 8.3 percent of that mass, 5.3 percent of that initial investment, 1.7 percent of that power, and 1 percent of that annual production (p. 44).
- The mining hardware concept is based on the NASA RASSOR excavator: 20 kg haul capacity, 20 cm/s travel speed, 25 kg mass, powered by two 12V 19Ah lithium iron phosphate batteries in series, drawing 5A (120W) while driving, 10A (240W) while positioning, or 2A (48W) while excavating, requiring 8 hours of recharge after 16 hours of operation (p. 59). At an assumed 100 m maximum travel distance inside the PSR, each excavator moves 960 kg/day over 16 hours of operation; the MVP architecture requires two such excavators (p. 59). A Transporter of similar mass and power hauls Aqua Factorem output 4 km to the sunlit water-cleanup plant, also at up to 960 kg/day (p. 59).
- The water cleanup process is specified as an 11-step sequence (Table 21, pp. 58 to 59): sieving (4.0 mm mesh) and crushing, ice melting with 500 and 100 micron filtration stages, degasification-1 (vacuum spray), reverse osmosis (5 micron inlet filter), ion exchange resin, catalytic carbon filter, degasification-2, electrolysis, LH2/LO2 liquefaction, and storage. Surge storage is sized for one month of production: 258.08 kg LH2 (3,646.15 liters) and 2,066.93 kg LO2 (1,812.08 liters) (pp. 58 to 59).
- Surface power is planned as sun-tracking vertical solar PV "wings" at an estimated 50 We/kg, baselined at about 3 kW per wing with the option to scale unit count and power level, sited on high terrain with short lunar nights (about 9 Earth-days) and fuel-cell keepalive power through the night (p. 55).
- Transportation-architecture sensitivity results for GTO-to-GEO boosts (section 3.2.5, pp. 49 to 50): switching from the baseline 5.6:1 oxidizer:fuel ratio to stoichiometric 8:1 reduces propellant needs by 29 percent, the single largest lever identified; adding aerobraking alone to the baseline case is a 3 percent net loss because thermal-protection mass exceeds the propellant saved; an orbital (non-landing) Tug reduces propellant by 15 percent; ion propulsion for the GEO-to-LLO return leg reduces propellant by 11 percent; and combining the orbital Tug, stoichiometric propellant, and aerobraking ("OT All") reduces total GTO-GEO propellant needs by 46 percent.
- For LEO-to-GEO boosts (section 3.2.6, pp. 51 to 52), baseline propellant demand is about 57,000 kg, more than six times the GTO-GEO case; aerobraking alone is a 39 percent reduction (unlike the GTO case), and a split-lift-with-aerobraking configuration is a 60 percent reduction, estimated at about 16,330 kg with stoichiometric propellant or about 14,700 kg with added ion propulsion.
- A baseline Lander-Tug pair can deliver the MVP Surface Segment (under 2,500 kg) to the lunar surface within a single-digit-ton total mass in low lunar orbit (under 6 t stated for the MVP case); a larger-scale version of the architecture supporting 12 missions per year is sized at an 8,770 kg Surface Segment, deliverable in two landings of a 5-ton-capacity Service Lander or one landing of a 10-ton-capacity lander (section 3.2.8, pp. 53 to 54).
- Section 3.8 (pp. 60 to 62) identifies secondary business opportunities: excess liquid oxygen (about 3 tons every four months at the baseline O:F ratio, against a stated NASA baseline demand near 10 tons/year), a transportation/ferry service to and from a Gateway-class depot, and disposal of dead GEO satellites into graveyard orbits at lower delta-v than an active-satellite boost.
- Section 4.0 (p. 62) notes the beneficiation approach could extend to other lunar resources: anorthite (a precursor for aluminum extraction, concentrated in the non-magnetic fraction of highlands soils per the source), enhanced ilmenite via electrostatic beneficiation, and free metal grains (about 1 wt% of soil in some locations) via magnetic separation.
- The conclusion (section 7.0, p. 63) states the Phase I objectives were satisfied: the architecture meets its top-level requirements, achieves a 98.3 percent reduction in mining energy, and can be built for about $213M with only 3,822 kg of hardware delivered to the Moon.

### Limitations

The report is a NIAC Phase I conceptual/feasibility study; hardware is at benchtop/laboratory scale, and the source states technology development to TRL-6 and a Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) flight demonstration are the next steps (section 5.0, p. 62), not yet performed. The physical-state-of-ice argument (section 1.3) is explicitly presented as reasoned inference from indirect geological, modeling, and remote-sensing evidence rather than in-situ ground truth; the source states directly that "we need ground-truth to prove the physical state of the ice" (p. 5) and that site selection and prospecting remain risk-mitigation strategies rather than resolved unknowns.

Some quantitative content in Appendix B (Tables B.1 and B.2) could only be recovered from the source PDF as page images because the underlying text extraction detached row labels from their numeric values; those figures are reproduced above from that image-based transcription and cross-checked internally against Table 21/22 and running-text figures (e.g., $213M, 3,822 kg, 50 kW), which came through the text extraction cleanly and are consistent with the appendix tables. Appendix A's particle-size-distribution fitting function (p. Appendix p. 1) reports four best-fit parameter values (30.0984, 1.15507, 3.70218, 35491.2, with R-squared = 0.9983) whose Greek-letter parameter symbols were rendered as blank or missing characters in the text extraction; this is a text-extraction artifact rather than an omission in the source.

The transportation/business-case analysis (section 3.2) is explicitly lower-fidelity for LEO-origin scenarios, which the source states were not modeled at the same level of rigor as the GTO baseline and were deferred to a proposed Phase II (p. 51). Astrodynamics modeling excluded boil-off tracking, zero-boil-off mass penalties, low-thrust modeling for the ion-propulsion return leg, and station-keeping for an orbital Tug remaining in LLO (p. 48). Water cleanup hardware requirements depend on the chemical content of lunar ice, which the source characterizes from prior remote and modeled data (Tables 19, 20, pp. 55 to 58) rather than direct in-situ measurement of the target deposit.

### Topic mapping

Section 1 (pp. 1 to 15) addresses the physical-state-of-ice argument and the mechanical extraction/beneficiation method concept. Section 2 (pp. 15 to 43) addresses laboratory viability testing of each beneficiation stage. Section 3 (pp. 43 to 62) addresses the integrated MVP architecture: transportation and propellant utilization (3.2), surface power (3.3), water cleanup and electrolysis (3.4), mining/beneficiation (3.5), site preparation (3.6), and the mass/power roll-up (3.7, with Appendix B). Sections 4 through 7 (pp. 62 to 63) address extension to other lunar resources, a path to market, outreach activities, and the Phase I conclusion. Appendix A (Appendix pp. 1 to 5) gives lunar soil and ice particle-size distribution models; Appendix B (Appendix pp. 6 to 8 / PDF pp. 72 to 74) gives the MVP mass and power roll-up tables (B.1, B.2) underlying the energy and mass figures cited above.
