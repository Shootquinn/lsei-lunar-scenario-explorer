## Abstract

Final report of the NIAC Phase I study of "thermal mining," a method of extracting water and other volatiles from cold solar-system bodies by using concentrated sunlight to sublimate subsurface ice and capturing the vapor in a cold trap, without excavating the bulk regolith. The report surveys thermal-mining targets across the solar system, then develops a detailed mission concept for lunar polar ice mining: an architecture of concentrating secondary optics, a deployable capture tent placed over permanently shadowed ground, and a cold-trap hauler, delivered by a XEUS (Centaur-derived) lander. It includes lunar polar ice-distribution analysis, functional and subsystem design, mass and cost estimates, a propellant-market business-case analysis, and proof-of-concept laboratory testing with icy regolith simulants.

---

### Background and objective

- Thermal mining sublimates ice in place with concentrated solar heat and captures the vapor, rather than digging and hauling regolith. This targets the physics of ice-bearing regolith directly and aims to reduce the mechanical burden of excavation.
- The study first assesses where thermal mining could apply across the solar system (the Moon, comets such as 67P, Ceres, Themis and other asteroids, icy moons of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, and trans-Neptunian objects), then focuses on lunar polar ice as the near-term mission context.

### Methods and scope

- Solar System Survey (Section 3): catalogs potential thermal-mining targets and their ice exposures, and considers thermal mining beyond the Moon.
- Lunar Polar Ice Mining (Section 4): lunar polar ice distribution analysis (including a Colorado School of Mines model and a comparison of ice concentration versus depth for three lunar terrain types); the system architecture (space supply chain, propellant-production architecture, ice-extraction concept, and IHOP water processing by Paragon); functional analysis (capture tent, cold-trap hauler, secondary optics functional trees); the ice-extraction subsystem (capture tent, cold-trap hauler, secondary concentrating optics); the power subsystem; deployment and setup via the Vulcan/Centaur rocket and the XEUS Centaur-5 landing stage; operations (ice collection and transport, tent placement and repositioning, hauler duty cycle); mass and cost estimates by subsystem and total; and a business-case analysis of the cislunar propellant market with multiple IRR scenarios.
- Proof of Concept Testing (Section 5): laboratory testing with icy regolith simulants (Lunar Highlands Simulant-1 from Exolith Labs, and a granular icy regolith simulant at about 12 percent water by weight), across Block 1 and Block 2 test apparatus and methodology.

### Key findings

- The extraction concept uses secondary concentrating optics to beam sunlight into a capture tent sealed over permanently shadowed, ice-bearing ground; the sublimated vapor is drawn to a cold trap and hauled out for processing into propellant (LO2 and LH2).
- Thermal mining is most effective where ice concentration is high enough that most of the applied heat goes to sublimating ice rather than warming barren regolith; where ice concentration is too low, most of the heat is expended warming the regolith and very little sublimates ice, so extraction energy per unit water rises as grade falls.
- The mission architecture is sized for a lunar polar propellant-production operation delivered by a XEUS (Centaur-derived) lander, with the capture tent repositioned across a PSR to work successive areas. The companion peer-reviewed paper (Sowers and Dreyer 2019, New Space) reports the production-scale figures for this concept (about 350 kW to produce roughly 1600 metric tons of water per year at about 4 wt% ice, with the system sized at about 500 kW).
- The report provides subsystem and total mass estimates, subsystem and total development-and-production cost estimates, and a business-case analysis with three scenarios of internal-rate-of-return sensitivity to propellant price and to non-recurring cost or NASA investment, plus cumulative-cash projections for a propellant-production company and for NASA.
- Proof-of-concept testing with icy regolith simulants (about 12 percent water) validated the basic sublimation-and-capture physics at laboratory scale (Block 1 and Block 2 apparatus).

### Limitations

- The report is a NIAC Phase I concept study; the mission architecture, mass, cost, and business-case results are analytical estimates for a proposed system, not demonstrated flight or production performance.
- Laboratory testing was at small scale with icy simulants at a specific water content (about 12 percent), higher than the low-grade lunar cases; performance at low ice grade is bounded by the heat-versus-grade physics rather than demonstrated at scale.
- Ice-distribution inputs rest on modeling and remote-sensing rather than in-situ measurement, and the business case depends on assumed propellant prices and market demand.
