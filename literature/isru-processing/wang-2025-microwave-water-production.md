## Abstract

Reports a bench-scale microwave heating system that extracts water from cryogenic (-80 degC) icy lunar regolith simulant (LRS) compacted into 70 mm diameter by 70 mm height cylinders, with initial water contents from 1.96% to 13.79%. Eight heating trials are run, five at 800 W and three at 400 W, each to a 100 degC set temperature and 750 s total heating time. At 800 W the samples are dried almost completely at an energy cost of 1.9 to 10.0 W.h/g, with water collection rates up to 1.57 g/min. At 400 W the initial heating period is prolonged, but once collection begins the rate is comparable to the 800 W case. The paper characterizes power partition between water and regolith, spatial residual-water distribution after heating, and simulant thermal/particle properties, and states the method is suitable for future engineering-scale application.

### Background and objective

The paper opens with a survey of evidence for lunar water: orbital remote sensing from Clementine, Lunar Prospector, Chandrayaan-1 (M3), LRO/LCROSS, and SOFIA, and laboratory analysis of Apollo and Chang'E-5/Chang'E-6 returned samples, including SIMS and FTIS hydroxyl measurements in glasses and apatites and a Chang'E-5 hydrated mineral find of about 41 wt % H2O ((NH4)MgCl3.6H2O) (p. 1). LCROSS impact-plume results indicated about 5.6 +/- 2.9 wt % water in the target permanently shadowed region (p. 1).

The stated problem is that massive water production from icy lunar regolith is difficult because of low regolith heat conductivity and harsh lunar conditions. Prior thermal extraction approaches are reviewed: the Planetary Volatiles Extractor (PVEx) combining mining and extraction, reporting over 50% water efficiency at 5% initial water content; a small ceramic heater method (Reiss et al.) operating on regolith with 0.1% to 0.2% water from -50 to 300 degC; a laboratory-lamp surface-heating method (Purrington et al.) limited to a sublimation rate below 1.0 g/h because it dries only the surface; and a pilot-scale drilling-heating apparatus (Liu et al., ref. 46) achieving up to 0.75 g/min collection at an energy cost of 8.6 to 37.9 W.h/g (p. 2). The authors state that traditional conductive/thermal methods are energy intensive and inefficient because of regolith's low conductivity, and that extracting crystallized water conductively requires heating above 200 degC (p. 2).

The objective is to investigate microwave heating, on the reasoning that microwave energy couples directly to polar water molecules and heats a sample volumetrically rather than by surface conduction, and to demonstrate massive water production and its energy cost from a large hydrated LRS sample using an integrated microwave heating system, comparing a high-power (800 W) and a low-power (400 W) heating pattern (p. 2).

### Methods and scope

Lunar regolith simulant (LRS). The simulant is formulated to match the mineral and chemical composition of Apollo-16 highland regolith, on the basis that lunar water ice is concentrated at the poles, which are closest to highland terrain. Two Earth ores, Damiao anorthosite and Jinchuan basalt, are crushed, sieved, and mixed 80% anorthosite to 20% basalt by the paper's recipe. Table 2 (p. 8) gives oxide weight percent for the Apollo-16 sample, the anorthosite, the basalt, and the resulting LRS: SiO2 45.1 / 65.13 / 47.79 / 64.6; Al2O3 26.8 / 19.31 / 16.32 / 16.8; CaO 15.6 / 6.42 / 6.96 / 3.3; FeO (all Fe as FeO) 5.4 / 1.29 / 12.38 / 3.0; MgO 5.7 / 0.06 / 6.89 / 1.4; TiO2 0.60 / 0.04 / 1.07 / 0.5; MnO 0.22 / 0.01 / 0.33 / 0.2; Na2O 0.43 / 4.97 / 5.39 / 5.6; K2O 0.14 / 2.76 / 2.47 / 3.3; P2O5 0.10 / 0.01 / 0.40 / 0.1; totals 100.1 / 100.0 / 100.0 / 98.8. The LRS is deliberately kept low in Fe (3.0% FeO) to match Apollo-16, because Fe content governs the sample's microwave absorption behavior (p. 8).

Particles below 500 micrometers are used. Particle size distribution spans 1 to 400 micrometers with median grain size D50 = 55.5 micrometers, compared against an Apollo-16 average of 12 subsamples spanning mainly 1 to 500 micrometers (Fig. 9A, p. 8). Real particle density is 2.70 g/cm3, bulk density 1.47 g/cm3, calculated porosity 45.6% (p. 8). Heat capacity (Cp) is measured by differential scanning calorimetry (sapphire method) from -170 to 220 degC and found to track theoretical Apollo 14/15/16 relationships closely; the linear fit over -100 to 100 degC is Cp [J/(kg.K)] = 1.5625 x T[degC] + 620.7 (p. 8-9).

Cylinder sample preparation. Dry LRS carries an intrinsic water content of 0.27% +/- 0.3%. For each sample, 513 g of dry LRS is weighed and mixed with pure water by the "mud-pie" method in an electric blender (OYD-15, mixing time over 30 min) to reach one of five target water contents (1.96%, 4.76%, 7.41%, 10.71%, 13.79%). The hydrated LRS is molded in a stainless-steel triaxial mold to a fixed cylinder of 70 mm diameter by 70 mm height; calculated bulk density of the compacted cylinder is 1.904 g/cm3. The cylinder is wrapped in three layers of thin plastic sheet and frozen in a refrigerator (MELING DW-HL858) at -80 degC for over 48 hours to form crystalline ice, then a 3.3 mm diameter, approximately 25 mm deep hole is drilled to seat a Pt100 sensor before each heating run; the sample is reweighed after drilling to account for the removed mass (p. 7-9).

Experimental system. An integrated microwave furnace (ANKS-M12) generates 2.45 GHz microwaves at 0 to 1000 W into a chamber sized 110 mm x 110 mm x 130 mm. A Pt100 sensor (WZPK-291, range -200 to 500 degC) is inserted into the sample's drilled hole. An air pump circulates air at a fixed 45 l/min through inlet/outlet tubes; the system runs at positive pressure and is sealed with PU quick connectors, high-temperature sealant, and a rubber door seal. Several silicon carbide (SiC) pieces sit in the chamber to protect the microwave generator by absorbing excess microwaves once the sample has fully dried. Evaporated vapor is pumped to a ball condenser (400 mm cooling length) chilled to 5 degC, and liquid water is collected after condensation. A Sony FDR-AX45 camera records the collection process at 25 frames per second (p. 9).

Experimental matrix and data reduction. Table 1 (p. 3) lists eight trials: samples 1-1 through 1-5 at 800 W (initial water content 13.79%, 10.71%, 7.41%, 4.76%, 1.96%) and samples 2-1 through 2-3 at 400 W (10.71%, 4.76%, 1.96%). Every trial uses a 100 degC heating set temperature and a fixed total heating time of 750 s, split into a continuous-power heating period (theating) and an intermittent-power holding period (tholding) once 100 degC is reached. Water extracted is m1,water = G0 - Gf (initial minus final sample weight, Eq. 1); initial water weight m0,water = G0 x wwater (Eq. 2); extraction ratio gamma1,water = m1,water / m0,water (Eq. 3). Power absorbed by water is Wwater = (hvapor - hice) x m1,water (Eq. 4), using hvapor = 2,675.53 kJ/kg (saturated vapor enthalpy at 1.0 atm) and hice approximately zero given the -80 degC starting condition. Power absorbed by the LRS is WLRS = Cp,LRS x Gf x (Theating - T0) (Eq. 5). Collection ratio gamma2,water = m2,water / m1,water (Eq. 6). After heating, each cylinder is decomposed into six zones for residual-moisture measurement: three vertical levels (L1, L2, L3) each split into an outer ring (R2, 26.25 mm) and inner ring (R1, 8.75 mm); each zone's particles are oven-dried at 105 degC for over 2 hours and weighed before/after to obtain residual water content (p. 9). Measurement uncertainties (Table 4, p. 9): cylinder mass +/-0.1 g and water mass +/-0.1 g (Yingheng YHC-L01 scale), water content +/-0.1 mg (LC-FA004 high-precision scale), sample temperature +/-1.0 degC (Pt100).

Footnote a (sample 1-1): water vapor leaks to the environment because of inadequate condensation efficiency in the condenser, so the 23.0 g collected and 34.6% collection ratio understate actual extraction for that highest-water trial (p. 3).

High-power (800 W) pattern. Sample temperature departs from the -80 degC storage value, starting between -30 and -60 degC because of thermal lag after removal from the freezer and contact with the chamber atmosphere (Fig. 1A, p. 4). Heating period length and temperature rise rate depend on initial water content: theating falls from 385 s to 179 s as wwater falls from 13.79% to 1.96%. For wwater above 10%, a temperature "retard" plateau appears where heat is absorbed to melt ice rather than raise temperature, at about 20 degC for sample 1-2 and about 65 degC for sample 1-1 (p. 3). During the holding period the microwave input is intermittent (Fig. 1B, p. 4). Over 90% to 95% of water is extracted at 800 W within the 750 s budget (p. 3). Water extracted scales nearly linearly with initial water content (Fig. 2, p. 4); total power input Wtotal falls from about 461.6 kJ (13.79%) toward a minimum near 4.76% before a slight uptick, tracking Table 1. Wheating dominates power input when wwater is at or above 7.41%; Wholding dominates when wwater is at or below 4.76% (p. 4, and restated in Conclusion, p. 7).

Power absorption and energy efficiency (Fig. 3, p. 5, described in the interim as load-bearing). As wwater decreases from 13.79% to 1.96%, Gamma_water decreases almost linearly from 38.5% to 7.4%, while Gamma_LRS increases from about 18.3% to about 28.6%-29.7%. The remaining fraction, about 43.2% to 64.0% of delivered power, is absorbed by the SiC pieces at the chamber bottom and lost to the environment (p. 4). Energy cost eta rises only from 1.9 to 3.9 W.h/g as wwater falls from 13.79% to 4.76%, then increases sharply to 10.0 W.h/g at 1.96% (p. 5). The paper states this is markedly lower than the drilling-heating method of ref. 46, cited at about 8.6 W.h/g for 6% water content and about 37.9 W.h/g for 2% water content under similar cryogenic conditions (p. 5). The full plotted (wwater, eta) pairs at 800 W are (13.79%, 1.9), (10.71%, 2.4), (7.41%, 2.9), (4.76%, 3.9), (1.96%, 10.0) W.h/g; at 400 W the pairs are (10.71%, 2.0), (4.76%, 3.5), (1.96%, 7.4) W.h/g.

Water collection dynamics at 800 W (Fig. 4, p. 5). The first liquid drop is collected at about 100 s when wwater exceeds 4.76% (samples 1-1, 1-2, 1-3) and postponed to about 200 s when wwater is 4.76% or below (samples 1-4, 1-5), attributed to the roughly 1 m vapor transport path in the collection system. Collection continues well past the 750 s heating cutoff as the air pump clears residual vapor and liquid. Average collection rate is about 0.84 to 1.57 g/min when wwater exceeds 7.41%, dropping to about 0.35 g/min when wwater is 4.76% or below. Water collection ratio is about 30% to 40% overall (individually 24.3% to 40.4% per sample in Table 1); the gap between extraction (90%+) and collection (30-40%) is attributed to two causes: positive-pressure vapor leakage from furnace slits and connection points, and inadequate condenser capacity at high vapor loads, most notably for sample 1-1 (footnote a) (p. 5-6).

Spatial residual water distribution at 800 W (Fig. 5, p. 6). For samples 1-1, 1-2, 1-3, and 1-5, residual water content is below 0.1% at both the outer ring (R2 = 26.25 mm) and inner ring (R1 = 8.75 mm) positions, essentially fully dried and roughly uniform with height. Sample 1-4 (4.76%) is the exception: outer-ring residual water is about 0.5% to 1.0% with a bulge at the middle vertical level, while the inner ring stays below 0.2%. The text interprets this as evidence that microwave heating proceeds from the sample center outward, with the middle vertical level (L2) retaining the most water because its vapor escape path is longest (p. 5-6). The source's own panel sub-caption for Fig. 5 labels panel A as R1 and panel B as R2, which is transposed relative to the in-panel annotations, where panel A is drawn as the outer position R2 = 26.25 mm and panel B as the inner position R1 = 8.75 mm; reported here as a discrepancy noted in the source, not corrected.

Low-power (400 W) pattern. Reducing power to 400 W substantially prolongs the heating period: theating is 724 s for sample 2-1 (10.71%) versus 438 s for sample 2-3 (1.96%) (Fig. 6, p. 6). Temperature-rise retard is more pronounced than at 800 W, at about -10 degC for sample 2-1 and about -20 degC for sample 2-2. Total power input Wtotal stays below 300 kJ for all three 400 W samples, with Wheating occupying 75% to 97% of that total. Extraction ratio at 400 W is about 75.7% to 89.6%, somewhat lower than at 800 W. Power absorption ratios by both water and regolith are higher at 400 W than at 800 W for comparable samples, attributed to less energy lost to the environment at the lower power level (p. 6). Energy costs at 400 W are 2.0, 3.5, and 7.4 W.h/g for samples 2-1, 2-2, and 2-3 respectively.

Spatial residual water distribution at 400 W (Fig. 7, p. 6). Samples 2-2 (4.76%) and 2-3 (1.96%) are dried to below 0.2% residual water at both ring positions. Sample 2-1 (10.71%) is not fully dried: about 2.0% to 2.7% residual water at the outer ring and about 1.0% to 2.5% at the inner ring, again peaking at the middle vertical level. The text states the 750 s heating budget at 400 W is adequate to fully dry the 4.76% and 1.96% samples but not the 10.71% sample, which the Conclusion (p. 7) restates as "residual water content of about 2.0%." The source's Fig. 7 panel A is annotated Pmicrowave = 800 W in the panel image, while the figure caption and the surrounding text both describe 400 W results; reported here as a discrepancy noted in the source, not corrected.

Water collection at 400 W (Fig. 8, p. 7). First liquid drop is delayed to about 300 s, later than at 800 W. Collected volume then rises nearly linearly through the end of heating. Average collection rates are 0.71, 0.61, 0.39 g/min for samples 2-1, 2-2, 2-3 (decreasing with decreasing wwater). Final collection ratio is about 29% to 51.7% (individually 29.1% to 48.3% per sample in Table 1), and the text characterizes both the collection rate and final ratio as comparable to the 800 W case despite the delayed onset, concluding that 400 W operation is adequate for water extraction and that total heating time could be extended for higher-grade feedstock rather than raising power (p. 7).

Simulant properties (Fig. 9, p. 8). Particle size distribution and heat capacity of the LRS closely track Apollo-16/14/15 reference values; the LRS PSD is finer than the Apollo-16 average below about 150 micrometers and marginally coarser in cumulative terms above it. Measured Cp rises from roughly 100 J/(kg.K) near -170 degC to about 850-900 J/(kg.K) near 220 degC, consistent with the theoretical Apollo relationships (Eqs. 7-9 in the source, piecewise below and above 350 K).

Conclusion (as stated by the source, p. 7). The LRS sample is heated uniformly by microwave; sample temperature rises from -80 to 100 degC in the experiment. At 800 W, over 90% of water content is extracted and samples are dried almost completely. As wwater decreases from 13.79% to 1.96%, Wtotal reduces from 461 kJ to 228 kJ (Conclusion's rounded figures; Table 1 gives 461.6 and 230.4 kJ for the nearest matching entries). Wheating dominates power input when wwater is 7.41% or above; Wholding dominates when wwater is 4.76% or below. Gamma_water decreases linearly from 38.5% to 7.4% as wwater falls from 13.79% to 1.96%; eta rises from 1.9 to 3.9 W.h/g over 13.79% to 4.76%, then to 10.0 W.h/g at 1.96%. Collection ratio is about 30% to 40%; collection rate is about 0.84 to 1.57 g/min above 7.41% wwater, falling to about 0.35 g/min below 4.76%. At 400 W, theating is prolonged, power absorption ratios by both water and regolith are higher than at 800 W, and eta reduces to 2.0-7.4 W.h/g across the tested 400 W samples. The 750 s heating budget fully dries the 4.76% and 1.96% samples at 400 W but not the 10.71% sample, which retains about 2.0% residual water. First-drop collection is delayed to about 300 s at 400 W, but the subsequent collection rate and final ratio are comparable to 800 W.

### Limitations

The system operates at positive pressure with an air-pump-driven flow path, and the paper attributes measurable vapor loss to slit leakage from the furnace and connection tubes plus inadequate condenser capacity at high vapor loads (explicitly flagged for sample 1-1, footnote a, p. 3); collection ratios (about 24% to 48%) are far below extraction ratios (about 76% to 96%), meaning most of the accounting gap between "water removed from the regolith" and "water recovered as liquid" occurs in the transport/condensation train rather than in the regolith itself. The paper does not discuss how a positive-pressure, terrestrial-atmosphere test bench translates to vacuum lunar operating conditions; the experimental system description gives no indication the tests were run under vacuum. Only two microwave power levels (800 W and 400 W) and five/three water-content points respectively were tested, all at a single heating temperature (100 degC) and single total heating time (750 s); the paper does not report sensitivity to heating temperature or to a longer/shorter total heating budget beyond noting that a prolonged holding period would extract more water at higher energy cost (p. 6). The LRS is a chemical/mineral simulant selected to match Apollo-16 highland bulk composition and Fe content, not a returned icy sample; ice was formed by wetting and freezing a compacted cylinder rather than sampled from an in situ regolith/ice mixture. The 70 mm cylinder scale is a laboratory bench unit; the authors describe the results as demonstrating feasibility for "future engineering applications" without presenting a scaled engineering design. Two apparent in-figure/caption discrepancies are present in the source and are reported neutrally above rather than corrected: the Fig. 5 panel A/B sub-caption labels for R1/R2 are transposed relative to the in-panel annotations, and Fig. 7 panel A is annotated Pmicrowave = 800 W where the caption and surrounding text describe 400 W results.
