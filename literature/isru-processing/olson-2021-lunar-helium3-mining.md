# Lunar Helium-3: Mining Concepts, Extraction Research, and Potential ISRU Synergies

## Citation

Olson, A. D. S. (2021). Lunar helium-3: Mining concepts, extraction research, and potential ISRU
synergies. Presented at AIAA ASCEND 2021, NASA Kennedy Space Center. NASA NTRS record 20210022801.

No DOI is printed in the extracted text; the document is a NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)
conference paper record rather than a journal article with an assigned DOI.

Publisher URL: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210022801

## Abstract

A single-author NASA Kennedy Space Center review paper (15 pages) describing lunar helium-3
(3He) mining concepts developed since the 1980s (the Mark series miners, spiral mining, and
in-situ volatile release/domed enclosure concepts), summarizing 2015-2018 helium implantation and
extraction experiments (SWIM, HEAT, SCAN) conducted at the University of Wisconsin Fusion
Technology Institute (FTI) in collaboration with NASA KSC Swamp Works, and briefly discussing
potential synergy between 3He collection and lunar water/oxygen ISRU. The paper is oriented almost
entirely toward supply-side mining and extraction engineering; it carries only one short,
non-quantified passage on the commercial fusion-reactor market.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens by framing 3He as a fusion fuel that, paired with deuterium in a D-3He reaction,
produces charged-particle energy output with far fewer high-energy neutrons than deuterium-tritium
(DT) fusion, giving lower radioactivity, less required shielding, and reduced proliferation risk
relative to DT reactors. It states only about 100 kg of 3He is presently available on Earth, and
that fused with deuterium, 3He could yield approximately 19 MWyr/kg of fusion energy, so the
existing terrestrial stock could produce about 1.9 GWyr, sufficient for research and reactor
development but not for meaningful global energy production. The paper cites 1985 University of
Wisconsin Fusion Technology Institute (FTI) analysis of Apollo soil samples that first proposed
lunar 3He as a power source, with FTI researchers estimating at least a million tonnes of 3He
within the first 3 meters of the lunar surface, which the paper states could produce about 19
million GWyr of electrical energy in a D-3He reactor, roughly 7 times the energy projected to be
used across the entire 21st century. The paper's stated purpose is to describe mining system
concepts proposed for lunar 3He, summarize recent helium extraction experiments on lunar regolith
simulant, and touch on potential synergy between lunar propellant production and 3He mining.

### Methods and scope

The paper is a narrative literature and program review, not an original experimental study for
most of its length, though Section III reports the author's own 2015-2018 extraction experiments.
Section II reviews mining system concepts developed mainly by the Wisconsin Center for Space
Automation and Robotics (WCSAR, formed 1987) and the FTI: three successive Mark-series miner
designs (M-1, M-2, M-3, the last completed in 2006), a "spiral mining" tethered-miner concept, and
an in-situ volatile release concept using a heated, gas-tight surface enclosure. Section III
describes three experimental systems built at FTI and NASA KSC Swamp Works: the Solar Wind
Implanter (SWIM, 2015), which implants helium into regolith simulant to mimic solar-wind exposure;
the Helium Extraction & Acquisition Testbed (HEAT), which flows implanted simulant through a heat
pipe heat exchanger (HPHX) to test agitation- and heating-based release; and the Sample
Concentration Analysis (SCAN) system, which measures remaining helium concentration in processed
simulant samples by stepped vacuum heating and mass spectrometry. Section IV briefly discusses
ISRU synergy between 3He collection and water/oxygen extraction for lunar settlements, fuel depots,
and space tourism. Values throughout are drawn from Apollo-era soil sample analyses (mainly Apollo
11 sample 10084/10086), prior WCSAR/FTI design reports referenced as [16] through [24], and the
author's own referenced experimental papers [25] through [29].

### Key findings

**He-3 fusion market statement (the paper's only demand-side content).** On page 2, the paper
states: "In 2021, there are several commercial companies working to develop fusion reactors that
could be fueled with 3He. These companies have secured over a billion dollars to develop their
technology and are planning to bring reactors to market as early as the 2030s." No company names,
individual funding amounts, reactor power ratings, fuel consumption rates, or projected 3He prices
accompany this statement, and no citation of current (non-fusion) commercial uses of He-3, such as
neutron detection or cryogenics, appears anywhere in the paper.

**Regolith 3He grade.** Apollo 11 bulk sample 10084 averaged 11.8 parts per billion (ppb) 3He by
mass, ranging from 9.22 to 17.9 ppb (page 2, citing Hintenberger et al. 1970). Helium-3 nuclei make
up about 0.002% of the solar wind. Solar wind particles arrive at 300 to 900 km/s (0.5 to 4.0
keV/amu) with an average flux of 3x10^10 ion/(cm2-s) and an average speed of about 450 km/s near
Earth. At 1 keV/amu, 3He is implanted about 26 nm deep into ilmenite, with other solar wind
constituents implanted 14 to 32 nm deep; helium is preferentially retained in titanium-dioxide-rich
minerals such as ilmenite, more abundant in lunar mare regions. About 88% of the helium in Apollo
11 sample 10084 was found in particles smaller than 100 micrometers, and concentration did not
appear correlated with depth into the surface.

**Thermal release behavior.** Vacuum-furnace heating experiments on Apollo sample 10084 show about
50% of embedded 3He released by 500 degrees Celsius and 95% released by 800 degrees Celsius (page
2, citing Pepin et al. 1970).

**Mark-III (M-3) miner design point (page 4).** Completed in 2006, the M-3 was designed to collect
33 kg of 3He per year, excavating to 3 meters depth over 1 square kilometer per year with a 4-meter
diameter bucket wheel excavator. The paper states this quantity of 3He would fuel one approximately
400 MW D-3He fusion power plant per year. The 33 kg/yr figure assumes a 10 ppb 3He concentration
and operation during 90% of the lunar daytime (3,942 hours/year). The M-3 is designed to excavate
1,258 tonnes/hour, heat 556 tonnes/hour, move at 23 m/hour, and consume about 350 kW of electrical
power. If undisturbed maria regolith concentration is 20 ppb rather than 10 ppb (citing Schmitt
2005), the paper states collected 3He would be closer to 66 kg/yr.

**Volatile co-production (Table 1, page 4).** For lunar mare regolith heated to 700 degrees Celsius
(after beneficiation to isolate 450 kg of sub-100-micrometer fines), the paper tabulates other
solar-wind volatiles released alongside 3He, at 20 ppb 3He and 1,258 tonnes/hour excavation: H2 at
43 tonnes/yr (6,100 g per tonne of regolith mined, 201 kg per kg of 3He evolved); 4He at 22 tonnes/
yr (3,100 g/tonne, 102 kg/kg-3He); H2O at 23 tonnes/yr (3,300 g/tonne, 109 kg/kg-3He); N2 at 4.0
tonnes/yr (500 g/tonne, 16.5 kg/kg-3He); CH4 at 11 tonnes/yr (1,600 g/tonne, 53 kg/kg-3He); CO at
13.5 tonnes/yr (1,900 g/tonne, 63 kg/kg-3He); and CO2 at 12 tonnes/yr (1,700 g/tonne, 56 kg/kg-3He).
The table's own 3He row did not extract cleanly from the source PDF (the numeric values ran
together with the row label across the OCR text) and is not reported here as a discrete figure; the
33 to 66 kg/yr 3He totals stated in prose elsewhere in the paper are used instead.

**M-3 heating system engineering.** The miner runs at 15 kPa internal pressure (mostly solar-wind
evolved hydrogen). Regolith is beneficiated through sieves, screw conveyors, and a fluidized bed to
isolate sub-100-micrometer particles before heating. The heat pipe heat exchanger (HPHX) uses a
moving-bed, counter-flow, staged design with a solar thermal heat source: a 10-meter diameter solar
collector receives reflected light from a 110-meter diameter heliostat and heats regolith from an
average 30 degrees Celsius inlet temperature up to 700 degrees Celsius. The collector must supply
12.3 MW to heat 157 kg/s of regolith at an assumed 85% energy recovery efficiency; without energy
recovery, the paper states 82 MW would be required. The HPHX contains 21,500 U-shaped heat pipes
(1.5 cm outer diameter, 1-meter condenser and evaporator sections), using water (copper pipes) in
the lowest-temperature stages and mercury, potassium, or sodium (stainless steel pipes) at
progressively higher temperatures; regolith flows downward at an effective 15 cm/s. Preheater and
recuperator sections each have 91 stages of 118 heat pipes, spaced 1.7 cm apart in a 60-degree
triangular pitch. The full HPHX is about 5 m long, 2 m wide, 3 m tall, and an estimated 9 tonnes.
Six staged compressors and three intercoolers store evolved volatiles: water and carbon dioxide as
liquids, other volatiles as gas at 20 MPa, in tanks collected by a separate vehicle for later
gas/isotopic separation.

**Spiral mining and in-situ volatile release concepts (pages 6 to 8).** The spiral mining concept
tethers a mobile miner to a central processing station via a support arm, potentially eliminating
a separate transport vehicle and reducing individual miner mass and complexity; the paper describes
it as conceptual, pending further design of the support arm bearing and gas/power transfer. The
in-situ volatile release concept heats an enclosed area of regolith without excavation. In a domed
enclosure variant, an underground dike holds a 101 kPa hydrogen atmosphere; 50-cm outer diameter
heating elements at 800 degrees Celsius are buried 3 meters deep, reaching an estimated 627 degrees
Celsius in the coldest regolith after 320 hours of lunar-day heating, releasing about 80% of
embedded 3He. Moving the dome after each lunar day (13 times/Earth year) across three 100-meter
diameter enclosures could yield about 30 kg 3He/yr at 10 ppb concentration, comparable to the M-3.
The paper estimates total mining-operation mass at 174 tonnes for this concept (excluding crew
accommodations) with only about 5 tonnes of regolith disturbed, versus an estimated 50-tonne mass
and 5 million tonnes of regolith excavated for a full M-3-based operation.

**Extraction experiments (Section III, pages 8 to 14).** 2018 experiments at FTI with NASA KSC
Swamp Works measured flow-induced agitation release of helium from JSC-1A lunar regolith simulant
in a heat pipe heat exchanger, finding that agitation loss increased with simulant flow rate. The
SWIM system (2015) implants up to 2 kg batches of sub-100-micrometer simulant with helium ions
electrostatically accelerated to solar-wind speeds (300 to 900 km/s, average about 450 km/s) inside
a vacuum chamber, using 4He as a surrogate for 3He due to near-identical diffusion behavior and
easier availability. The HEAT testbed is a 5-stage counter-flow HPHX of U-shaped heat pipes wrapped
over 4 rows of cartridge heaters, instrumented with 14 type-K thermocouples and an infrared
thermopile camera. The SCAN system measures helium concentration using a 100 cm3 crucible, an
8-mm-diameter cartridge heater, and a vacuum system with a Residual Gas Analyzer, heating 20-gram
simulant samples in isochronal steps to 600 degrees Celsius.

**Agitation release results (pages 13 to 14).** Two batches of SWIM-implanted simulant were run
through HEAT at 1.5 g/s and 9.0 g/s. Remaining 4He concentration after implantation was 2.7 ppb
(1.5 g/s batch) and 2.1 ppb (9 g/s batch); after passing through HEAT and correcting for control
concentration, remaining 4He was 0.7 ppb and 0.1 ppb respectively. Normalized against implanted
concentration, this corresponds to 70% of implanted 4He removed at 1.5 g/s and 96% removed at 9
g/s, achieved by agitation alone with no heating above room temperature. The paper notes this
release magnitude corresponds to the level of thermal release seen at about 500 degrees Celsius in
heated Apollo samples, suggesting agitation could substantially reduce, or potentially eliminate,
the heating otherwise required to extract 3He. The paper caveats that SWIM implantation replicates
only part of the solar wind energy spectrum and that JSC-1A simulant cannot fully replicate real
lunar regolith surface structure.

**ISRU synergy (Section IV, page 14).** The paper states that a near-term pilot operation
demonstrating collection of 15 tonnes of water (for liquid oxygen/liquid hydrogen propellant) from
5%-water-rich regolith may require excavating about 400 tonnes of regolith, and that this same
pilot scale could also demonstrate extraction of about 6 grams of 3He, assuming permanently
shadowed region 3He concentration is similar to equatorial regions. It suggests a 3He collection
subsystem (via agitation) could be added to water/oxygen extraction plants, excavators, and
conveyors, allowing passively collected 3He to be separated and stored for eventual transport to
Earth.

### Limitations

This paper carries essentially no He-3 demand or market data. Its only market-facing statement is
the unsourced, non-quantified sentence on page 2 that commercial fusion companies had "secured over
a billion dollars" in 2021 and were targeting reactors "as early as the 2030s." It gives no company
names, no market size or projected fuel-demand figures, no current or historical He-3 price, and no
mention of existing non-fusion He-3 markets (neutron detection, cryogenics, or other current uses),
which the FA7 sub-question specifically seeks. A dedicated He-3 demand/market source is still
needed to answer the FA7 sub-questions on fusion timeline realism and current market size; this
paper cannot substitute for one.

On the supply side, the paper's own figures show internal inconsistency in reported 3He yield: the
same M-3 design and 10 ppb baseline is variously stated to produce "33 kg 3He per year" (page 4)
while a separate passage (page 6) frames the requirement to acquire "33 kg" of 3He as needing
"nearly five million tonnes" of regolith excavated at 10 ppb, a ratio the paper does not reconcile
against the M-3's own stated 1,258 tonnes/hour x 3,942 hours/year excavation rate (about 4.96
million tonnes/year, roughly consistent, derived here). Table 1's own 3He row did not extract
cleanly from the source PDF text and is omitted rather than guessed at. All mining and extraction
concepts described (Mark series, spiral, domed enclosure) are decades-old design studies (1988 to
2006) or the author's own subscale laboratory simulant experiments; none constitute a flown or
field-tested lunar hardware demonstration. The SWIM/HEAT/SCAN experiments used 4He as a surrogate
for 3He and JSC-1A simulant as a surrogate for real lunar regolith, both flagged by the author as
imperfect analogs. No funding source or conflict-of-interest statement was found in the extracted
text.
