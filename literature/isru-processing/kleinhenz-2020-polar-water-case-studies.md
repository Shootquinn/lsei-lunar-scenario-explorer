## Abstract

Documents a system-level model of an end-to-end lunar ISRU architecture that mines polar water ice and converts it into liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen propellant. The architecture places fixed hardware at two sites, a permanently shadowed "mine" site and an illuminated "ridge" propellant-production site, connected by two alternating water-tanker mobility platforms. For a baseline requirement of 10 metric tons (mT) of oxygen, plus hydrogen to support a 6:1 oxygen/fuel mixture ratio, produced over 225 days, the model returns subsystem-level mass and power estimates (no surface power system is included) and reports sensitivity results across four variables: production requirement, regolith water concentration, dry overburden depth, and time between water-tanker transports. Closes with a mass and power comparison against a separately sized carbothermal-reduction oxygen-from-regolith system targeting the same production requirement.

---

### Background and objective

NASA's Artemis plan, as described at the time of writing, targeted a return to the Moon by 2024 and a sustainable surface presence by 2028, with In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) identified as central to sustainability (p. 2). Mission plans targeted the lunar South Pole for its extended periods of solar illumination and the possibility of accessing water ice held in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) near the poles. Electrolyzed water can supply both the fuel and oxidizer needed to refuel a lander or ascent vehicle.

The paper frames its results explicitly as a sensitivity study rather than a decisional design, because both the mission architecture for ISRU infusion and the properties of the polar water deposits were still undefined. The stated objective was to examine how the modeled system responds to key variables and to identify break points where the system becomes untenable, and, separately, to help define the criteria a lunar water deposit would need to meet to constitute a usable ISRU "reserve" (p. 3, citing Ref. 2).

The paper also notes that oxygen bound in silicate minerals is available almost everywhere on the lunar surface (as much as 40 percent of lunar regolith by the paper's figure, p. 3), in contrast to water ice, which is geographically restricted to cold traps. Extracting mineral oxygen is described as a higher-energy process than extracting water. NASA was reported to be carrying both resource options forward pending further information on the water deposits, and the paper closes with a comparison between the two.

### Methods and scope

The model was built in Excel, reusing subsystem and component modules from an earlier Mars ISRU propellant-production case study by the same authors (cited as Ref. 4, AIAA SciTech 2017) with lunar-adjusted inputs. Component modules are described as largely empirical, anchored to performance data from existing or in-house/NASA-solicited technologies rather than optimized for flight (p. 3). The modeled scope is ISRU system hardware only. Surface power generation and communications architectures are explicitly excluded from the mass and power totals; power needs are estimated at each site so that a power architecture can be traded separately. Propellant delivery to a user vehicle after production is not addressed.

The end-to-end architecture comprises three primary systems: a water mining system at the shadowed mine site (excavation and extraction of water from regolith), two alternating water tankers that transport frozen water between sites, and a propellant production system at the illuminated ridge site (water processing, electrolysis, and cryogenic liquefaction of O2 and H2). The ridge site is deliberately not co-located with candidate Human Landing System (HLS) sites, both to avoid interference between ISRU and landing/ascent operations and to keep the ISRU system independent of a specific landing location (p. 6).

Baseline ground rules are given in Table 1 (p. 3): oxygen/hydrogen mixture ratio 6; total production time 225 days, based on a COMPASS-study estimate of continuous polar illumination duration (Ref. 5); commissioning time 48 hours; mass margins of 20 percent growth plus 15 percent structure; power margin of 20 percent growth; water extraction efficiency 75 percent; maximum battery discharge 80 percent; regolith density 1.3 g/cc; PSR minimum/maximum/sink temperatures of 50 K/120 K/85 K; ridge minimum/maximum/sink temperatures of 100 K/300 K/152 K; radiative sky temperature 4 K; a 1.5x traverse multiplier in the PSR for hazard avoidance; excavator recharge time 5 hours; tanker recharge time 10 hours; and battery energy density 140 Wh/kg. All masses and powers reported in the paper include these margins.

Baseline site selection used the LROC Quickmap tool to identify a permanently shadowed crater near the Shackleton-de Gerlache Ridge with favorable ice-stability and maximum-temperature characteristics (Fig. 2, p. 4), positioned near two candidate Artemis HLS sites. Coordinates given in the text: Artemis 001 at 222.69 deg E, -89.45 deg lat; Artemis 004 at 204.27 deg E, -89.78 deg lat; ISRU Ridge 1 at 214.73 deg E, -89.56 deg lat; ISRU Ridge 4 at 197.05 deg E, -89.69 deg lat; and the ISRU PSR mine site at 198.582 deg E, -89.579 deg lat. Shackleton crater, despite having the largest nearby ice-stability region, was rejected as a mine site because of steep slopes (over 30 degrees) and long traverse distances (p. 4).

Traverse elevation and slope profiles from the mine site to both candidate ridge/HLS destinations were generated in Quickmap (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, pp. 4 to 5). The mine-to-extraction-site distance within the mine area is fixed at 100 m, and a modeled 3.5 km straight-line traverse distance becomes 5.2 km once the 1.5x path-finding multiplier is applied (p. 5). Reading Figure 4 (p. 5): elevation rises from about 1,050 to 1,100 m at the mine site to about 1,650 to 1,700 m by roughly 3,000 m of distance, then diverges to about 1,850 m near Site 001 (around 7,000 to 7,500 m) versus leveling near 1,700 to 1,750 m for the Site 004 path past about 4,000 m. The paired slope profile peaks at about 16 to 18 degrees within the first 3,000 m and drops to generally under 8 degrees beyond that; the Ridge 4 and Ridge 1 waypoints are marked at approximately 3,400 m and 3,800 m along the path.

Table 2 (p. 7) lists the technology selected for each subsystem: the excavator is the RASSOR dual-bucket-drum vehicle (Ref. 11); water extraction uses an auger-dryer design sized from a terrestrial screw-conveyor drying model (Ref. 12); the water-tanker mobility platform and tank are fully calculated rather than based on an existing system, using a payload mass ratio of 1.5; fluid transfer uses a Dust Tolerant Automated Umbilical (DTAU, Ref. 14) with a commercial-off-the-shelf pump and motor selected from a flow-rate lookup table; electrolysis uses a liquid-cathode polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) system; gas drying uses regenerative desiccant dryers modeled on in-house JSC breadboard hardware; hydrogen and oxygen liquefaction both use cryocooler models that include radiator mass, with the hydrogen liquefaction model supplied by the NASA cryofluid management group and the radiator temperature set to 300 K as the lowest-mass solution; and storage tanks are sized in thin-wall (3 mm) aluminum. Water cleanup hardware is listed as not yet defined and is not included in the model, because contaminant criteria for the water were still unknown at the time of writing.

A previously traded mobile-processor alternative, a 12.7 cm diameter by 1 m deep heated core drill (Ref. 8), was estimated to require on the order of 20,000 drill holes to meet the 10 mT oxygen requirement at 5 wt% water content over the full drill depth, and did not trade well against a bit lifespan on the order of 100 holes (p. 5).

The concept of operations (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, pp. 6 to 7) has excavators removing dry overburden in a pit-mine approach, delivering water-rich regolith to a fixed water extractor, and returning dried tailings to a tailings site located at half the mine length plus 10 m from the mine. Water vapor from the extractor freezes directly inside a tanker's tank, requiring no active power to maintain state at the cold mine site. At the ridge, the tank thaws passively via solar gain and radiative loss before the liquid water is pumped into the propellant-production system's water tank. Two tankers are required so that one can remain at the mine site collecting water while the other transits to or from the ridge. The number of excavators used is set by an iterative calculation that balances overburden-removal time against on-demand regolith delivery and a fixed 5-hour battery recharge constraint.

### Key findings

**Baseline case (Table 3, Table 4, Fig. 8; pp. 8 to 9).** For the 10 mT O2, 225-day baseline, total production is 13 mT O2 and 1.7 mT H2 from 15 mT of water. At 75 percent extraction efficiency and 5 wt% regolith water concentration, this requires processing 398 mT of regolith, corresponding to a mine footprint of 32 m by 32 m at 30 cm depth, with tanker transport occurring every 10 days (about 20 trips over the mission).

Total system mass is 4.9 mT (given as approximately 5 mT in the paper's own abstract), split among the ridge propellant-production system (2.6 mT), the two water tankers (1.8 mT), and mine-site hardware (0.49 mT). Total power is 68 kW, split between the ridge (46 kW) and the mine/PSR (22 kW). Three subsystems each draw approximately 20 kW: hydrogen liquefaction and electrolysis, both at the ridge, and the water extractor, at the mine.

Reading the log-scale bars in Figure 8 (p. 9) against these totals gives approximate per-subsystem values:
- Water extractor: about 300 kg; about 20 kW at the PSR.
- Excavator(s): about 150 kg; about 70 W.
- O2 liquefaction: about 150 kg; about 700 W at the ridge. O2 tank: about 250 kg.
- H2 liquefaction: about 1,500 to 2,000 kg, the largest single mass item; about 20 kW at the ridge. H2 tank: about 350 kg.
- H2 and O2 dryers: about 50 kg; about 30 W.
- Electrolyzer: about 50 kg; about 20 kW at the ridge.
- Ridge water tank: about 120 kg; about 70 W.
- Tankers (both, combined): about 1,800 kg; about 120 W at the ridge and about 150 W at the PSR for maintenance power.

**Sensitivity trades (Fig. 9, p. 11).** Each of four variables was varied individually against the baseline, with results plotted as mass and power stacked-bar panels.

- Oxygen production requirement (Panel A; 1, 5, 10, 15 mT O2): Total-PSR mass data labels are 263 kg (1 mT, described as "pilot" scale), 326 kg (5 mT), 491 kg (10 mT baseline), and 650 kg (15 mT). Total end-to-end mass runs approximately 800, 2,600, 4,900, and 7,200 kg over the same range; total power runs approximately 5, 33, 68, and 100 kW.
- Regolith bulk water concentration (Panel B; 1, 2, 5, 10 wt%, baseline 5 wt%, consistent with neutron-spectrometer data cited as Ref. 15): Total-PSR mass data labels are 1,725 kg (1 percent), 845 kg (2 percent), 491 kg (5 percent), and 348 kg (10 percent); total end-to-end mass runs approximately 6,900, 5,600, 4,900, and 4,700 kg, with total power approximately 152, 95, 68, and 58 kW. The paper identifies the transition between 1 and 2 percent as the most notable break point, driven by the mine/PSR system, whose plotted power rises to roughly 90 to 95 kW at 1 percent versus about 22 kW at the 5 percent baseline; regolith requirement at 1 percent approaches 2,000 mT, forcing multiple water-extractor units, while above 5 percent the curve levels once the minimum extractor count is reached.
- Dry overburden depth (Panel C; 20, 50, 80, 120 cm, baseline 20 cm): Total-PSR mass data labels are 491 kg (20 cm), 539 kg (50 cm), 630 kg (80 cm), and 747 kg (120 cm); total end-to-end mass runs approximately 4,900, 6,200, 6,500, and 9,200 kg, with power approximately 68, 88, 92, and 140 kW. The text notes that both 20 cm and 50 cm require 2 excavators, so the mass and power differences at those points reflect a shorter available production window at 50 cm; a third excavator is added by 80 cm; and above 1 m the production rate begins to become untenable.
- Days between water transport (Panel D; 2, 10, 20, 30 days, baseline 10 days): Total-PSR mass data labels are nearly flat at 490, 491, 499, and 509 kg, while total end-to-end mass rises approximately 3,200, 4,900, 7,000, and 9,200 kg, driven almost entirely by tanker tank size, and power rises modestly, approximately 66, 68, 72, and 77 kW. Corresponding trip counts are 97 trips at 2 days, about 20 trips at the 10-day baseline, and 6 trips at 30 days. The paper states it could not judge an appropriate traverse count because "no planetary exploration vehicle has ever performed at this level," citing a traverse distance of roughly 5 km each way over slopes around 16 degrees (p. 11).

Two additional factors are discussed qualitatively rather than graphed (p. 11 to 12). Mine depth: a shallower mine trades reduced trenching depth for a larger required surface area, and the model does not capture the added ramp-building time that deeper mines would require during active mining. Mine-to-ridge transport distance: mass and power are described as largely insensitive to distance until travel time begins to intrude on the water-processing schedule, which for the baseline scenario becomes significant around a 50 km one-way distance, that is, a 100 km round trip across 20 trips.

**Water-ice versus oxygen-from-regolith comparison (Section IV.D, p. 12).** A companion Compass-study system (Ref. 3, sized in 2019) using carbothermal reduction of silicate minerals, a three-step process run at 1,650 to 1,800 degC using a recycled methane reductant, was sized to the same 10 mT O2 target over the same 225 days. Its ISRU-only hardware mass is stated as 927 kg, excluding an oxygen tank (assumed reused from the lander) and excluding hydrogen fuel that would have to be transported from Earth. Adding an oxygen tank and 1.67 mT of Earth-supplied hydrogen (converted using water-system tank assumptions to 2.3 mT once its own tank is included), and removing the 498 kg mass of the oxygen system's solar array (since the water-system totals exclude power system mass), yields comparable masses of 2.7 mT for the oxygen-from-regolith system versus 4.9 mT for the water system.

On power, the oxygen-from-regolith system requires 11.8 kW of electrical power plus 33.3 kW of direct solar-thermal heating for the carbothermal reactor, for 45 kW total, versus 68 kW for the water system, which includes about 20 kW of thermal power for water extraction. The paper concludes that the oxygen-from-regolith system trades better in both mass and power at this single-mission scale, but that the water system's relative position would improve over successive missions, since each oxygen-from-regolith mission carries an additional roughly 2 mT of Earth-supplied hydrogen up-mass that the water system avoids.

### Limitations

The paper repeatedly states that its baseline case, technology selections, and baseline site are illustrative rather than decisional (pp. 3, 4, 12), reflecting that the mission architecture, ISRU infusion approach, technology maturity, and site characterization were all still open at the time of writing. Technology selections favored components with empirical or laboratory performance data over optimized designs, so the modeled system is explicitly described as not necessarily the most optimized solution (p. 12).

No power generation architecture is defined anywhere in the model; only the power requirement at each site is estimated, so none of the reported mass totals include a surface power system. Water cleanup hardware is entirely absent from the model because contaminant specifications for the polar water deposits had not been defined. The water extraction subsystem is described as conceptual, with substantial remaining uncertainty in extraction efficiencies and regolith parameters that the paper expects continued technology development to resolve (p. 9).

Wear and mission risk associated with tanker traverse frequency are discussed only qualitatively; the model does not quantify them. Ramp-building time for deeper mines during active operation is not captured. The oxygen-from-regolith comparison is explicitly called preliminary, and the paper states that further work would be needed to confirm equivalent assumptions and assessment methods between the two systems before the comparison could be considered final (p. 12).

Site selection relied on remote-sensing data (LROC Quickmap, the Diviner ice-stability overlay) rather than ground truth; the paper notes that an actual ISRU water reserve cannot be identified until ground-based reconnaissance, citing the then-planned VIPER rover (Ref. 6), becomes available.
