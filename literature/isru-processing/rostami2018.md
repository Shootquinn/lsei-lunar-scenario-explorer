## Abstract

Conceptual discussion paper proposing the adaptation of terrestrial tunnel boring machine (TBM) technology to excavate underground human-habitat space, lava-tube access tunnels, and polar ice-access tunnels on the Moon. Surveys the case for underground lunar construction (thermal stability, radiation shielding, micrometeorite protection), summarizes terrestrial TBM history, classification, and performance envelope, and enumerates lunar-specific design constraints for a "lunar TBM" (LTBM) -- weight, power, cutter abrasivity wear, vacuum operation, muck/coolant fluid substitution, temperature extremes, ground support, air-pressure containment, material transport, utilities, and forward geological detection -- together with candidate mitigations for each. Presents no specific LTBM design, prototype, or quantitative lunar-mission engineering analysis; conclusions are qualitative, drawn from terrestrial TBM industry parameters and cited lunar-science literature.

---

### Background and objective

The paper's premise is that current and future human activity on the Moon would be facilitated by underground tunnels: shielding from radiation, protection from micrometeorites, structures to maintain atmospheric pressure in living quarters, and a means to survive the cold lunar night. It argues that locating a habitat several meters below the surface gives greater protection than a surface habitat covered in regolith, plus a more stable thermal environment. The authors note that post-Apollo lunar base illustrations (Figure 1, a 1969 National Geographic rendering reproduced from McKay, 1992) already depicted large underground openings, and that an early lunar-TBM conceptual design was proposed by Allen (1988, NASA-CR-184746). The stated objective is to reexamine the value of underground access in light of newer discoveries -- large lava tubes and volatiles in permanently shadowed polar craters -- and recent advances in terrestrial TBM technology, and to discuss lunar base design concepts, LTBM design constraints (cutter design, mass, power), and potential solutions, including fabricating heavier LTBM components from lunar resources.

### Methods and scope

The paper is a literature-informed conceptual/discussion treatment rather than an original experimental, computational, or design study. It draws on: established lunar surface-condition literature for temperature, radiation, and regolith geotechnical properties (Vaniman, 1991; Silberberg et al., 1985; Hertzberg, 1992; Blair et al., 2017); terrestrial TBM engineering and industry data, including a TBM ground-condition classification scheme (Rostami, 2016) and a UK utility-installation cost comparison (Hunt et al., 2014); and ground-characterization methods for forward detection (Kahraman et al., 2016). It applies qualitative reasoning to translate terrestrial TBM parameters and known operational challenges into the lunar environment. Three lunar tunneling use cases are addressed: (a) excavating human-habitat tunnels from the surface, (b) boring access into an intact (uncollapsed) section of a lava tube, and (c) boring from a sunlit region into a permanently shadowed polar crater to access ice.

### Key findings

**Case for underground habitation.** Lunar equatorial surface temperature is stated to vary from roughly -150 to +150 degrees C between day and night; a separate passage later in the paper describes "extreme temperatures of roughly +/- 120 degrees C" expected at the surface, without reconciling the two figures. The day/night temperature swing decreases to 3 K at 30 cm depth and there is essentially no day/night variation below 70 cm (citing Vaniman, 1991). Recommended radiation shielding is about 2 m of densely packed regolith, nearly doubled during intense solar flares (citing Silberberg et al., 1985). An LTBM's cutting force is applied through wall grippers rather than machine weight, an advantage over surface cut-and-cover (C&C) excavation given that lunar gravity is only 1/6 of Earth's.

**Lava tubes and polar ice.** Figure 2 shows an example lunar lava tube stated to be 40 km long and 500 m wide, with both collapsed (skylight) and intact sections, citing recent work (Blair, 2017) indicating lunar lava tubes can be very large. The paper argues an LTBM could bore into an intact section, which is more stable and less prone to further collapse than entry through a skylight, potentially reaching a space sealed off from the surface environment for a long time. For polar ice access, permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) reach temperatures as low as 40 K in the deepest craters; the paper proposes tunneling from a sunlit region into a PSR, drawing an analogy to terrestrial water-supply tunnels (New York City Water Tunnels 1, 2, and 3; the Colorado-Big Thompson Project), and suggests transporting ice by vehicle or as water vapor rather than as liquid, to minimize surface disturbance and volatile loss.

**Lunar ground conditions.** The anticipated regolith layer is about 10 m thick, highly compacted within the first 10-20 cm of depth, grading into boulders, fractured rock, and then intact bedrock (basaltic in the Mare, anorthositic in the Highlands). At the poles, frozen regolith combines fine- to coarse-grained rock (including anorthosite boulders) with icy volatiles, forming a permafrost with variable mechanical strength. Until excavation reaches fully competent bedrock, the paper characterizes the cutting environment as "mixed conditions."

**Terrestrial TBM background.** TBMs were first proposed in the mid-19th century for a tunnel under the Thames in London; widespread use began in the 1950s when James Robbins introduced disc cutters on a machine used in Ohio rock tunneling. The 1960s-70s saw expansion of hard-rock TBMs and full-face shield machines for pressurized soft-ground tunneling, with early compressed-air shields succeeded by slurry shields and then earth pressure balance (EPB) shields. Figure 3 classifies TBMs by ground condition into a Rock TBM branch (Open TBM for stable wall/face; Shielded for possibly weak wall, subdividing into Double Shield where the wall can be gripped and Single Shield where the gripper sinks, the latter further split into Open Mode for stable face/low groundwater and Pressurized Face Shields for unstable/running face with high groundwater pressure) and a Soft Ground/Soil Shield branch (Open Face Digger Shield above the water table, EPB, Slurry/Hydro Shield, and Hybrid EPB-Slurry machines).

**TBM size and performance envelope.** Remotely operated microtunneling machines (no man-entry) range from 250 mm to 3 m in diameter, propelled by jacked pipe. Man-entry tunneling starts around 2 m diameter and reaches 14.5 m in rock and 17.6 m in soft ground/soil; plans exist for machines as large as 19 m and one nearly 24 m in diameter. Machines can negotiate curves as tight as 300 m radius, with some designs down to 50 m. Machines operating under groundwater pressure commonly withstand 7-8 bar; the highest reported, on a recent Nevada project, was 17 bar of static pressure in a smaller (5-6 m range) diameter machine.

**Trenching-vs-tunneling economics (Figure 4, from Hunt et al., 2014).** A UK cost comparison of installing utility pipes by open-trench excavation-and-reinstatement (E&R) versus multi-utility tunnel (MUT) shows trenching cost rising steeply and near-linearly with each added pipe and with each additional E&R cycle, starting from near-zero cost, while tunneling carries a higher fixed cost but a much shallower per-pipe slope. The paper states that at 10 or more utility pipes placed -- which scales with the resulting tunnel or trench size and depth -- tunneling becomes lower-cost than trenching. It further notes that tunneling cost per unit length changes little with depth, whereas trenching cost is highly depth-sensitive, and trenching costs rise faster in rock than in soil.

**Weight.** A 2-3 m diameter TBM can weigh over 100 tons including backup systems and trailing gear, primarily because low-cost steel forms the primary support structure and shields, with custom hardened steel alloys used for the gearbox, cutterhead, and disc cutters (Figure 5 shows a 19-inch face/gage disc cutter assembly with a back-loading arrangement, courtesy of Robbins Co. and Palmeri SPA). Proposed mitigations include manufacturing a large fraction of the primary support structure from lunar resources (cast basalt or compressed sintered regolith reinforced with metallic members or basaltic tensile fibers), switching structural elements to carbon fiber composites or lighter alloys (with implications for welding/repair), and using lighter, higher-hardness cutting-tool materials (at increased cost).

**Power.** A typical 2-3 m diameter TBM requires 500-700 kWe of input power, using variable frequency drive (VFD) systems fed by 5-25 kV cables from the portal or shafts, stepped down to 480, 660, or 1000 V for the drive units. The paper states this demand could be met by nuclear or solar power and considers the target within reach, while flagging that such power ratings are uncommon in current space applications.

**Abrasivity.** Lunar regolith and basaltic bedrock are described as highly abrasive due to harder silicate mineralogy and jagged, sharp-edged grains produced by impact fragmentation (rather than the erosional processes that shape terrestrial regolith), implying faster cutting-tool wear and more frequent inspection/replacement of disc cutters.

**Vacuum.** Sealing the tunnel to build up internal pressure is described as beneficial for three reasons: capturing volatiles released during tunneling, later use of the tunnel to contain atmosphere for habitation, and enabling "Earth-normal" TBM methods that simplify LTBM design. The lack of convective heat transfer in vacuum could cause excessive heating of discs, cutting tools, and drive-system components. Maintenance requiring human entry is complicated by the mobility limits of space suits in the confined excavated envelope, which the paper says will place special emphasis on automation and robotics.

**Lack of flushing/cooling fluid.** The Moon's lack of atmosphere and fluids removes the flushing medium normally used for muck transport in smaller drilled holes (e.g., probe holes, roof-bolt holes) and for heat dissipation from motors, VFD drives, pumps, and transformer cooling systems. Fluidizing cuttings in a gas stream is proposed as a substitute for some of these functions.

**Temperature extremes.** Higher surface temperatures require cooling for equipment (and possibly worker) function; low temperatures at ice-bearing locations present permafrost-handling challenges. Temperature extremes dampen with depth, but mined material must still be brought to the surface, which the paper states keeps these extremes a critical operational challenge.

**Ground stability and support.** Standard terrestrial support (rock bolts/dowels, i.e., steel rebar placed in drilled holes and resin/grout-anchored, tensioned with a plate and nut) is discussed alongside proposed lunar analogs: Kevlar or carbon fiber mesh with light polymer- or resin-based sprayed cements, and cast segments from light materials forming airtight rings behind a shielded machine (as in single-shield TBM segmental lining, extruded as the machine advances), potentially 3D-printed from rock powder or regolith.

**Maintaining air pressure.** Anticipated lunar tunnel depths are less than 100 m, likely no more than 10 m, keeping ground pressure low. Shielded machines with airtight tail-shield-installed support are proposed; in regolith or granular material a thin membrane could contain air while loads transfer to surrounding ground support, analogous to terrestrial cementitious grout injection behind the shield, though new cement chemistry compatible with regolith mineral content would need development. Cast segments and 3D-printed sintered regolith segments are noted as further options.

**Material transportation.** Open-mode mucking uses buckets; closed (pressurized-face) mode mixes excavated material with mud, a polymer-based thick fluid, or foam for extraction through the cutting chamber and plenum, after which the carrying medium is separated and recirculated while muck moves out via rail, trackless vehicles, or conveyor to the portal or launch shaft. Airlocks are proposed to transition material between vacuum and atmospheric zones; a gas-supplied fluidized particle flow is also proposed as an alternative muck-transport method.

**Utilities.** Electricity, delivered by insulated high-voltage cable, is identified as the first-priority utility, with cables extended every 300-500 m of tunnel advance; compressed air lines are a second priority (atmosphere supply plus a power source for back-up equipment). Water lines for cooling/dust suppression, essential on Earth, are not considered critical for lunar TBM tunneling unless the muck-transport medium is water-based. Ventilation is proposed via specialized air filtering/cleaning similar to current space-station systems, with heat exchangers substituting for the heat-transfer role ventilation air plays terrestrially.

**Other issues.** Hydraulic oil, common in terrestrial machines, is flagged as relatively heavy and requiring regular testing/maintenance, motivating a shift to mechanical ram systems in place of hydraulic cylinders. Operations are not anticipated to occur under cryogenic or elevated temperatures at this stage, but temperature-sensitive components would need insulated transfer from support vehicles to the assembly site; electronics are considered unlikely to be affected by radiation during in-tunnel operation but may need protection during transit to the site.

**Forward geological detection.** Detecting geological features ahead of the machine is called "a very critical issue" for lunar TBM operation and is explicitly noted as still unsolved even in terrestrial applications. Candidate approaches discussed are ground-penetrating radar (GPR) integrated into machine operation, geophysical/micro-seismic methods using geophones and the cutterhead itself as a wave source to detect hard/soft formations, voids, and joints via pressure-wave velocity contrasts, and smart, instrumented probe drilling ahead of the machine (Kahraman et al., 2016), illustrated in Figure 6 (a Robbins Co. schematic of probe drilling fanned out ahead of a shielded TBM).

**Conclusions.** The paper concludes that underground space is a "logical," "perhaps inevitable" choice for lunar bases, protecting inhabitants and equipment from meteorite impact, radiation, harsh temperatures, storms, and other unknown hazards, while enabling atmospheric-pressure living and mining of target materials. Among excavation methods, it identifies TBMs as the most promising for lunar application, citing their terrestrial track record, high performance, and relative ease of automation and remote operation, and characterizes the paper's content as "initial thoughts" on the necessary components and challenges.

### Limitations

The paper is explicitly framed as an initial conceptual discussion rather than a completed engineering design: it presents no specific LTBM configuration, mass budget, launch/assembly plan, or lunar-mission cost estimate. Several issues are flagged by the authors themselves as unresolved even in terrestrial TBM practice, most notably forward geological detection ahead of the cutterhead. None of the proposed lunar-adapted solutions -- 3D-printed regolith ground support, Kevlar/carbon-fiber sprayed lining, gas-fluidized muck transport, cast-basalt or sintered-regolith structural members -- is backed by experimental, simulation, or prototype data in this paper; each is presented as a possibility to explore. The paper states two different lunar surface temperature-extreme figures in separate passages (-150 to +150 degrees C at the equator versus "+/- 120 degrees C" elsewhere) without reconciling them. The trenching-versus-tunneling cost comparison (Figure 4) is drawn from a terrestrial UK utility-corridor study (Hunt et al., 2014) rather than lunar-specific cost modeling. No numbered tables appear in the paper; quantitative parameters (temperatures, dimensions, weights, power) are stated only in running text, and Figure 4 is the paper's only quantitative plot. The paper does not apply a Technology Readiness Level (TRL) framework or assign TRLs to any proposed LTBM subsystem.

### Topic mapping

The Introduction and "Underground Space for Human Habitat" sections cover the rationale for underground lunar construction (thermal stability, radiation shielding, micrometeorite protection) and introduce the lava-tube and polar-ice-access use cases, including the Figure 2 lava-tube example. The "Lunar Material and Geotechnical Considerations" section covers regolith layering, bedrock composition, and mixed-ground cutting conditions. The "Tunnel Boring Machines (TBM) and Their Application" section covers terrestrial TBM history, the Figure 3 ground-condition classification, the TBM size/performance envelope, and the Figure 4 trenching-versus-tunneling cost comparison. The "Challenges for Development of LTBM" section covers weight, power, abrasivity, vacuum, flushing/cooling, temperature extremes, ground stability and support, air-pressure maintenance, material transportation, utilities, other issues (hydraulics, thermal/radiation handling), and forward geological detection, including the Figure 5 disc-cutter detail and Figure 6 probe-drilling schematic. The "Conclusions" section states the paper's overall feasibility argument for TBM use on the Moon.
