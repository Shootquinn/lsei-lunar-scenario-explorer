## Figure 1 (page 2)

Caption (verbatim): "Figure 1. A large lunar base with underground space from National Geographic, 1969. (McKay, 1992)"

What it depicts: A color artist's illustration/painting of a large lunar surface base with an extensive cutaway view of underground/buried habitat spaces. The rendering shows domed and cylindrical modules partly excavated into and beneath the regolith, with a multi-level cutaway of interior living/working decks, suited astronauts working on the surface in the foreground, surface vehicles and tracks, a communications/antenna structure, a U.S. flag on one module, and Earth visible in the black sky at upper right. Axes/variables/units: None (pictorial illustration; no scale bar or quantitative axes).

Key content/finding carried: Establishes the historical (1969) vision that post-Apollo lunar base concepts already depicted large underground openings for habitation. Supports the paper's premise that underground access on the Moon provides protection from meteor impact, radiation, and gives thermal stability. No quantitative data.

## Figure 2 (page 4)

Caption (verbatim): "Figure 2. A 40 km long and 500 m wide lava tube with collapsed and intact sections. (https://photojournal.jpl.nasa.gov/catalog/PIA14010 retrieved September 13, 2017)"

What it depicts: A grayscale orbital spacecraft image of a lunar surface region (cratered terrain) showing a sinuous rille/lava tube trace running roughly top-to-bottom through the frame. Two white text labels with arrows annotate the feature: "Irregular Depression" (pointing to a dark pit/collapse near the top of the tube) and "Continuous Uncollapsed Segment" (pointing to a continuous linear trace lower in the frame). The image shows a mix of collapsed pit sections and intact (uncollapsed) roof segments of the same tube.

Axes/variables/units: None (annotated orbital photo). Scale is given verbally in the caption: the lava tube is stated to be 40 km long and 500 m wide.

Key content/finding carried: Documents that lunar lava tubes exist with both collapsed sections (skylights/irregular depressions) and long continuous intact/uncollapsed segments. The caption quantifies the example feature: 40 km length and 500 m width. This is the visual basis for the paper's argument that a tunnel boring machine could bore into an intact (more stable) section of a lava tube rather than entering through a collapse/skylight, which is more prone to further collapse.

## Figure 3 (page 5)

Caption (verbatim): "Figure 3. General classification of TBMs for various ground conditions (Rostami, 2016)."

What it depicts: A block/flow classification diagram (decision-tree style) that sorts Tunnel Boring Machines by ground condition, interleaved with small color photographs/cutaway renderings of representative machines. Starting from a root box "TBM Tunneling," the tree branches into two main families:
- "Rock TBMs" branch, which splits into "Stable Wall/Face - Open TBM" and "Possibly Weak Wall - Shielded." The shielded path then leads to "Wall Can be Gripped - Double Shield TBM" and "Weak Wall, Gripper Sinks - Single Shield," which further leads to "Stable Face, Low G-water - Open Mode Single Shield" and "Unstable / Running Face, High G-water Pressure - Pressurized Face Shields."
- "Soft Ground / Soil Shield" branch, which splits into "Open Face, Digger Shield, above GW," "Earth Pressure Balance (EPB)," and "Slurry / Hydro Shield." A "Hybrid EPB - Slurry Machines" box is shown at lower left. Grouping labels on the right/bottom read "Soil Machines Using Scrapers" and "EPB or Slurry Shields with Disc Cutters for Rock Cutting."

Axes/variables/units: None (categorical classification chart). The organizing variables are ground/face stability, groundwater ("G-water") pressure, and rock vs. soil vs. mixed conditions.

Key content/finding carried: Shows that TBM type selection is driven by ground conditions (face stability and groundwater pressure), and that a spectrum exists from open rock machines, through single/double shielded machines, to pressurized-face soft-ground machines (EPB, slurry) and hybrids. Relevant to the paper because lunar "mixed conditions" (frozen regolith, boulders, bedrock) would call for machine types combining disc cutters with shield/pressure capability. No numeric data.

## Figure 4 (page 6)

Caption (verbatim): "Figure 4: From Hunt et al. 2014. 'E&R' = excavation and reinstatement of an open-trench, i.e. trenching. 'MUT' = multi-utility tunnels, i.e. tunnels made by a TBM. At 10 or more utility pipes placed, which scales with the size and depth of the tunnel or trench, tunneling has lower cost relative to trenching."

What it depicts: A line chart (the only quantitative plot in the paper) comparing the cost of installing utilities by open-trenching versus by tunneling (multi-utility tunnel / MUT), as a function of how many utility pipes are placed.

Axes/variables/units:
- Y-axis: "Cost (£k/m)" — cost in thousands of British pounds per meter — ranging 0.00 to 4.50, gridlines every 0.50.
- X-axis: "No. Utility pipes placed" — integer 0 to 15.

Data series (as labeled):
- Trenching / open-trench cost lines (dashed straight lines rising steeply from near the origin, each labeled by the number of excavation-and-reinstatement cycles): "12 E&Rs," "9 E&Rs," "6 E&Rs," "3 E&Rs," and "1 E&R." More E&Rs = steeper line. The 12 E&Rs line reaches the 4.50 ceiling at roughly 4 pipes; 9 E&Rs at roughly 5-6 pipes; 6 E&Rs at roughly 8 pipes; 3 E&Rs reaches about 4.50 near 15 pipes; 1 E&R rises only to about 2.6-2.7 at 15 pipes. All trenching lines originate at essentially zero cost at zero pipes (near-zero fixed cost, high per-pipe slope).
- Tunneling / MUT cost lines (dotted lines with markers, high fixed intercept but shallow slope), per the legend: "Open-cut" (solid line, from 0 at 0 pipes rising to about 1.8 at 15 pipes); "Flush-fitting MUT" (square markers, from about 0.65 at 0 pipes to about 1.35 at 15); "Shallow MUT" (triangle markers, from about 1.7 to about 2.4); "Deep MUT (Soft)" (diamond markers, from about 1.6 to about 2.3); "Deep MUT (Rock)" (circle markers, from about 2.9 to about 3.6). A single point labeled "D" sits near 5 pipes at roughly 1.0 £k/m.

Key quantitative trend/finding: Trenching cost starts near zero but climbs steeply with each added pipe and with each additional excavation-and-reinstatement cycle, whereas tunneling (MUT) carries a high fixed cost but a nearly flat per-pipe slope. The lines therefore cross: for a small number of pipes, trenching is cheaper; beyond a threshold, tunneling is cheaper. The caption states the crossover at roughly 10 or more utility pipes placed (which scales with tunnel/trench size and depth), tunneling becomes lower cost than trenching. The chart shows that the more times a trench must be re-opened (higher E&Rs), the sooner tunneling wins. This is the paper's economic argument (from Hunt et al. 2014) that tunneling/TBM excavation becomes cost-competitive as depth, size, and number of installed services increase.

## Figure 5 (page 8)

Caption (verbatim): "Figure 5. Schematic drawing of Disc cutter parts and picture of a disc cutter (Bottom right), and back loading assembly (top right). (courtesy of Robbins Co and Palmeri SPA)."

What it depicts: A three-part composite figure detailing a TBM disc cutter (the rock-cutting tool):
- Left: A labeled exploded/cutaway engineering schematic titled "Face/Gage Cutter Assembly" for a 19" (19-inch) Face/Gage Cutter. Called-out parts include: Retaining Nut, Shaft Seal Retainer, Seal Set, Bearing Cone, Split Ring, 19" Face/Gage Hub, Bearing Cup, Inner Seal Retainer, Flanged Seal Retainer, Shaft, Inner Seal Retainer, Disc Ring, Crush Spacer, Bearing Cup, Bearing Cone, Seal Set. It shows the internal bearing/seal stack and the mounting of the hardened disc ring on the hub and shaft.
- Top right: A line/CAD schematic (cross-section with hatching) illustrating the back-loading arrangement of the cutter into its saddle/housing (how the cutter is mounted/removed from behind the cutterhead).
- Bottom right: A color photograph of three physical disc cutters mounted in white saddle/housing blocks, showing the actual steel disc cutter rings and hubs.

Axes/variables/units: None (engineering schematic and photo). The one explicit dimension is the "19"" (19-inch) face/gage cutter size labeled on the schematic.

Key content/finding carried: Illustrates that the dominant rock-cutting tool for TBMs is the heavy hardened-steel disc cutter, comprising a disc ring, hub, bearing cones/cups, seals, and shaft, and that cutters can be back-loaded (installed/replaced from behind the cutterhead). Supports the paper's discussion that disc cutters are heavy and made of custom hardened steel (a mass/weight challenge for a lunar TBM), wear quickly in abrasive lunar regolith, and require inspection/replacement, motivating alternative/lighter tooling. The 19-inch cutter size is the specific example shown.

## Figure 6 (page 12)

Caption (verbatim): "Figure 6. Schematic drawing of Probe drilling ahead of Shielded TBM (Source: The Robbins Co)."

What it depicts: A 3D rendering/cutaway of a shielded (cylindrical) tunnel boring machine, with the circular cutterhead (studded with disc cutters) at the front left and the shield body and trailing gantry/back-up extending to the right. Numerous long probe/drill rods are shown radiating forward and outward from around the cutterhead periphery in a fan/cone pattern (rendered in orange/red), representing probe drilling ahead of and around the machine to characterize the ground before excavation. The machine carries a "Robbins" logo.

Axes/variables/units: None (schematic rendering; no scale bar).

Key content/finding carried: Depicts probe/smart drilling deployed ahead of a shielded TBM as a means of ground characterization and detection of geological features (hard/soft formations, voids, joints) ahead of the machine along the tunnel alignment. Ties to the text's point that forward detection of obstacles is a critical, still-unsolved issue for lunar TBM operation, with candidate solutions including ground-penetrating radar (GPR), micro-seismic methods using the cutterhead as a source, and instrumented/smart probe drilling (Kahraman et al. 2016). No numeric data.
