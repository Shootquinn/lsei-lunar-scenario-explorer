## Abstract

Final report of a NASA NIAC Phase I award (grant 80NSSC19K0964) to Colorado School of Mines,
covering three tasks: a solar-system-wide survey of candidate Thermal Mining targets (Section
3), a detailed point-design architecture for extracting water ice from lunar polar permanently
shadowed regions (PSRs) via passive solar thermal mining and converting it to LO2/LH2
propellant (Section 4), and cryogenic-vacuum laboratory testing of surface and subsurface
sublimation from icy regolith simulants (Section 5). The lunar point design is sized to
produce 1,100 metric tons (mT) of propellant per year from 1,600 mT of ice, with a Capture
Tent, cold-trap/ice-hauler, heliostat, and processing-plant architecture whose subsystem
masses, costs, and business-case economics are worked in detail. Laboratory testing across
nearly fifty runs demonstrates that surface ice sublimates rapidly under simulated PSR
conditions, while subsurface ice extraction is slowed by a self-forming desiccated regolith
layer, a phenomenon investigated with granular, "mud pie," and frost-layer icy regolith
simulants and partially mitigated in testing with a perforated conducting rod.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The report documents work performed by CSM under NIAC Phase I grant 80NSSC19K0964, organized
into three tasks: Task 1, a survey of the solar system to identify promising Thermal Mining
targets; Task 2, development of an architecture applying Thermal Mining to lunar polar PSR
water ice; and Task 3, proof-of-concept testing of direct-heat sublimation from icy regolith
simulants. The report frames lunar resource use around a 2016 commercial signal in which
United Launch Alliance (ULA) offered to buy LO2/LH2 propellant on the lunar surface at
$500/kg for 1,100 mT/year, and states the propellant production facility developed here
weighs "a bit over 26 tons," produces 1,100 mT of propellant per year, and can be developed
and deployed to the Moon for around $2.5B (page 13). Section 3 surveys Thermal Mining targets
beyond the Moon, citing Mars (polar and mid-latitude ice), the dwarf planet Ceres (surface ice
releasing an estimated 6 kg/s of water vapor), main-belt asteroids 24 Themis and 65 Cybele,
and icy moons of Jupiter (Ganymede, Callisto, Europa) and Saturn (Titan, Rhea, Iapetus, Dione,
Tethys, Enceladus), plus Phoebe, Kuiper Belt objects, and Pluto, as candidate future targets.

### Methods and scope

Section 4 develops the lunar architecture using the same three-subsystem ice-extraction
approach as the companion journal article (Capture Tent, secondary optics, cold-trap/ice-
hauler assemblies) powered by rim heliostats reflecting sunlight into the PSR, but carries the
analysis to a specific point design with detailed subsystem mass estimates (methodology:
publicly available data on similar terrestrial or space hardware, not bottom-up mass
calculations), mass-based cost-estimating relationships (CERs) for development, production,
and launch, and a multi-scenario business case using Internal Rate of Return (IRR) and
cumulative cash flow. Section 4.5 (Power Subsystem) explicitly states the power subsystem
"was not the focus of this Phase I study" and instead surveys power-delivery options
(reflected sunlight via heliostats, microwave transmission, laser transmission, nuclear
power), citing an external study (Kornuta et al., 2019, the Commercial Lunar Propellant
Architecture study) for headline power figures rather than deriving the report's own point-
design power total. Section 5 used a Block 1 laboratory apparatus (a halogen lamp calibrated
to 1,371 W/m2 solar-equivalent output, a liquid-nitrogen-cooled sample container, and a
vacuum chamber reaching about 1x10-4 Torr) to test icy regolith simulants built from LHS-1
lunar highlands simulant (University of Central Florida Exolith Lab) mixed with water ice in
three configurations: granular (loose ice and regolith grains, analogous to dry sand), "mud
pie" (mechanically compacted regolith and water, analogous to terrestrial permafrost), and
frost layer (thin ice sheet added to a dry regolith surface). A planned Block 2 apparatus,
intended to push the isothermal cold boundary farther from the heat source and add a space-
background thermal boundary, was not fabricated in time for Phase I testing.

### Key findings

**Sublimation energy and power sizing (this report does not state a specific-energy figure).**
Section 4.5 (page 53) states that a separate study, Kornuta et al. (2019, the Commercial
Lunar Propellant Architecture study), "determined that 800kWt is required for heating and
sublimation and that 2.0MWe is needed for the remaining functions"; this report cites those
figures from the other study rather than presenting its own derived power total for its
1,100 mT/1,600 mT point design, and explicitly states the power subsystem was not this
Phase I study's focus. No specific energy figure in MJ/kg or kWh/kg for sublimating lunar
PSR water ice, and no total point-design power requirement (kW or MW) for the report's own
Section 4.7 architecture, is stated anywhere in the document; this is a gap in the source,
not an omission in this summary. The closest quantity the report itself states, in a table
comparing its architecture against Jones et al. (2019) (Table 4.9.5, page 90), is a power-
efficiency metric of 1.8 kW per metric ton of annual propellant production for the Thermal
Mining architecture, against 48 kW/mT/yr for Jones' molten-regolith-electrolysis-based
comparison case; the same table cites reflected-sunlight specific mass of 5.8 kg/kW versus
75 kg/kW assumed by Jones for nuclear power, and 18 kg/kW for nuclear power per a cited
industry seminar (Morrison, 2020, page 89). These are the report's own printed comparison
figures, not a derived sublimation-energy budget, and are reported here as such.

**Point design (Section 4.7, page 60-61).** The point design is sized to an annual propellant
production rate of 1,100 mT (the Scenario 1 commercial case discussed under the business
case), which at a propellant mixture ratio of 5.5:1 requires extraction of 1,600 mT of ice per
year. Table 4.7.1 point-design assumptions: Capture Tent diameter 30 m; tent placements 156
per year; tent dwell time 44 hours; maximum cold-trap dwell time 4 hours; move time 12 hours;
ice mass loss 10%. Table 4.7.2 derived requirements: average ice production per placement
10,256 kg; average ice production per placement per area 14.5 kg/m2; average ice sublimation
per area 16.1 kg/m2; area mined per year 110,270 m2; parcel side length L, 423 m; maximum
delivery distance d_max, 1,432 m. Table 4.7.3 cold-trap design parameters: ice capacity 600
kg; diameter 2.0 m; length 2.5 m; volume 7.85 m3; material aluminum; wall thickness 4 mm.
Three cold-trap/ice-hauler units are used, with two normally attached to the tent collecting
while the third unloads and recharges; ice-hauler speed is 5 km/h; over the 44-hour tent
dwell each hauler makes seven round trips, giving a collection rate of 126 kg/h and average
ice collected of 506 kg per hauler cycle.

**Subsystem mass estimates (Tables 4.8.1-4.8.5, pages 66-70).** Capture tent 2,000 kg
(membrane 800, support structure 600, four skid assemblies 400, miscellaneous 200). Cold
traps (3) 900 kg (300 kg each: aluminum cylinder 250 kg plus 50 kg internal structure).
Ice haulers (3) 1,500 kg (500 kg each). Secondary optics 1,200 kg (elliptical mirror 500,
support structure 600, power and controls 100). General-purpose vehicle (GPV) 1,000 kg.
Purification and electrolysis system 5,000 kg. Liquefaction system 3,000 kg (1,000 kg per
XEUS-derived lander, three landers). Solar energy system (heliostats, three sets of one large
plus one small heliostat) 7,500 kg total (2,500 kg per set: small heliostat 800 kg, large
heliostat 1,700 kg). Power system 4,000 kg (photovoltaic array 1,980, support structure 400,
batteries and heaters 1,000, cabling 100, charging station 100, pointing and controls 140,
margin 380). Communication system 100 kg. Table 4.8.5 total system mass: 26,200 kg.

**Total cost estimates (Sections 4.8.3-4.8.4, pages 71-75).** Development cost factor $50,000/kg
nominal (adjusted per subsystem complexity); production cost factors typically $20,000 to
$30,000/kg with a 0.9 learning-curve exponent applied to multi-unit items. Table 4.8.6 total
development cost: $883M (on a 17,600 kg development-mass basis that excludes items whose
development is shared with another line item, e.g., ice haulers costed at $0 because shared
with the GPV). Table 4.8.7 total production cost: $613,471k (about $613.5M), on the same
17,600 kg basis but multiplied out over actual unit quantities. Launch cost factor $35,000/kg
for a single Vulcan/XEUS launch (4,000 kg to the lunar surface, $140M) and a 10% premium for
a dual/distributed-lift launch (12,000 kg, $308M); Table 4.8.9 total launch cost across nine
launches (three single, three dual, plus three more dual counted in the campaign): $1,062M.
Table 4.8.10 total non-recurring cost (development + production + launch): development $883M,
production $614M, launch $1,062M, total $2,559M, consistent with the executive summary's
statement of "around $2.5B" to develop and deploy the system (page 13). Recurring operations
cost (Table 4.8.11) is estimated at a flat $3,000/kg/year factor applied to the full 26,200
kg system mass, giving $78,600k (about $78.6M) per year. Deployment campaign: nine commercial
launches and six commercial landings, using ULA's Vulcan/XEUS lander (4,000 kg single-launch,
12,000 kg dual-launch/distributed-lift payload capability to the lunar surface), with 18
months from first launch to start of production, and a stated total launch mass margin of
10,800 kg (over 40%) built into the campaign.

**Business case scenarios and results (Sections 4.9.2-4.9.3, Tables 4.9.2-4.9.5, pages
81-90).** Three scenarios are modeled: Scenario 1, commercial demand only, private investment,
total lunar-surface propellant demand 1,100 mT/year at $500/kg; Scenario 2, a public-private
partnership (PPP) modeled on NASA's COTS/CRS/CCDev programs, adding Gateway and lander demand,
total demand 1,158 mT/year; Scenario 3, Scenario 2 plus an ongoing Mars program (hardware and
propellant delivery), total demand 1,882 mT/year. Table 4.9.3 (printed with column headers
reading "Scenario 1," "Scenario 3," and "Scenario 4," apparently a mislabeling of Scenarios 1,
2, and 3 as described in the surrounding prose; both the mislabeled headers and the underlying
figures are reproduced here as printed) gives: development cost $883.0M / $929.6M / $1,435.1M;
production cost $613.5M / $645.8M / $997.0M; transportation (launch) cost $1,062M / $1,062M /
$1,370M; operations cost $78.6M / $82.7M / $127.7M per year; NASA investment $0 / $800M /
$1,200M; propellant price $500/kg in all three; annual revenue $550M / $579M / $971M; IRR
8.84% / 15.8% / 15.4%. Cumulative cash breaks even in year 9-11 across scenarios and all three
end the 10-year operational life "in the black," netting between $2B and $5B. From NASA's
perspective (Scenario 2 and 3 savings, not company revenue), Scenario 2 savings are calculated
at $472.5M/year (propellant cost avoidance $172.5M/year plus reduced Gateway cargo-mission
count savings of $300M/year), with an IRR of 27% and net savings over $4B; Scenario 3 adds
Mars-program savings of $5,185M/year (derived from McVay et al., 2016, hardware/propellant
mass estimates), giving an IRR of 54% and net savings of about $47B. Table 4.9.4 compares this
architecture to Charania and DePasquale (2007): propellant price $500/kg versus their
$35,300/kg (2019-escalated), plant efficiency (kg propellant per kg plant mass) 42.0 versus
2.3. Table 4.9.5 compares to Jones et al. (2019): cislunar propellant price $1,100/kg versus
their $101,000/kg, plant efficiency 62.0 versus 8.4 (the different Thermal Mining efficiency
figure here, 62.0 versus 42.0 above, is attributed in the text to Jones accounting for power-
system mass separately).

**Laboratory test results (Section 5, pages 91-113).** Nearly fifty tests were run in a CSM
medium cryogenic-vacuum chamber. Mud pie simulant (mechanically compacted, water weight
percentages tested up to 12%): under an isothermal boundary condition, no measurable ice
sublimation occurred even after a 20-hour test; under an adiabatic boundary condition, a 12%
water-weight test over 5 hours yielded 1.18 g of ice at a rate of 0.24 g/hr, with only a thin
(about 4 mm) desiccated surface layer. Granular simulant (fine grain, 12% water weight,
adiabatic boundary): overall ice yield 2.05 g at 2.5 hours (0.82 g/hr), 3.25 g at 5.0 hours
(0.65 g/hr), 6.74 g at 20.0 hours (0.34 g/hr), showing sublimation rate declining over time.
At 5.6% water weight, adiabatic, yields were 1.29 g at 5 hours (0.26 g/hr) and 2.70 g at 20
hours (0.14 g/hr). Grain-size comparison at 12% water weight, 5-hour tests: coarse grain 3.11
g (0.62 g/hr), fine grain 3.10 g (0.62 g/hr), multi-grain 3.9 g (0.71 g/hr), with a standard
deviation of 0.21 g against an average yield of 3.37 g, judged small enough that subsequent
tests used fine grain only. A perforated copper conducting rod at 5.6% water weight, adiabatic
boundary, increased yields: 2.19 g at 5 hours (0.43 g/hr), 2.93 g at 10 hours (0.29 g/hr), 4.26
g at 20 hours (0.21 g/hr), described in the conclusions as a two-to-three-times increase in
subsurface ice extraction rate over configurations without a rod. Frost layer tests (10 g of
surface ice deposited over about a 6 cm diameter area): both test runs fully sublimated the
frost layer within the 5-hour test duration at a rate of 5 g/hr, with no measurable downward
ice migration. Ice redistribution data (Table 5.3.6, adiabatic, granular fine, 12% water
weight) show desiccated mass, total ice, sublimated ice, and redistributed ice all growing
with test duration (for example, at 20 hours: desiccated mass 70.4 g, total ice 9.60 g,
sublimated ice 6.74 g, redistributed ice 2.86 g), indicating a portion of subliming ice
refreezes within the sample rather than escaping. Thermal conductivity was not measured
directly for the LHS-1 simulant; the report instead cites a thermal conductivity of about
0.001 W/m2 for dry lunar regolith from Apollo-era measurements (Ulamec, 2010) and computes
bulk icy-regolith conductivity using a two-phase geophysical mixing equation (Tavman, 1996),
finding that even small ice fractions cause a sharp rise in bulk thermal conductivity.
Qualitative cooldown-time comparisons (page 104) placed mud pie as cooling fastest (about 900
seconds to reach test temperature), granular simulant next (about 1,700 seconds), and dry
regolith slowest (about 4,200 seconds to reach 100 K), used as an indirect indicator of
relative thermal conductivity. Subsurface temperature profiles showed a slope change between
160 and 180 K, associated with formation of a harder "ice lens" structural layer within
granular samples, and the report notes that ice sublimates at a fast rate around 180 K
(citing Andreas, 2006) yet that vapor pressure, not temperature alone, appears to limit escape
of sublimated vapor once an overlying desiccated layer has formed.

### Limitations

The power subsystem was explicitly stated as not the focus of Phase I, and the report supplies
no specific-energy or total point-design power figure of its own for the 1,100 mT/1,600 mT
architecture, relying instead on citing an external study's (Kornuta et al., 2019) power
totals for a broadly similar but not identical system. Subsystem masses were estimated from
publicly available data on similar terrestrial or existing space hardware rather than from
detailed engineering mass calculations, a limitation the report states directly. Cost
estimates rest on simple mass-based $/kg CERs rather than bottom-up estimates. The mass basis
underlying the development and production cost tables (17,600 kg) differs from the total
flight system mass (26,200 kg) because certain shared or multiply produced components are
costed once at unit mass in the development table; the report does not walk through this
reconciliation explicitly, and the printed column headers of Table 4.9.3 read "Scenario 1,"
"Scenario 3," and "Scenario 4" rather than 1, 2, and 3 as used in the surrounding prose, an
unreconciled labeling inconsistency reproduced here as printed rather than corrected. The
Block 2 test apparatus, intended to provide more realistic thermal boundary conditions, was
not fabricated in time for any testing during this Phase I period, so all reported sublimation
data come from the acknowledged less-representative Block 1 isothermal or adiabatic boundary
conditions, neither of which the report states matches actual PSR conditions. Most Block 1
tests used small samples (an 8.9 cm diameter, 7.0 cm deep container) with a liquid-nitrogen
cold boundary close to the heat source, which the conclusions state artificially limits
subsurface ice yield compared to the much larger, more distant cold boundary expected on the
Moon. Icy regolith simulants used only water as the volatile species and assumed lunar-
highlands-analogous regolith properties; actual lunar PSR regolith structure, cohesion, and
volatile content remain unconfirmed pending a dedicated resource exploration campaign, which
the report treats throughout as a precondition for validating the point design's 4 wt% (or
similar) ice-concentration assumptions carried over from the broader lunar architecture work.

FA5. Full-report companion to source list entry 2 (Sowers & Dreyer, 2019, New Space journal
article). Sub-Q 3, 4. The source provides the detailed engineering and economic substrate
behind the condensed lunar thermal-mining architecture: point-design sizing (tent placements,
dwell times, ice/propellant mass balance), cold-trap and ice-hauler engineering parameters,
itemized subsystem mass and cost estimates, a multi-scenario business case with IRR and
cumulative-cash results plus NASA savings estimates, comparative figures against two other
published lunar-propellant cost studies, and laboratory proof-of-concept data on sublimation
rates, thermal conductivity, and subsurface ice migration in simulated icy regolith. It also
situates the lunar case within a broader solar-system survey of Thermal Mining targets

which the shorter journal article does not cover.
