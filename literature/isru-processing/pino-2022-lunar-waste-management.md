# Waste Management for Lunar Resources Activities

## Citation

Pino, P., Salmeri, A., Hugo, A., & Hume, S. (2022). Waste management for lunar resources activities: Toward a circular lunar economy. *New Space, 10*(3), 274-283. https://doi.org/10.1089/space.2021.0012

Publisher URL: https://www.liebertpub.com/doi/10.1089/space.2021.0012

## Abstract

A review-and-recommendation article from the Space Generation Advisory Council's Space Exploration Project Group, treating waste management for lunar space-resources utilization (SRU) and a lunar circular economy as under-explored but foundational issues. It surveys state-of-the-art extraction/processing technologies for two resource classes (volatiles/water and regolith/minerals), maps their byproducts, storage needs, and applications, and analyzes scalability and logistics. It closes with technical best-practice tables and three legal recommendations for regulating early lunar activity. The work is a conceptual synthesis of prior literature and policy, presenting no new experimental data; scope is technical, economic, and legal grounds for sustainable SRU waste management.

## Summary

### Background and objective
The article situates space resources utilization (SRU) within active global effort, citing ESA's SRU strategy (aiming at a pilot plant by 2040) and NASA's Artemis program (targeting renewed human lunar presence from 2024), alongside private actors such as ispace, TransAstra, and OffWorld. It argues that although SRU waste management (SRUWM) has been poorly explored because the field is young, lacks detailed regulation and compelling business cases, and has few near-term actors, the groundwork must be laid now. The stated aim is to assess the technical implications of SRUWM based on state-of-the-art Moon-mining systems and to argue that advances in SRUWM are foundational for a Moon circular economy in which virtually no waste is produced or only minimal input resources are needed. The study builds on the technical panel of The Hague Space Resources Working Group.

### Methods and scope
The methodology (Figure 1) begins with a high-level overview of important lunar resources, narrowed for this preliminary effort to two study cases chosen for their first-SRU importance: lunar volatiles and regolith. For each resource, state-of-the-art extraction and processing principles from several proposed technologies are broken into main steps to identify which byproducts or wastes are produced, how, where, and in what amounts, laying ground for reduction, reuse, and recycle analysis, complemented by scalability and logistics considerations. The goal is to identify sustainable waste-management strategies and critical aspects that can inform standards, protocols, policies, and regulations. Terrestrial mining-waste classification (by economic value, and by environmental hazard) is used as a reference point.

### Key findings

**Resources of interest.** Volatiles lower the cost of human and robotic activity; the lunar surface is believed to hold water ice in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) at concentrations up to 30% by mass, with additional vast areas of tiny cold traps. Lunar ice can be electrolyzed into hydrogen and oxygen for LOX/LH2 propellant, and water also serves chemical/biological processes and radiation shielding (among the most effective substances per unit mass). Minerals, most accessibly as regolith (the unconsolidated surface deposit), serve as building material and oxygen source; oxygen makes up 40%-50% by weight of regolith but is chemically bound and requires significant energy (high temperatures) to extract at high yield. Regolith can form berms/barriers directly, be processed into compact components (beams, pillars, shields, bricks, walls, pads), or be mixed with an alkaline solution to trigger polymerization of its silicon and aluminum compounds into a cementitious geopolymer.

**Water/ice extraction techniques.** Traditional terrestrial mining equipment is largely incompatible (mass, gravity dependence). Two NIAC-award approaches are discussed: thermal mining (using reflected sunlight, laser, or microwave energy, optionally with embedded thermal rods, to melt PSR ices then cold-trap the gas as ice for storage; most effective with near-surface ice, requiring roughly 4 wt% or above, though estimates of deposit concentration range widely up to 30 wt%) and beneficiation (processing lunar material to separate ice from minerals/metals, via phase changes needing high power transported to PSRs, or strip mining that transports unwanted silicates with added risk and waste). A newer concept, Aqua Factorem, would use grain-sorting to extract ice at only about 100 watts versus about 800 kW for thermal extraction, with simpler logistics, but remains under study and not immediately viable. Extracted water is purified and can be used as steam/plasma for low-to-medium thrust or electrolyzed for propellant.

**Mineral processing techniques.** Two building-production processes are noted: molten regolith casting and additive manufacturing (AM); AM is favored for higher energy efficiency, lower mass, and shape versatility, bonding layers by sintering regolith or promoting chemical reactions. Sintering methods judged most promising are microwave irradiation and concentrated-sunlight heating; regolith can also be processed into fibers. Geopolymer production requires mixing and contour-crafting-type equipment plus plasticizers; sourcing water/alkalis on the Moon increases chances of secondary wastes and a more complex supply chain. Oxygen extraction always involves reduction of metal oxides; technologies considered include vapor phase pyrolysis, molten regolith electrolysis, solid regolith electrolysis, and ion plasma separation (reviewed by Schlüter and Cowley). The solid regolith electrolysis Cambridge FFC process requires the lowest temperatures (molten salt around 900 deg C, with regolith sintered into electrodes and a graphite electrode) and is presented as the best candidate; molten regolith electrolysis eliminates molten salts and electrode sintering but requires higher temperature (about 1,600 deg C).

**Waste generation and management.** LCROSS 2009 impact-ejecta spectroscopy of the Cabeus crater revealed that PSR bottoms contain many compounds beyond water ice, including hydrogen sulfide (H2S), ammonia (NH3), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and ethylene (C2H4) in varying abundance, which the authors argue should be utilized, stored, or responsibly abandoned sustainably. In regolith-based building, little or no waste arises during manufacturing itself, but preprocessing (beneficiation) may be needed because AM quality depends on powder size distribution, particle shape, and chemical composition, and regolith is highly heterogeneous by location. For oxygen extraction, cited technologies still have very low efficiencies on the order of 35% at small scales, meaning ideally about 650 kg of byproducts per tonne of regolith processed; prior chemical beneficiation could raise efficiency and yield more stable, tunable byproducts (e.g., metal alloys) but generates its own slugs and wastes. Generation of slugs and spent molten salts is flagged as environmentally critical. High-temperature regolith processing liberates volatiles (hydrogen, helium, carbon dioxide) on the order of tens to hundreds of grams per cubic meter of treated regolith, potentially valuable but requiring high-pressure/cooling storage. End-of-life processing plants leave machinery reusable if designed for easy dismantling, retrieval, and reconfiguration. **Scalability and logistics.** The recommended approach to scaling SRU is modularity, allowing incremental capacity addition and iterative improvement. Propellant production is the first area to scale; thermal mining minimizes scaling costs to moving mined volatiles to processing facilities, but storing accompanying volatiles grows harder with scale and equipment permanently inside craters may hinder other activities at end of service. AM scaling is judged relatively simpler and less energy-intensive than oxygen extraction, generating little waste (additive, not subtractive) with mobile, repurposable platforms. Oxygen extraction is hardest to scale, needing larger power facilities and Earth resupply of complex, often expendable parts, plus large-volume volatile storage. Logistics require a growing supply chain of miners, refiners, manufacturers, and customers, with waste handling, transport, storage, and disposal; storage may be easier in permanently/quasi-permanently shadowed cold areas. Export from the Moon to cislunar space is favored because it requires a lower change in velocity than launching from Earth. The authors note that long-term activity at a consistent injection rate of about 60 kg/s (132 pounds/s) could shift the Moon's atmosphere from photoionization-loss-dominated to thermal-escape-dominated, with pollutant gases taking hundreds of years to dissipate versus days under the current near-vacuum.

**Recommendations and best practices.** Investment should favor highly scalable, cost-efficient technologies (examples: storage tech, space nuclear power, in-situ solar power, thermal mining, repurposable tech, electrolysis, modular facilities, closed-loop habitats, recycling). Lunar waste should be classified along three axes: perishability, hazards, and usefulness (economic, scientific, or cultural), with parallels to terrestrial mining-waste categories (topsoil, waste rocks, gangue, tailings).

**Legal recommendations.** Within international space law (the Corpus Iuris Spatialis), the authors note that by 2019 no state objected to the legality of SRU (following 2018 UNCOPUOS agenda adoption), with debate shifting to governance mechanisms; they assume national-level regulation will prevail and offer three recommendations: (1) prompt, detailed, transparent registration of planned and existing lunar activities in coordinated national lunar registers with a mutual-recognition clause to avoid competing claims (guarding against spurious "booking" registrations by requiring realistic, feasible, territorially/temporally bounded activities); (2) a lunar-resource classification system with a standardized waste definition for licensing, invoking the Article IX Outer Space Treaty "due regard" principle to minimize loss of nonrenewable lunar resources; and (3) national licensing procedures with at least three stages (interested area available without harmful interference, demonstrated feasibility, and a Lunar Impact Assessment Plan tested against the classification system).

### Limitations
The authors describe the effort as preliminary and the field as early, restricting the technical analysis to two resource cases (volatiles and regolith). Quantitative claims are drawn from cited prior studies and concepts rather than original experiments, and several technologies (e.g., Aqua Factorem) are noted as still under study and not immediately implementable. Deposit-concentration estimates are acknowledged to be in widespread disagreement, requiring ground truth. Surface characterization data are described as limited. The legal analysis rests on an assumed outcome (that national-level regulation will prevail) and the recommendations are stated to be only a portion of a more comprehensive regulatory approach.
