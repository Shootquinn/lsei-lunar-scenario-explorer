## Abstract

Reports oedometer consolidation tests on Korean Lunar Simulant 1 (KLS-1) mixed with discrete granular water ice at 0, 1, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 wt %.

Samples were compacted while frozen under two overburden regimes (maximum 4.2 kPa and maximum 10.4 kPa) and then thawed to 20 degrees C at atmospheric pressure.

Measures height change, bulk density, porosity and void ratio through three phases: instantaneous frozen compaction under stepped load, long-term frozen compaction, and long-term consolidation after thaw.

Finds water ice content the dominant driver of post-thaw consolidation magnitude, with 20-25 wt % mixes losing roughly half their height on thawing, and derives a saturation point for KLS-1 of 17.5 wt % water ice.

### Background and objective

The paper is motivated by terrestrial permafrost-thaw analogues. Thawing ground ice causes soil consolidation that has damaged buildings, pavements and pipelines on Earth (p. 3, citing Hjort et al. 2018; Nelson et al. 2001; Terzaghi & Peck 1967).

The authors argue an equivalent hazard exists for lunar infrastructure sited near or on water-ice-bearing regolith. The stated trigger mechanism is human activity: landing spacecraft, surface traverse and water-ice extraction can transfer heat into regolith containing discrete ice, causing thawing and consolidation that could tilt or unevenly settle nearby infrastructure (p. 1-2).

The introduction traces the observational basis for lunar polar water ice. Salvail & Fanale (1994) modeled Peary Crater shielded floors below 120 K. Killen et al. (1997) and Vasavada et al. (1999) established water-ice-stable temperature thresholds, first 112-115 K and later below 110 K. Feldman et al. (1998, Lunar Prospector) detected hydrogen signatures in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs).

Colaprete et al. (2010) and Gladstone et al. (2010) reported the LCROSS Cabeus Crater impact result of 5.6 +/- 2.9 wt % water by mass in the ejecta. Li et al. (2018, M3) found surface ice exposures in only about 3.5% of mapped cold traps, with spectral modeling indicating regolith may contain up to 30 wt % water ice in places (p. 2-3).

The paper notes lunar water ice can occur in several textures - discrete ice, ice rinds, ice-cemented regolith, ice breccia (Johnson et al. 2024). Below about 110 K under vacuum, ice forms as porous Amorphous Solid Water (ASW) via vapor deposition, which anneals to crystalline ice under heat or pressure (Ricardo et al. 2023; Chaplin 2019). ASW consolidation behavior is stated as beyond the paper's scope because of the difficulty of preparing large ASW samples (Bar-Nun & Laufer 2003).

The paper distinguishes terrestrial "consolidation" from lunar conditions. On Earth, consolidation is densification from expulsion of liquid water under gradual load (Terzaghi & Peck 1967). At lunar surface pressure, water cannot exist as a liquid, and instead moves directly between solid (ice) and gas (sublimation or deposition).

By this definition the authors state that consolidation in the terrestrial sense cannot occur at lunar surface conditions. The regolith instead compacts while ice remains solid, and only during a thaw or sublimation event does consolidation-like behavior occur (p. 3-4).

Apollo-era precedent is used to motivate the safety question. NASA's Apollo Lunar Landing Strategy (Cheatham 1966) defined the lander tip-over balance point at about 40 degrees from vertical. A 12-degree tilt was later identified as the point beyond which astronauts might not be able to launch themselves off the surface (Mueller 2020).

Table 1 (p. 4) reproduces recorded Apollo lander footpad penetration depths and resulting vehicle tilt across all six crewed landings.

| Mission | Footpad penetration -Y (cm) | Footpad penetration +Y (cm) | Footpad penetration -Z (cm) | Footpad penetration +Z (cm) | Tilt from vertical (deg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apollo 11 | 2.5-7.5 | 2.5-7.5 | 2.5-7.5 | 2.5-7.5 | 4.5 |
| Apollo 12 | 10 | 1-2 | 1-2 | 1-2 | 4 |
| Apollo 14 | 2-4 | 15-20 | 2-4 | 15-20 | 7 |
| Apollo 15 | several centimetres each | - | - | - | 11 |
| Apollo 16 | 8-10 | minimal penetration | minimal penetration | minimal penetration | 2.5 |

Apollo 15 shows the largest recorded tilt (11 degrees) despite penetration described only as "several centimetres each," and Apollo 17 has no recorded penetration or tilt data at all. The stated objective is a first experimental look at KLS-1 compaction and consolidation behavior across varying ice content, tested under Earth atmospheric conditions because the apparatus could not operate at lunar surface conditions (p. 4).

### Methods and scope

**Simulant selection and modeling assumptions.** Korean Lunar Simulant 1 (KLS-1) was selected because lunar water ice is most likely located in polar highland terrain, and KLS-1 was developed to match the mineralogy and particle size distribution of Apollo 14 highland sample 14163 (Byung-Hyun et al. 2017; Ryu et al. 2018).

Stated modeling assumptions were: homogeneous, uncontaminated dry regolith; homogeneous, uncontaminated water ice; ice existing as granular icy regolith with discrete particles distributed through the regolith and sized within the regolith particle size distribution (Johnson et al. 2024; Heiken & McKay 1974); ice melting rather than sublimating when thawed; consolidation of icy regolith following thaw; an initial overburden of 3.4 kg (1.0 kPa) common to all tests; testing at 1 atmosphere; and preparation following Purrington et al. (2022) procedures.

**Test apparatus.** The ASTM D4546-21 oedometer test measured one-dimensional vertical displacement of a cylindrical soil sample under radial constraint (Figure 1, p. 5-6).

The acrylic mold, 20 cm in diameter, had a height-to-diameter ratio of about 0.53-0.67 to limit side-wall friction (Monden 1969; Yao et al. 2023). The mold was insulated to control heat-flow direction and was heated one-dimensionally from a base plate.

Two parallel test rigs ran simultaneously: one at maximum overburden 33.4 kg (10.4 kPa), one at maximum overburden 13.4 kg (4.2 kPa).

**Ice preparation.** Water ice was made from distilled water, one-dimensionally cooled at -15 degrees C in pull-away molds, then transferred to a cool room held below -10 degrees C.

Ice was crushed with a low-power ice crusher, then blended in 100 mL batches limited to 20 seconds per batch to avoid melt-bonding, then hand-sieved through an 850 micron sieve. A 425 micron cut was also produced for one comparison mix, requiring the cool room at -20 degrees C and roughly double the processing time for equal yield.

Hand-sieving worked best below -15 degrees C. Above that threshold, sieved particulates clumped into snowballs, and operators' hand warmth through gloves was sufficient to warm the sieve.

**Regolith-ice mixing.** Regolith was cooled in a sealed 20 kg container for at least 24 hours below -10 degrees C before mixing. Table 2 (p. 9) gives the exact mass recipe per 1000 g of mix.

| Water ice (wt %) | Water ice (g) | Lunar regolith simulant (g) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1000 |
| 1 | 10 | 990 |
| 5 | 50 | 950 |
| 10 | 100 | 900 |
| 15 | 150 | 850 |
| 20 | 200 | 800 |
| 25 | 250 | 750 |

Regolith was placed in the cooled bowl first, then ice added on top and hand-folded through with plastic spatulas until no clumps remained, then folded 10 further times for homogeneity. Mixes above 15 wt % ice tended to clump and required more mixing time.

True water content of each batch was verified by drying a representative sample at 120 degrees C for 22 hours and reweighing.

**Consolidation procedure and instrumentation.** Icy regolith was transferred to a second cool room, held below -5 degrees C, and loaded into the test chamber to a maximum depth of 135 mm in 20 mm lifts. Each lift was flattened with a 2.7 kg (0.8 kPa) weight for 10 seconds, and height was checked at three points 120 degrees apart.

A drain bottle below the chamber captured any liquid water from thaw, sealed against moisture loss. The chamber cap (3.4 kg / 1.0 kPa) plus an LVDT displacement sensor were installed, with thermometer and LVDT logged every 30 seconds.

For the 20 and 25 wt % mixes, the standard 110 mm fill depth and the 50 mm LVDT sensor travel were both insufficient, since thaw travel reached 59-63 mm. This required the fill depth increased to 135 mm and the LVDT tongue re-oriented for extended travel (Figures 5-6, p. 10-11).

During the resulting sensor repositioning the logging interval was temporarily increased to 5 seconds, and the manually observed displacement during the adjustment window (about 0.01-0.03 mm per 5 s) was added back into the total sensor displacement.

Three test stages followed, each analyzed as a separate phase (Figure 9, p. 14):

- Instantaneous compaction of frozen icy regolith as 10 kg increments were added every 30 seconds up to the target overburden.
- Long-term compaction of the still-frozen mix, logged every 5 minutes until the rate fell below 0.04 mm per 30 minutes, temperature held below -5 degrees C throughout.
- After all target load was applied, the chamber was heated to 20 degrees C and held a minimum of 12 hours until the consolidation rate fell below 0.01 mm per hour and all thermometers read above 1 degree C. Final height was verified by three-point manual measurement, and drained water mass was weighed.

**Additional comparison mixes.** Beyond the granular <850 micron series, three further 10 wt % mixes were tested to isolate ice texture and particle-size effects.

Granular ice <425 micron followed the same mixing procedure as the main series.

Frozen-aggregate Sample #1 used warm regolith hand-mixed for 10 minutes with warm 10 wt % water, then frozen at -15 degrees C for 12 hours and re-stirred while frozen.

Frozen-aggregate Sample #2 used regolith pre-cooled to -15 degrees C, with water cooled to 1 degree C poured in at 50 mL/min while continuously hand-stirred to coat particles, then frozen at -15 degrees C for 12 hours and re-stirred.

Both aggregate samples showed visible particle clumping larger than the base KLS-1 grain size after freezing (Figure 8, p. 13), attributed to freeze-bonding between regolith particles, described as an "iceglutinate" per Johnson et al. (2024). The mechanics of that clumping were stated as beyond the paper's scope.

**Calculations.** Porosity (Equation 1) was computed from dry-regolith particle density, an assumed/measured ice particle density of 0.75 g/cm3, and the weight ratios of regolith and ice in the mix - as a solid regolith-plus-ice mixture while frozen, and as regolith alone once thawed. Void ratio (Equation 2) followed from porosity.

KLS-1 saturation water content was derived (Equations 3-5) from a literature void-ratio range of e_min = 0.46 (well-compacted) to e_max = 0.98 (very loose) and specific gravity Gs = 2.94 g/cm3 (Ryu et al. 2018), combined with the study's own measured thawed packed dry-regolith density of 1.81 g/cm3 and corresponding void ratio of 0.63.

### Key findings

**Compaction under increasing frozen load.** Water ice content, ice particle size, and physical texture did not measurably change the frozen-state compaction response to increasing overburden (Tables 3-4, p. 15).

Long-term frozen compaction at 4.2 kPa ranged 0.88-1.54% height reduction for the 5-25 wt % mixes, and was negligible for dry and 1 wt % mixes. Frozen-aggregate Sample #1 showed the largest frozen-state compaction observed, 2.19%.

At 10.4 kPa, long-term frozen compaction increased with ice content across 10-25 wt %, reaching a maximum of 4.63% at 25 wt %. The 10 wt % <425 micron mix behaved similarly to the 5 wt % <850 micron mix.

**Consolidation on thaw - the dominant effect.** Thawing produced by far the largest height change of any test stage, for every sample tested. Long-term post-thaw consolidation, from Tables 3-4, expressed as percent height loss versus the initial sample height:

| Water ice (wt %) | 4.2 kPa, long-term thawed (%) | 10.4 kPa, long-term thawed (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | -0.50 | -0.90 |
| 1 | -0.36 | -2.45 |
| 5 | -1.84 | -3.98 |
| 10 | -7.66 | -10.95 |
| 15 | -9.00 | -25.74 |
| 20 | -45.61 | -50.75 |
| 25 | -53.29 | -55.69 |
| 10 wt %, <425 micron ice | -7.51 | -12.28 |
| 10 wt %, ice-rind Sample #1 | -13.74 | -20.31 |
| 10 wt %, ice-rind Sample #2 | -11.91 | -17.43 |

The 25 wt % mixes lost roughly half their original height on thawing under both overburden regimes, 53.29% at 4.2 kPa and 55.69% at 10.4 kPa. The paper states this as the sample halving in height.

The two frozen-aggregate, ice-rind-morphology mixes (Samples #1 and #2, both nominally 10 wt % water) consolidated up to twice as much as the equivalent 10 wt % granular <850 micron mix. This isolates ice texture, not just ice content, as a secondary but measurable driver of consolidation magnitude.

**Density.** Initial density fell with increasing ice fraction, since a dry mix is denser than an ice-diluted mix. At 1.0 kPa, density ran from 1.80 g/cm3 (0 wt %) down to 0.97 g/cm3 (25 wt %) in the 4.2 kPa series, and from 1.80 to 0.98 g/cm3 in the 10.4 kPa series (Tables 5-6, p. 17).

Reducing ice particle size from <850 to <425 micron at fixed 10 wt % reduced density by 0.05 g/cm3. Ice-rind Samples #1 and #2 had bulk densities comparable to 15-20 wt % granular mixes.

Density was essentially static while frozen under sustained load: less than 0.03 g/cm3 change at 4.2 kPa, and less than 0.05 g/cm3 at 10.4 kPa, over the long-term frozen hold.

After thaw, mixes with 0-15 wt % initial ice content showed a density increase proportionate to ice content, up to a maximum increase of 0.42 g/cm3. Mixes with 20-25 wt % initial ice content showed a much larger, near-doubling density increase relative to their own initial mix density, ending up denser after thaw than the 0 wt % (dry) samples.

The 25 wt % mixes illustrate this reversal directly: post-thaw density reached 1.93 g/cm3 (4.2 kPa) and 2.00 g/cm3 (10.4 kPa), versus 1.81-1.82 g/cm3 for the dry sample under the same conditions.

**Porosity.** Porosity increased with ice content while frozen (Tables 7-8, p. 18-19), from 0.38 (0 wt %) to 0.59 (25 wt %) at both overburden levels.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.38 | 0.38 | 0.38 | 0.38 | 0.37 | 0.37 |
| 1 | 0.42 | 0.42 | 0.42 | 0.42 | 0.40 | 0.41 |

| 10 | 0.50 | 0.49 | 0.49 | 0.49 | 0.48 | 0.47 |
| 15 | 0.53 | 0.52 | 0.54 | 0.53 | 0.51 | 0.44 |
| 20 | 0.57 | 0.56 | 0.34 | 0.57 | 0.55 | 0.33 |
| 25 | 0.59 | 0.58 | 0.33 | 0.59 | 0.57 | 0.31 |
| 10 wt %, <425 micron | 0.52 | 0.51 | 0.52 | 0.51 | 0.50 | 0.48 |
| 10 wt %, Sample #1 | 0.57 | 0.56 | 0.54 | 0.58 | 0.56 | 0.51 |
| 10 wt %, Sample #2 | 0.55 | 0.55 | 0.53 | 0.55 | 0.54 | 0.50 |

The 10 wt % <425 micron mix ran 2-3% higher porosity than the equivalent <850 micron mix. The ice-rind Samples #1/#2 ran 6-9% higher porosity than the granular 10 wt % mix while frozen, and 3-5% higher after thaw.

Frozen-state porosity varied less than 2% under long-term load, regardless of ice content or texture.

After thaw, porosity behavior split by ice content. The 1-15 wt % mixes at 4.2 kPa, and the 1-5 wt % mixes at 10.4 kPa, showed increasing relative porosity with initial ice content. Higher-content mixes - 20-25 wt % at both loads, plus the ice-rind samples - showed porosity decreasing after thaw instead: by 22-26% for the 20-25 wt % mixes regardless of overburden, and by 6% for the 15 wt % mix at 10.4 kPa.

Post-thaw, the 20-25 wt % mixes had the lowest porosity of any sample tested. The 25 wt % mix, for example, fell from 0.59 while frozen (long-term) to 0.31-0.33 after thaw.

**Void space ratio.** Void ratio (Tables 9-10, Equation 2) tracked porosity accordingly, with the 20-25 wt % mixes showing the sharpest post-thaw drop.

At 4.2 kPa, the 25 wt % mix fell from e = 1.44 (long-term frozen) to e = 0.50 (long-term thawed). At 10.4 kPa, the 25 wt % mix fell from e = 1.41 (long-term frozen) to e = 0.45 (long-term thawed). The 20 wt % mixes showed the same reversal pattern, though less extreme: e = 1.31 to e = 0.53 (4.2 kPa) and e = 1.33 to e = 0.49 (10.4 kPa).

Lower ice-content mixes (0-15 wt %) did not show this reversal; their void ratios remained roughly flat or increased slightly through the thaw stage, consistent with the porosity behavior described above.

**Saturation point.** Using the derived void ratio (e = 0.63) and specific gravity (Gs = 2.94 g/cm3) for KLS-1's thawed packed dry density of 1.81 g/cm3, the calculated water content at saturation is 21.3% w/w, weight of water per weight of dry regolith. This converts (Equation 5) to a saturation point of 17.5 wt % water ice in the mix.

**Drainage above saturation.** Because the derived KLS-1 saturation point, 17.5 wt %, sits between the 15 and 20 wt % test points, samples at and above 20 wt % produced excess free water at the chamber base during thaw (Table 11, p. 21).

The authors suggest migrating excess water may have improved packing efficiency and contributed to the elevated post-thaw bulk density seen in the 20-25 wt % samples.

**Lander tilt implications.** Using the Apollo geometry - the 40-degree tip-over point, the 12-degree astronaut-egress limit, and footpad spacing from Williams (2022), Figure 11 - the paper calculates that a compaction difference of 0.12 m between adjacent footpads, or 0.16 m between opposing footpads, produces 1 degree of lander tilt.

The Apollo 15 lander, fitted with an extended engine bell, made hard contact with the surface during landing, resulting in slight bell damage (NASA 1972a). The paper cites this as a precedent for undercarriage-clearance sensitivity to compaction.

**Infrastructure code threshold.** Using the New York City Construction Code 2022 allowable uneven-settlement limit of 25.4 mm (1 inch) as a stand-in civil standard, the paper computes the depth of icy regolith that must thaw to accumulate that much settlement, as a function of ice content (Figure 12, p. 24).

Stated results: for 10-25 wt % ice content, 25 to 5 cm of regolith-ice mix, respectively, must thaw to exceed the code limit. At the LCROSS-detected concentration of about 5 wt %, thawing of granular icy regolith to a depth less than 70 cm can exceed the code limit. At 1 wt %, a minimum depth of less than 110 cm must thaw to exceed 25 mm of settlement.

The vertical axis is broken at the top: ticks run 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 140 at even 20 cm spacing, and a final tick reading 705 sits above the 140 tick at roughly half the normal interval. Bar values read against the gridlines, to about 1 cm:

|---|---|---|
| 1 | 705 (bar drawn to the broken axis top and labelled at that tick) | 104 |
| 5 | 137 | 65 |

The 1 wt % 4.2 kPa bar cannot be read against the axis because the axis is broken there; its value is taken from the 705 tick label the bar terminates on.

The prose thresholds correspond to the 10.4 kPa series. The "less than 70 cm" at about 5 wt % matches the 65 cm bar and the "less than 110 cm" at 1 wt % matches the 104 cm bar. The 4.2 kPa series at the same two ice contents is 137 cm and 705 cm, and neither figure appears in the surrounding prose.

The two overburden series converge as ice content rises rather than tracking each other or diverging. The 4.2 kPa value is about seven times the 10.4 kPa value at 1 wt %, about twice at 5 wt %, about 1.4 times at 10 wt %, about 2.8 times at 15 wt %, and equal within reading precision at 20 and 25 wt %. The convergence is not monotonic, since the 15 wt % pair separates more than the 10 wt % pair.

The saturation point of 17.5 wt % that the paper derives for KLS-1 (p. 20) falls between the 15 and 20 wt % categories, and that is the step across which the chart's behaviour changes. The 4.2 kPa series falls from 27 cm to 5 cm and the 10.4 kPa series from 10 cm to 5 cm across that step, the two series become indistinguishable, and both flatten across the following 20 to 25 wt % step. The paper states separately (p. 21) that samples above 20 wt % discharged excess water from the base of the sample during thawing because they became supersaturated.

### Limitations

The authors describe the results as a preliminary, first-look benchmark rather than a design-grade dataset.

Time constraints limited both the number of tests and each test's duration, restricting observation of longer-term, time-dependent consolidation behavior.

Tests were run in Earth gravity and at 1 atmosphere, so lunar vacuum and 1/6 g effects on the compaction and consolidation mechanism are untested.

The study modeled ice melting to liquid water, whereas in lunar vacuum conditions ice more likely sublimates directly to gas. The authors state the packing-efficiency effect of gas-phase versus liquid-phase water venting through the regolith is unknown and could change consolidation behavior.

Moonquakes and weathering-driven differences between KLS-1 and true lunar regolith particulates were not tested.

Amorphous Solid Water (ASW) ice texture, considered the low-temperature vacuum-deposited polymorph most representative of some in-situ lunar ice, was excluded because of sample-preparation difficulty.

The freeze-bonding and clumping mechanism observed in the ice-rind aggregate samples was noted but its consolidation behavior was not characterized, stated as beyond scope.

No ground-truth data exists on how water ice actually occurs - texture, distribution, depth - in real lunar regolith, so the paper's simulant-based results are explicitly flagged as requiring validation against future in-situ measurements.

### Topic mapping
