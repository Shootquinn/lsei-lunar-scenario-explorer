## Abstract

Reports the experimental campaign of the EU funded LUWEX project (Validation of Lunar Water Extraction and Purification Technologies for In Situ Propellant and Consumables Production), demonstrating a fully integrated thermal water extraction and capturing system under simulated lunar polar conditions. A crucible based heating system with mechanical stirring sublimated water from icy regolith and icy glass bead simulants (up to 13 kg per run, 5 wt% ice) inside a dusty thermal vacuum chamber (DTVAC); vapor was deposited on a liquid nitrogen cooled cold trap before liquefaction. Four primary test campaigns varied simulant type, stirring, and methanol contamination. States water recovery of 50 to 73%, capture percentages up to 89%, and recovery energy efficiencies from 22.88 to 66.33 g/kWh, while identifying dust mobilization and volatile contamination as unresolved operational challenges.

### Background and objective

The paper frames water as a multifunctional ISRU resource for life support, plant cultivation, and propellant/energy storage via electrolysis (p. 6368).

It surveys prior lunar water detections: neutron spectroscopy indicating polar hydrogen enrichment consistent with water equivalent hydrogen up to 0 to 0.5 wt% near the surface (Elphic et al., 2007; Feldman et al., 1998; Sanin et al., 2017); near infrared and ultraviolet reflectance evidence of surficial ice or frost in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) (Gladstone et al., 2012; Hayne et al., 2015; Li et al., 2018); and the LCROSS impact experiment at Cabeus crater, which detected 5.6 plus or minus 2.9 wt% H2O in the ejecta plume alongside other volatiles (Colaprete et al., 2010; Gladstone et al., 2010) (p. 6369). The source states that sunlit OH/H2O is adsorbed at concentrations of a few hundred ppm, whereas cold trapped ice in PSRs may locally reach tens of weight percent (p. 6369).

The source states that most published lunar water ISRU studies to date have tested individual process steps, such as extraction or capture in isolation, at gram to sub kilogram scales, and that fully integrated, pilot scale demonstrations combining extraction, vapor transport, and capture under realistic thermal vacuum conditions remain scarce (Kiewiet et al., 2025) (p. 6369).

The LUWEX project was launched to address this gap, encompassing three subsystems: thermal extraction, water capture, and downstream water purification including quality monitoring. This paper covers only the thermal extraction and capture subsystems; downstream purification and water quality characterization are reported separately in Boscheri et al. (2025) (p. 6369). The stated overarching objective is to raise the technology readiness level (TRL) of these technologies to TRL 4, and the source notes that although formally targeting TRL 4, testing used integrated hardware in a high fidelity environment approaching TRL 5 conditions (p. 6369).

Stated objectives of the experimental campaign: demonstrate feasibility of a fully integrated thermal extraction and capture process at a larger scale than prior studies (up to 13 kg of simulant per run); assess the effect of mechanical stirring on water release; evaluate system sensitivity to contaminants; attempt an initial purification step by selectively cold trapping water ice; and measure water recovery efficiencies to inform future mission designs (p. 6369).

### Methods and scope

Experiments were conducted in a Dusty Thermal Vacuum Chamber (DTVAC) known as the L-Chamber (Kreuzig et al., 2021), an approximately 1 m by 1 m by 2 m facility originally designed for cometary physics experiments and adapted to simulate lunar PSR conditions (p. 6369).

Table 1 (DTVAC parameters and instrumentation, p. 6370) gives: inside dimensions 1530 by 700 by 700 mm3; a liquid nitrogen (LN2) cooled pillow plate cold shroud covering the top, sides, and bottom plate; LN2 coolant temperature approximately minus 170 C; LUWEX system operating pressure approximately 10^-3 mbar; surrounding chamber pressure approximately 10^-5 mbar; a turbomolecular main pump; and a dry multi stage roots backing pump. During operation, LUWEX internal pressures were maintained between 10^-3 and 10^-1 mbar to permit ice sublimation, while the surrounding chamber reached approximately 10^-5 mbar (p. 6369-6370).

Two icy simulant types were used. Icy regolith simulant, supplied by Lunex GmbH (Berlin), is a mixture of 75% anorthosite (TUBS-T) and 25% basalt (LX-M), with a granular, highly angular particle form, bulk density 1.24 g/cm3, and particle size distribution below 0.01 to 1 mm (p. 6370).

Its oxide composition (Table 2, p. 6371) is SiO2 48.7 wt%, Al2O3 26.1 wt%, CaO 13.0 wt%, FeO 3.3 wt%, Na2O 3.2 wt%, MgO 2.6 wt%, K2O 0.6 wt%, TiO2 0.7 wt%, MnO 0.1 wt%, P2O5 0.1 wt%, and Cr2O3 less than 0.1 wt%. Its mineral composition (Table 3, p. 6371) is dominated by plagioclase feldspar, 72.75 vol% bytownite and 9.6 vol% labradorite, with pyroxene (augite) 10.48 vol%, olivine (forsterite) 4.6 vol%, titanomagnetite 0.25 vol%, and alkali feldspar 0.08 vol%.

Icy glass beads (approximately 1 mm diameter, primarily silica, Sigmund Lindner GmbH, 2018) were used in early tests to eliminate dust mobilization issues and simplify interpretation of heating, transport, and capture behavior (p. 6371).

Micrometer scale ice particles were produced by a piezoelectric nozzle atomizing distilled water into droplets that froze while falling through a liquid nitrogen cooled channel (Kreuzig et al., 2023), yielding spherical particles with a mean radius of 2.4 plus or minus 0.1 micrometers, collected in liquid nitrogen and vacuum dried (p. 6370). The baseline ice content in the simulant was 5 wt%, chosen to follow LCROSS impact plume results (p. 6370).

In selected experiments approximately 1 wt% methanol (relative to ice mass) was added to the water before ice production, representing the methanol concentration detected in the LCROSS plume, given as up to 0.67 mol% relative to water vapor or approximately 11.9 g/L based on updated post mission analyses (p. 6370-6371). The source states methanol was chosen because its phase diagram closely resembles water's, making it the most challenging contaminant to separate during cold trap capturing, and thus the best available proxy for volatile sensitivity (Holquist et al., 2020) (p. 6371).

The Water Extraction and Capturing System (WECS) comprises the Water Extraction Subsystem (WES) and the Water Capturing and Liquefaction Subsystem (WCS) (p. 6371-6372). The WES centers on a cylindrical stainless steel crucible with 8 internal cartridge heaters and externally wrapped heating wire, heated to approximately 60 C, plus a mechanical stirring mechanism on which the cartridge heaters are mounted (design informed by Purrington et al., 2023 for stirring, and Reiss et al., 2019 for wall heating) (p. 6372).

The WCS centers on two conical copper cold fingers cooled internally with liquid nitrogen to approximately minus 170 C as primary vapor deposition surfaces, with surrounding surfaces heated to 0 to 20 C to prevent unintended frost, a design informed by Holquist et al. (2021); ice is periodically delaminated by briefly heating the cold fingers, and a pneumatic slider valve isolates the liquefaction chamber for melting (p. 6372).

Table 5 (sensors and measurement equipment, p. 6379) specifies instrumentation across the system. Temperature was measured by 40 Pt 1000 RTDs across all subsystems, accuracy plus or minus (0.1 plus 0.0017 times absolute value of temperature in C), and 4 Pt 100 RTDs on cartridge heater sensors, accuracy plus or minus (0.3 plus 0.005 times absolute value of temperature in C).

Pressure was measured by a Pfeiffer PKR 251 high vacuum gauge, accuracy plus or minus 30%, range 10^-8 to 10^-2 mbar, and a Pfeiffer TPR 280 low vacuum gauge, accuracy plus or minus 15%, range 10^-3 to 10^2 mbar. Mass was measured by a Kern 572-33 precision balance, accuracy plus or minus 0.01 g, for water, and two Ranger 300 R31P15 balances, accuracy plus or minus 0.5 g, for simulant and residual material.

Imaging used a Daheng MER-1520-7GC camera at 4608 by 3288 pixels logged every 15 seconds. Control and data acquisition ran through LabVIEW with National Instruments NI-9425, NI-9375, NI-9226, and NI-9220 modules sampling at 1 Hz.

Sample preparation involved oven drying material 12 h at 110 C following NASA (2021) dust exposure test practices, cryogenic storage in liquid nitrogen for at least 12 h, and manual mixing of ice particles into the precooled simulant to 5 wt% inside a cooled enclosure, with homogeneity checked by drying and weighing subsamples (p. 6374-6375). The crucible was filled through a dedicated tube under an argon purge, then evacuated to roughly 10^-2 to 10^-3 mbar before extraction (p. 6375).

A typical run followed an extraction, capturing, delamination (ECD) sequence: cartridge heaters cycled on a duty cycle (for example 10 s on, 50 s off) sublimated ice while vapor deposited on the cold fingers; extraction stopped once vapor pressure stabilized or the cold fingers appeared full; delamination then released accumulated ice into the liquefaction chamber; refilling with fresh icy simulant on top of desiccated material began a new test number, while continued ECD cycling without refill was logged as an additional ECD run (p. 6375-6377). At the end of each experiment the crucible was emptied, weighed, oven dried, and reweighed to determine residual water content (p. 6377-6378).

Fourteen performance metrics were defined by equations 1 through 14 (pp. 6378-6379), derived from the authors' prior review (Kiewiet et al., 2025): total active time, total energy used, total extraction time, WES energy used, recovery percentage, recovery rate, residual ice mass, extracted mass, extraction percentage, extraction rate, capturing percentage, loss mass, extraction energy efficiency, and recovery energy efficiency.

Recovery percentage is recovered water mass divided by initial ice mass; extraction percentage is extracted water mass divided by initial ice mass; capturing percentage is recovered mass divided by extracted mass; loss mass is initial mass minus residual mass minus recovered mass; extraction energy efficiency is extracted mass divided by WES energy; recovery energy efficiency is recovered mass divided by total energy used (p. 6378-6379).

The source notes that the residual ice mass after the experiment was not measured for Experiment 1, so the extraction related metrics (extracted water mass, extraction percentage, extraction rate, capture percentage, loss mass, and extraction energy efficiency) could not be calculated for that run; only recovery percentage, recovery rate, and recovery energy efficiency are reported for Experiment 1 (p. 6382).

Experiment 1 served as an initial proof of concept with glass beads, primarily to gain operational experience rather than to control ice fraction precisely (p. 6381).

Experiment 2, using glass beads with a consistently maintained 5% ice fraction and five extraction cycles across three refills up to 13 kg total, gave the highest recovery rate among the four runs (7.76 g/h) and the highest recovery energy efficiency of the calculable runs (66.33 g/kWh), with a small residual ice mass indicating high extraction percentage (92%) (p. 6381-6382).

Experiment 3, in which stirring was withheld during the first half of each cycle and approximately 1% methanol was added, showed a recovery percentage roughly 10 percentage points lower and a lower recovery rate than Experiment 2, alongside a capturing percentage (54%) that the source attributes to methanol inhibiting the cold trap's ability to deposit water vapor (p. 6382).

Experiment 4, using regolith simulant loaded as a single 13 kg fill without stirring, gave the highest recovery percentage (73%) and highest capture percentage (89%) of all runs, but the lowest recovery rate (2.06 g/h) and lowest recovery energy efficiency (22.88 g/kWh), which the source attributes to slower processing keeping the system operational, and consuming energy, for longer (p. 6382).

Qualitative observations from camera monitoring showed ice deposition on the cold fingers being tracked for accretion rate analysis (Fig. 11 shows ice visibly accreting sideways on the conical cold fingers of Experiment 2.0 at t = 0, 2, and 4 h, p. 6383).

Delamination was often incomplete, with ice undergoing partial sublimation and detaching in fragmented pieces rather than releasing cleanly. Sublimated vapor unintentionally redeposited inside the liquefaction chamber, acting as an unplanned secondary cold trap that complicated final water accounting (p. 6382-6383).

In Experiment 3, a liquid, most likely pure methanol, was observed visibly condensing on the cold finger surfaces (Fig. 12), a phenomenon not seen in methanol free experiments, and one the authors state was unanticipated since methanol was expected to remain gaseous under the design temperature and pressure (p. 6383-6384).

Figure 13 (phase diagram, p. 6384), compiled from Ambrose and Walton (1989), Linstrom (1997), Murphy and Koop (2005), and Wagner and Pruss (2002), plots temperature (100 to 400 K) against pressure (10^-6 to 10^3 mbar, log scale) with water and methanol phase boundaries. The water triple point sits at approximately 273 K and 6 mbar, the methanol triple point at approximately 175 K and 2 times 10^-3 mbar, and the observed liquid methanol condition is marked at roughly 170 to 195 K and 2 to 3 times 10^-2 mbar. An "ideal capturing zone" lies between the water solid vapor boundary and the methanol vapor boundary, with an ideal capturing point (solid H2O, vapor CH3OH) at approximately 205 K and 1.5 times 10^-2 mbar; the source states these pressures relate to LUWEX internal process volumes, not lunar surface pressure (p. 6384).

In Experiment 3, stirring was activated after several hours once power consumption and pressure had settled to steady values. The source reports a small, modest increase in heater power following stirring onset, but a pressure rise of nearly an order of magnitude within minutes, visible in Figure 14 (stirring impact across temperature, power, and pressure for Test 3.1, Extraction Phase 1/1, glass beads with 1% CH3OH, 6000 g sample, 316 g ice, 5.00% ice, p. 6385).

The stirring start (approximately 09:00 to 10:00 on 11 October 2024 in the plotted run) shows temperature rising from a near 0 C plateau back toward 50 to 60 C, total heater power rising modestly from a decayed baseline of roughly 50 to 80 W, and cold trap pressure spiking sharply toward approximately 0.8 mbar. Despite this pressure spike, no corresponding increase in ice accretion thickness on the cold trap was detected, which the source interprets as evidence that the capturing subsystem was already operating near its maximum effective rate before stirring, so additional vapor released by stirring could not be deposited and was instead lost to pumping, supporting the cold trap as a bottleneck in the current configuration (p. 6384-6385).

Dust mobilization was reported as more severe than anticipated: circulating dust obscured camera views (Fig. 15, regolith dust accumulated in the cold trap viewing port during Experiment 4.0, p. 6385) and interfered with the pneumatic slider valve seal in the capturing subsystem, preventing proper sealing and allowing water vapor to leak during liquefaction. The team's workaround was to perform multiple extraction capture cycles before a single final liquefaction step per run (p. 6385).

Aggregated across campaigns, the source states total water recovery ranged 50 to 70%, peaking near 73%, with capture efficiencies approaching 90% in the best runs, recovery energy efficiency up to 66.33 g/kWh for icy glass bead simulants while stirring, and 22.88 g/kWh for icy regolith simulant without stirring (p. 6387). Demonstrated recovery rates of 2.06 to 7.76 g/h correspond to an equivalent continuous production of approximately 0.05 to 0.19 kg/day (p. 6386-6387).

In a mission scenario discussion (Section 5.4, p. 6386-6387), the source compares this to ISS crew water use of about 1 gallon (3.8 kg) per crew member per day (Ewert et al., 2022) and a 98% water recovery rate for current ISS water processing (Schneider and Shull, 2017), implying a make up demand of 0.08 kg per crew day if a comparable closed loop system were available. Under simplifying assumptions that excavation, transport, downstream purification, and electrolysis are not rate limiting, the best demonstrated LUWEX throughput would cover make up water for 2 to 3 crew members, or about 5% of daily per person demand without recycling.

For propellant production, the source notes electrolysis conserves mass (1 kg H2O yields 1 kg of O2 plus H2, neglecting processing losses) and that an Apollo like lunar ascent to a 110 km orbit required on the order of 2377 kg of propellant (Orloff and Garber, 2000), implying 35 LUWEX units operating continuously for one year at the best demonstrated recovery rate. Using a fuel rich oxygen to hydrogen mixture ratio of about 6, hydrogen becomes limiting, requiring approximately 1.29 kg of water electrolysed per kilogram of propellant, raising the required water for an Apollo like ascent to 3056 kg and the corresponding scaling to 45 LUWEX units. The source characterizes these figures explicitly as order of magnitude context only (p. 6387).

The source's conclusion states that the LUWEX campaign validated a fully integrated lunar water extraction, capture, and liquefaction process at a scale and fidelity beyond most prior studies. Simply increasing heating power does not translate into higher liquid yield unless capturing capacity is matched, and dust mobilization and non water volatile condensation both impair hardware performance by degrading seals and reducing capture efficiency via liquid films on cold trapping surfaces (p. 6386-6387).

### Limitations

The cold fingers were actively cooled using circulating liquid nitrogen to approximately minus 170 C, which the source states is practical for ground testing but not representative of a lunar deployment, where cooling would need to come from space qualified cryocoolers or radiative cooling to deep space. The source states the present results may therefore overestimate capturing efficiency relative to a fully flight representative system (p. 6385).

Thermal control during delamination was limited: heating wires warming the cold finger surfaces did not deliver sufficient heat rapidly enough to cleanly detach deposited ice, attributed to inherently low thermal conduction in vacuum conditions, and the source suggests enhanced thermal interfaces or alternative techniques such as mechanical vibration for future designs (p. 6385).

Glass beads were selected in early experiments as a practical measure to avoid dust mobilization issues rather than to replicate lunar regolith characteristics, so the source notes that thermal and mechanical behaviors such as heat distribution and particle interaction observed with glass beads may differ from behavior with a representative lunar simulant (p. 6385).

Data logging at 1 Hz introduced uncertainty, particularly for short duration heater activations of a few seconds, which the source states risked being underrepresented in calculated energy metrics; pressure sensor readings are also described as primarily reflecting system equilibrium states between the pumps and sublimation rate rather than precise localized pressure values (p. 6385).

Periodic sample refilling to maintain approximately 5% ice by mass could have produced uneven ice distribution over time, with upper layers refreshed and lower layers progressively drier, potentially affecting extraction rate consistency, though the source notes all experiments used the same refilling method, providing a consistent basis for comparison (p. 6385).

Sensor placement was limited, resulting in incomplete thermal data, particularly for actual cold finger surface temperatures and crucible sample temperature, which the source states limited precise thermal control and assessment (p. 6384).

In Experiment 3 the source acknowledges that altering two parameters, methanol contamination and mechanical stirring, within a single experiment limited the ability to attribute observed effects to one specific factor, a tradeoff made in response to DTVAC time constraints (p. 6381).

Reported recovery and extraction rates include extended steady state hold periods, such as overnight operation, during which the system was intentionally held at stable conditions; the source describes the resulting rates as conservative, campaign level averages expected to improve with operational optimization (p. 6381).

The vacuum cleaner based crucible emptying method, while effective for reusing the crucible between runs on Earth, is stated not to translate directly to lunar conditions, where alternative removal methods would be required (p. 6377).

Residual ice mass was not measured for Experiment 1, preventing calculation of extracted mass, extraction percentage, extraction rate, capture percentage, loss mass, and extraction energy efficiency for that run; only recovery percentage, recovery rate, and recovery energy efficiency could be reported for Experiment 1 (p. 6382).
