## Abstract

Experimental feasibility study of hydrogen reduction of ilmenite performed in a static reactor, as opposed to the usual flowing-hydrogen configuration, for the ProSPA analytical module of the PROSPECT payload (planned for the ESA-Roscosmos Luna-27 mission). Because ProSPA processes samples statically, the study first assesses by diffusion modeling whether water vapor can leave the sample fast enough for the reaction to proceed, then demonstrates the reaction on a ProSPA breadboard with ilmenite samples up to about 45 mg heated at 900 C for 60 minutes, with water collected on a cold finger and quantified by mass spectrometry. Oxygen yields and reduction extents are reported as functions of sample mass, and SEM cross-sections show partial intragranular reduction.

---

### Background and objective

- PROSPECT (Package for Resource Observation and In-Situ Prospecting for Exploration, Commercial exploitation and Transportation) on Luna-27 includes ProSPA, an analytical module built at The Open University to identify and quantify volatiles released from polar regolith on heating to 1000 C, and to test water and oxygen extraction by hydrogen reduction.
- Unlike ROxygen, PILOT, and RESOLVE, which flow hydrogen through the feedstock, ProSPA operates in a static mode to simplify the design and avoid a gas-circulation system. The concern the paper addresses is that static operation could suppress the reaction through inefficient removal of water vapor above the regolith.
- The paper cites the flowing-system instruments ROxygen and PILOT as achieving oxygen yields of 1 to 2 wt% from regolith, as the comparison point.

### Methods and scope

- A first-order theoretical assessment of gas diffusion in the static system indicated water can diffuse through the system at an acceptable rate and be collected on the cold finger, allowing the reaction to proceed.
- Proof-of-concept experiments used a ProSPA breadboard with ilmenite samples. All reported runs used a fixed reagent dose of 0.30 mmol H2 at 900 C for 1 hour, varying sample mass. Water released from the sample was subsequently driven off the cold finger under vacuum and measured by mass spectrometer.
- SEM analysis of grain cross-sections assessed how completely individual ilmenite grains reduced (Fig. 13 reports grain counts of n = 39, 33, 47, and 44 across bins with per-bin reduction-extent fractions).

### Key findings

- The largest sample tested, 44.7 plus or minus 0.5 mg, released 17 plus or minus 1 micromoles of water, equating to a calculated oxygen yield of 0.6 plus or minus 0.1 wt% and a reduction extent of 5.8 plus or minus 0.4% (abstract; Table 1).
- The smallest sample, 11.2 plus or minus 0.5 mg, gave the greatest water-release oxygen yield, 1.4 plus or minus 0.2 wt%, at a reduction extent of 12.9 plus or minus 1.5% (abstract).
- Reported yields depend on which measure is used. The interim figure analysis of Table 1 gives a peak oxygen yield of 2.3 wt% measured during the reaction phase versus 1.4 wt% measured from water release, both at the 11.2 mg sample, declining to 0.8 wt% (reaction) and 0.6 wt% (water release) at 44.7 mg. Reduction extents span about 21.5% down to 5.8% across the sample-mass range. The theoretical maximum oxygen yield for the ilmenite used is 10.5 wt%.
- Water-trapping efficiency on the cold finger is about 68%.
- Yield and reduction extent fall as sample mass rises, which the authors attribute to the reaction being limited by furnace dimensions, reaction kinetics, and geometry rather than by reagent supply. SEM cross-sections confirm partial intragranular reduction, with some grains more reduced than others.
- The authors conclude the ProSPA static ISRU experiment should be capable of producing water, and therefore oxygen, by hydrogen reduction of ilmenite, and that this could ultimately be a viable technique for producing oxygen from ilmenite-bearing lunar regolith with ProSPA.

### Limitations

- The demonstration is at breadboard scale with milligram samples (up to about 45 mg), pure ilmenite rather than bulk regolith, and short single-hour runs; yields are well below the 10.5 wt% theoretical maximum and below the flowing-system 1 to 2 wt% comparison for the larger samples.
- Yield declines with sample mass over the tested range, so the results do not establish performance at the larger sample masses a flight or production system would process.
- Two distinct yield measures (reaction-phase versus water-release) appear in the source and differ (for example 2.3 versus 1.4 wt% at 11.2 mg); the difference reflects the measurement point, not a single settled yield.
