## Abstract

Reviews thirteen lunar regolith excavation and handling concepts identified in the literature for In Situ Resource Utilisation (ISRU), grouping them into discrete versus continuous excavators and, within each group, complete versus partial systems (with or without an attached mobility platform). For each group a common set of representative performance parameters is compared (excavation capability, depth of cut, mass, power/energy requirement, traverse speed, simulant used), and system-specific characteristics or limitations are noted. Finds that reported test data are inconsistent across studies, that regolith excavation research lags behind ISRU processing research despite being the first step in the process chain, and that essentially all reviewed concepts remain at low Technology Readiness Level. Proposes a baseline set of key performance parameters (excavation rate and traverse speed, power consumption, simulant/analogue properties) that future excavation experiments should report to enable comparability.

### Background and objective

The paper opens by situating regolith excavation within a renewed international push toward sustained lunar surface activity: ESA's PROSPECT instrument package flying on the Russian Luna-27 mission targeting the lunar south pole in the early 2020s, NASA's late-2018 partnership with nine companies for commercial lunar payload delivery, and China's Chang'e 4 far-side landing with Chang'e 5 sample return planned for late 2019 to early 2020 (p. 2). In Situ Resource Utilisation (ISRU) is presented as a means of reducing initial launch mass and cost and lowering dependence on Earth-supplied consumables for large-scale exploration (p. 2). More than 40 wt% of lunar regolith is oxygen by weight, usable as propellant and in life support, cited as the most likely initial ISRU application; other applications named include in situ manufacturing and habitat construction (sintering, 3D printing, selective laser melting, direct energy deposition), radiation shielding, metal production, and extraction of solar-wind-implanted volatiles such as helium-3 (p. 2).

The stated objective is to review regolith excavation techniques for lunar ISRU because, despite being the foundational first step in any ISRU process chain, "relatively little research is carried out in the area of regolith excavation and handling and almost no mature design concepts are found (Technology Readiness Level (TRL) < 4)," in contrast to the comparatively more mature body of processing-technique research (p. 2). The paper states it will (Section 2) walk through the ISRU process chain and background on ISRU generally, including applications (2.1), the importance of and challenges connected with excavation and handling (2.2), and the nature of lunar regolith (2.3); (Section 3) perform a parametric review of excavation techniques with existing system designs analyzed further (Section 4); and (Section 5) conclude with recommendations for a set of key performance parameters for future experimental campaigns (p. 2).

Two central design issues are identified for excavation, handling, and beneficiation equipment: (1) the geotechnical properties of lunar regolith, which differ significantly from terrestrial soils, and (2) lunar gravity at one sixth of Earth's, which limits the drawbar (tractive) force available to low-mass excavators (p. 2). As a worked example, Wilkinson and DeGennaro (2007) calculated that the 698.5 kg Apollo Lunar Roving Vehicle could generate only 239 N of drawbar pull on the Moon, limiting theoretical excavation depth to 1 to 15 cm depending on the excavation model used (p. 3).

### Methods and scope

The review classifies excavation systems along two independent axes (Section 3.1, p. 4). Discrete excavators must break contact with the soil between cuts to clear the cutting surface or dump material (e.g., front loaders, dozers, backhoes, scrapers). Continuous excavators keep multiple cutting surfaces in contact with the soil so cuts can be taken in overlapping succession (e.g., bucket wheels, bucket chains/drums). Orthogonally, partial systems are excavation equipment developed and tested in a test bed without being attached to a mobility platform, while complete systems have the excavation subsystem integrated into a rover or mobility platform; this distinction changes which performance parameters are meaningful for comparison (p. 4).

Thirteen concepts are reviewed: four discrete complete systems, four continuous partial systems, and five continuous complete systems (nine continuous systems total), classified in Fig. 3 (p. 4) with one worked example of each of the three main classes illustrated in Fig. 4 (p. 5). A maximum set of six common baseline comparison categories was established, varying slightly between the three system classes (Tables 3 to 5, p. 4 to 6): (1) excavation capability, (2) depth of single cut or maximum excavation depth (maximum reach), (3) mass of system or digging implement, (4) power or energy requirement, (5) traverse speed of the unit, and (6) regolith simulant or analogue used in testing. TRL is explicitly excluded as a tabulated column because almost all reviewed concepts do not exceed TRL 3, "Proof-of-Concept Demonstrated, Analytically and/or Experimentally" (p. 4).

Two additional large systems, Chariot (a truck-sized multipurpose NASA Johnson Space Center vehicle, optionally fitted with the LANCE bulldozer-blade attachment for berm construction) and ATHLETE (a six-legged mobility platform used for outpost site preparation), are described narratively but excluded from the parametric tables because of their significantly larger scale and non-excavation-primary purpose (p. 5 to 6). Drills are explicitly excluded from the review's scope; the authors state their primary planetary application to date is geological sampling of solid rock or high-strength material rather than bulk excavation of loose regolith for resource processing, and that the review targets excavators handling tens to hundreds of kilograms rather than sampling systems handling tens to hundreds of grams (p. 7). "Other concepts" noted but not parametrically compared, due to differing intended applications or a sole focus on excavation-force reduction, include explosive loosening, sub-surface excavators, magnetic excavation, and percussion/vibration-assisted excavation (p. 7 to 8).

- With an average particle size of 70 um, 10 to 20% of lunar regolith is dust finer than 20 um, which adheres to objects electrostatically (p. 3 to 4).
- Relative density increases rapidly with depth, exceeding 90% ("very dense") after just 30 cm, which the paper states makes excavation of deeper deposits significantly harder and may require drills (p. 4).
- Porosity is high, 40 to 50%, even when tightly packed, because irregular particle shapes prevent efficient packing (p. 4).
- The paper recommends that general ISRU excavation be limited to the top few centimeters of soil given how quickly density and cohesion increase with depth (p. 4).

| Water content (wt% mass) | Excavatability | Behavior similar to |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 0.3% | Very easy | Weak coal |

| Approximately 8.4% | Mechanical excavators required | Moderate-strength limestone, sandstone, shale |
| Approximately 10.6% | Massive excavators required | Strong limestone, sandstone |

- Feldman et al. (1998) neutron-spectrometer data from Lunar Prospector supported the presence of water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters; Li et al. (2018) later reported surface-exposed water ice up to 30 wt% in the near-surface environment of some permanently shadowed regions (p. 4).
- Gertsch et al. (2006) load-penetration tests on JSC-1 simulant with 0 to 11 wt% water ice found that compacted, frozen simulant behaves more like rock than a granular material (p. 4).
- Pitcher et al. (2016) penetration tests on highland simulant NU-LHT-2M observed a sharp increase in penetration resistance at 5 wt% water, indicating strong sensitivity of excavation force to water content (p. 4).

- Table 5 is the only table carrying normalized specific-energy figures alongside absolute power: the bucket-drum RASSOR system is reported at 0.761 Wh/kg and the pneumatic system at 2 Wh/kg (in addition to its 20 W absolute figure) (p. 6).
- RASSOR's specifications in Table 5 represent its latest iteration at the time of writing, stated as TRL 4, using two counter-rotating drums that counterbalance horizontal excavation forces, keeping the vehicle low mass and low traction (p. 7).
- The pneumatic system achieved lifting efficiencies of 3000:1 (simulant mass to gas mass) in early sand and JSC-1A tests at 300 K, and 5500:1 in a lunar-gravity-equivalent parabolic flight test transporting 100 g of JSC-1A with 18 mg of nitrogen at 0.21 bar (p. 7).
- Bucket wheel (Scarab) and bucket wheel (Polaris) were both tested with GRC-1 simulant; Scarab was additionally tested with gravity offload (five sixths of vehicle weight offloaded via rope support) at NASA's SLOPE lab (p. 7).

**TRL and overall system maturity (p. 7 to 8).**

- With the stated exception of NASA's RASSOR and Cratos platforms and the pneumatic excavation system, none of the reviewed technologies are described as being at an advanced TRL (p. 7).
- The majority of studies have not progressed past the proof-of-concept stage, which the authors attribute partly to insufficient funding or discontinued research programs (p. 7 to 8).
- Most reported tests and trials appear in conference proceedings or internal technical notes rather than peer-reviewed publications (p. 8).
- The authors state that without consistent reporting of key excavation parameters, it remains "challenging to highlight which technologies are most promising and most suitable for up-scaling and lunar surface implementation" (p. 7).

**Recommendations for future excavation studies (Section 5, p. 8 to 9).**

- For the current nascent period of lunar ISRU technology development, with small robotic demonstrator missions planned within the following decade, continuous excavators are judged more promising in the short term (p. 8).
- Taking many shallow cuts requires less excavation force per cut, permitting lower system mass, and avoids the soil pile-up in front of a static blade seen with scrapers and dozers (p. 8).
- Among continuous designs, bucket drums are described as more promising than bucket wheels because they eliminate the need for a secondary transport mechanism (auger or conveyor belt) (p. 8).
- Pneumatic systems are highlighted favorably for having no moving parts, though they require a compressed-gas consumable whose resupply or recycling must be addressed in mission design (p. 8).
- A set of four key performance metrics is recommended as always reportable in future excavation studies: excavation rate and traverse speed, power consumption, and properties of the simulant/analogue used, presented in the text as three headed recommendations (p. 8 to 9):
  - Excavation rate and traverse speed: report excavation rate in kg/h or m3/h, state the test/mission and soil conditions the rate depends on, and for complete systems also report the number of full cycles (excavation, driving, dumping, repositioning) achievable per hour, or at minimum the driving speed on the given terrain (p. 8).
  - Power consumption: report total system power in watts, ideally broken out between the excavation subsystem and driving, under stated test conditions (for example, driving fully loaded on a slope versus static excavation) (p. 8 to 9).
  - Combined with excavation rate, power consumption enables a normalized power-per-mass-excavated metric, which the paper states should be preferred over overall system power alone and expressed in W/kg (p. 8 to 9).
  - Properties of simulant/analogue used: geotechnical properties of the simulant materially affect excavation rate, power consumption, and force, so simulant properties and soil-preparation procedures (e.g., compaction) must be reported for reproducibility (p. 9).
  - CLASS Exolith Lab and ESA's Sample Analogue Curation Facility (SACF) are named as organizations improving simulant availability, while acquisition of characterized lunar simulants in large quantities is described as remaining costly (p. 9).
- An ideal future parameter set would be normalized by excavated mass and/or power consumption, for example W/kg/h, enabling benchmark comparisons across different mission scenarios; the paper notes that creating such benchmarks is currently difficult due to insufficient reliable data, and frames the recommended baseline parameters as a first step toward that goal (p. 9).
- Additional, secondary parameters suggested for inclusion are depth of cut (distinguished explicitly from maximum excavator reach), system mass, and details of excavation geometry, mobility-platform traction, TRL, and number of moving parts (p. 9).
- Gravity-offloading techniques used by some groups (Mueller et al., Skonieczny et al.) reduce vehicle weight but do not change how the offloaded soil itself behaves under gravity (p. 9).
- Parabolic-flight experiments under true lunar-gravity-equivalent conditions are described as necessary to address this gap, though the short duration of each parabola makes such excavation experiments difficult (p. 9).
- The authors state explicitly that the paper's intent is not to rate or rank the performance of the reviewed concepts, but to emphasize the need for reliable, lunar-specialized excavators and for consistent reporting that enables objective comparison (p. 9).
- The paper declares no conflicts of interest (p. 9).

### Limitations

- Few of the reviewed studies report the same key excavation testing parameters, which the authors state creates "a significant knowledge gap in assessing the suitability of a system for extended operation on the lunar surface" and makes direct comparison difficult (p. 7).
- Almost all reviewed concepts sit below TRL 4 (proof-of-concept), with RASSOR, Cratos, and the pneumatic system named as the only partial exceptions; most underlying test data appear in conference proceedings or internal technical notes rather than peer-reviewed sources (p. 7 to 8).
- Several systems have incompletely reported parameters within the paper's own tables: for example, Cratos lacks a reported traverse speed and maximum excavation depth; Juno lacks reported power consumption; the Glenn Digger lacks reported power and traverse speed; the flexible auger and impeller lack reported mass figures (Tables 3 and 4, p. 5 to 6).
- Low-fidelity analogues (e.g., unspecified sand) were used in several referenced test campaigns instead of characterized lunar simulants, which the paper attributes to the limited availability and high cost of proper simulants, and flags as a reproducibility concern (p. 4 to 5, p. 9).
- Gravity-offload testing (used to approximate lunar gravity) does not change the offloaded soil's own gravitational behavior, and parabolic-flight testing is constrained by very short test durations per parabola, limiting how well ground-based or flight testing can represent true lunar-gravity excavation (p. 9).
- The review's scope explicitly excludes drills and sub-surface sampling systems, which the authors treat as a separate category serving geological sampling rather than bulk resource-feedstock excavation (p. 7).
- The reviewed PDF is an article-in-press proof (running header shows placeholder "xxx (xxxx) xxx" instead of finalized volume/page), so this summary's bibliographic detail for volume and page follows the published record rather than a field printed verbatim in the document reviewed.
