## Abstract

Proposes a bank-based explanation for Japan's decade-long slowdown after the early-1990s
asset price collapse: undercapitalized banks engaged in sham loan restructurings to keep
credit flowing to insolvent borrowers ("zombies") rather than write off capital, so as to
maintain compliance with Basle capital standards. The paper builds a model in which
zombie survival congests markets, depressing profits, entry, and investment for healthy
competitors, and confirms with industry- and firm-level data that zombie-dominated
industries show more depressed job creation and destruction and lower productivity, and
that rising zombie prevalence depressed non-zombie investment and employment growth and
widened the zombie/non-zombie productivity gap.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper investigates whether misdirected bank lending prolonged the Japanese
macroeconomic stagnation that began in the early 1990s, focusing on the widespread
practice of continuing to lend to insolvent firms (page 3). The authors note the
triggering shocks are undisputed: stock prices lost roughly 60 percent of their value
from the 1989 peak within three years, and commercial land prices fell by roughly 50
percent after their 1992 peak over the following ten years (page 3). What is disputed is
the policy response. The political and regulatory reaction was to deny the problem and
delay bank restructuring; the paper cites regulators publicly denying the need for public
money as late as 1997, 1999, and 2002 (page 3, footnote 1). The one binding constraint
banks faced was the Basle minimum capital standard: writing off a non-performing loan
would force banks toward that minimum, so banks instead extended continued credit to
insolvent borrowers ("evergreening"), gambling on recovery or bailout (pages 3-4). The
government reinforced this by pressuring banks to keep lending to small and medium firms
to avoid the appearance of a credit crunch, especially after 1998 (page 4). The paper's
own framing situates this as a rational bank response to regulatory and political
pressure, not simply bank malfeasance (page 4).

The objective is to document the prevalence of this forbearance lending, model its
distorting effect on the healthy firms that compete with zombies, and test the model's
predictions against industry- and firm-level Japanese data. The paper states its
headline result in the introduction: compared with a hypothetical case in which the
1990s zombie share had stayed at its historical average, the authors' regression
coefficients imply investment at non-zombie firms was depressed by between four and 36
percent per year, depending on the industry (page 6).

### Methods and scope

**Zombie definition.** The authors depart from prior studies (which define zombies by
low profitability or productivity) and instead classify a firm as a zombie based only on
whether it appears to be receiving subsidized credit, so that the effect of zombies on
profitability and growth can be tested rather than assumed (page 7). For each firm-year
they construct a hypothetical minimum required interest payment, R*, using the average
short-term prime rate on short-term bank loans, the trailing five-year average long-term
prime rate on long-term bank loans, and the minimum convertible-bond coupon observed in
the last five years on bonds outstanding (pages 8-9). This R* is deliberately constructed
to be a lower bound, favorable to the firm, so that the resulting comparison is
conservative. The "interest rate gap," x, is the difference between actual interest
payments and R*, normalized by beginning-of-period total borrowing (page 9). Under the
baseline ("crisp") definition, a firm is a zombie in a given year whenever this gap is
negative (page 10). A second ("fuzzy") approach treats zombie status as a matter of
degree between two threshold parameters (0 and -25 basis points on one side, 50 and 75
basis points on the other), rather than a strict 0/1 classification (pages 10-11). The
authors note the measure cannot detect all subsidy types: it catches debt forgiveness,
interest concessions, debt-equity swaps, and payment moratoria, but misses new loans made
at normal rates that are used to pay off old loans, or generous asset purchases from a
client (page 10). A newspaper search of four Nihon Keizai Shimbun-sha publications
(1990-2004) for restructuring-assistance stories turned up 120 distinct cases and is used
to validate that interest concessions and debt forgiveness were the dominant assistance
types in practice (page 7, Table 1).

**Prevalence data.** The zombie indices are built from the Nikkei Needs Financial
dataset covering firms on the first and second sections of the Tokyo Stock Exchange,
1981-2002 (excluding the nine largest general trading companies), spanning manufacturing,
construction, real estate, retail, wholesale, and services (pages 4, 31). Sample size
ranges between 1,844 and 2,506 firms depending on the year (page 31). Eighteen
alternative zombie-classification schemes are also constructed to check robustness
against type I and type II misclassification (bond-rating exclusions, multi-year
averaging, multi-year maximums) and reported in Table 2 (pages 13-14).

**Model.** Section 3 develops a creative-destruction model of firm entry and exit in
which incumbents and potential entrants face idiosyncratic productivity shocks and a
congestion cost that rises with the number of operating units. The model is solved first
for a normal economy responding to a permanent negative profitability shock, then
re-solved for the case where "banks" subsidize a fraction of firms enough to prevent
their exit (zombies), holding destruction fixed at its pre-shock level and forcing all of
the adjustment onto creation (job/firm entry) instead (pages 15-24). The model is
extended from single production units to multi-project firms to connect it to the
firm-level empirical section (pages 24-28).

**Empirical tests.** Section 4 relates industry-level job creation, destruction, and
total factor productivity (TFP) changes (1991-93 vs. 1996-2000/1990-2000, depending on
the series) to changes in each industry's zombie index, using job-flow data from Genda et
al. (2003) and TFP data from Miyagawa, Ito and Harada (2004) and the EU KLEMS project
(pages 28-32). Section 5 turns to firm-level panel regressions (1981-2002) of investment
rate, employment growth, and a crude labor/capital-adjusted productivity proxy on an
industry zombie-asset-share variable interacted with a non-zombie dummy, with year and
industry fixed effects, industry-by-year fixed effects, and a sales-growth control tested
across specifications (pages 31-36, Table 3). Section 5 also computes cumulative
counterfactual investment and employment losses for the five most zombie-affected
non-manufacturing industries over 1993-2002 (Table 4).

### Key findings

**Zombie prevalence (Figure 1, page 4; page 10-14).** Treating all firms equally, the
(unweighted) share of publicly traded firms classified as zombies under the baseline
crisp definition hovered between 5 and 15 percent through 1993, then rose sharply in the
mid-1990s to above 25 percent in every year after 1994; the paper's introduction
describes roughly 30 percent of firms as "on life support from the banks" in the early
2000s (page 4). Asset-weighted, the share is lower, around 15 percent in the later
sample (page 4); the last-sample-year (2002) asset-weighted zombie percentage under the
baseline crisp definition is reported precisely as 14.96 percent (Table 2, page 52 in the
working paper's pagination), with the two fuzzy variants at 21.40 percent and 22.42
percent (page 14). By industry, the zombie problem was markedly worse outside
manufacturing: the crisp asset-weighted zombie index rose from a 3.11 percent average
(1981-1993) to a 9.58 percent average (1996-2002) in manufacturing, versus a rise from
4.47 percent to 20.35 percent in construction over the same two periods, with similarly
large increases in wholesale/retail, services, and real estate (page 11). The authors
attribute the manufacturing/non-manufacturing gap to global competitive pressure on
manufacturers (making subsidization more costly) and to the concentration of the
land-price shock in construction and real estate (pages 11-12). Restricting the
definition to firms that are zombies in two or three consecutive years lowers the 2002
estimate from 14.96 percent to 10.83 percent and 8.74 percent respectively (page 13,
footnote 13), which the authors present as a conservative floor rather than a competing
central estimate.

**Mechanism (Section 3).** In the model, a normal (non-zombie) economy responds to a
negative profitability shock with both a rise in destruction (exit) and a fall in
creation (entry); zombie subsidies hold destruction essentially fixed at its pre-shock
rate, so the entire adjustment is forced onto creation, which falls by more than it would
without zombies (pages 22-23). The model formalizes this as "sclerosis" (production units
preserved that would not survive without bank subsidy) paired with "scrambling"
(retention of less-productive incumbent projects at the expense of more-productive
potential entrants), terms attributed to Caballero and Hammour (page 23). A multi-project
extension of the model shows that the size of a non-zombie firm's contraction in
response to a negative shock is increasing in the proportion of zombies in its industry,
and that the productivity gap between zombie and non-zombie firms similarly widens as the
zombie share rises (pages 26-28, equation 23). The economic channel is congestion: by
continuing to operate, occupy market share, and bid for workers, zombies depress prices
and/or raise wages for the industry as a whole, lowering the profits and collateral
available to healthy incumbents and potential entrants and thereby discouraging their
investment and hiring (pages 4-5). The paper is explicit that this zombie/congestion
channel is intended to complement, not replace, standard credit-crunch or financial-
constraint explanations: an increase in zombies reduces the collateral value of good
firms in the industry and so tightens any financial constraint they face (page 5,
footnote 5; page 37).

**Industry-level evidence (Section 4, pages 29-32).** Job destruction rates rose in
every industry from the early to late 1990s, as expected after an adverse shock, but the
rise was smaller in industries where zombie prevalence grew the most; job creation
declined most sharply in the industries with the sharpest zombie growth (construction in
particular), while manufacturing (least affected by zombies) saw creation "hardly
change" (page 29). Average TFP growth from 1990 to 2000, plotted against the change in
each industry's crisp zombie index, is negatively associated with rising zombie share
(page 30). Using EU KLEMS sector-level TFP levels, manufacturing and non-manufacturing
productivity growth differed by 1.5 percentage points per year during the 1982-1991
recovery/expansion period, versus a gap of over 3.8 percentage points per year during the
1993-97 and 1999-2000 recovery periods (page 31).

**Firm-level regression coefficients (Table 3, page 54 of the working paper).** The core
specification regresses investment rate (I/K), employment growth (log employment
change), and the productivity proxy (log sales minus one-third log capital minus
two-thirds log employment) on a non-zombie dummy, the industry zombie asset share, and
their interaction, with year and industry dummies. The interaction coefficient (phi),
predicted by the model to be negative for investment and employment and positive for
productivity, comes in as: investment, -0.0885 (standard error 0.0330); employment
growth, -0.0232 (s.e. 0.0102); productivity, +0.2183 (s.e. 0.0756) (page 34, Table 3,
first specification). Replacing year and industry dummies with full industry-by-year
interaction dummies gives -0.0852 (s.e. 0.0333) for investment, -0.0188 (s.e. 0.0102,
significant at the 6 percent level) for employment, and +0.2315 (s.e. 0.0767) for
productivity (page 35). Adding firm sales growth as a control gives -0.0716 (s.e. 0.0321)
for investment, -0.0131 (s.e. 0.0098, significant only at the 20 percent level) for
employment, and +0.1943 (s.e. 0.0077) for productivity (page 36). Sample sizes for these
regressions range from about 22,400 to 23,100 firm-year observations, with the top and
bottom 2.5 percent of each dependent variable's distribution trimmed (page 53 of the
working paper). The authors read the consistently negative and mostly significant phi
on investment and employment, and the consistently positive and significant phi on
productivity, as confirming the model's prediction that rising zombie congestion harms
non-zombie firms' investment, hiring, and relative productivity (page 34). A parallel
regression run on US Compustat firms (1995-2004) found phi statistically indistinguishable
from zero for investment, which the authors offer as evidence against a purely mechanical
source of the Japanese result (page 33, footnote 24).

**Cumulative magnitude (Table 4, page 55 of the working paper).** For the five
non-manufacturing industries with the highest late-1990s zombie shares, the authors
compute how much more a typical non-zombie firm would have invested or hired had the
industry zombie index stayed at its 1981-1992 average ("Case 1") or matched
manufacturing's index for each year 1993-2002 ("Case 2"). For wholesale, the cumulative
lost investment (I/K) under Case 1 is 0.4323 (43.2 percent of capital), more than 3.5
years of the industry's actual average annual investment rate of 0.1184 over 1993-2002;
the "lower bound" version of this estimate (using only the interaction-term coefficient,
excluding the non-interacted zombie-share coefficient) is 0.1697, still over one year of
investment (page 36, Table 4A). For real estate, cumulative lost employment growth under
Case 1 is 0.0951 (9.5 percentage points), against an actual average employment growth
rate of only 0.0062 (0.62 percent) per year over 1993-2002; the lower-bound version of
this estimate is 0.0314 (page 36, Table 4B). Comparable Case 1/Case 2 and lower-bound
figures are also reported for retail, construction, and services (Table 4, pages 55). The
authors describe these calculations as subject to "substantial uncertainty," ignoring
general-equilibrium feedback, but "still informative and suggestive of the large negative
impact of zombies" (page 36).

**Link to bank health / regulatory forbearance.** The paper's own case for the mechanism
is institutional rather than a separate bank-level regression: it argues that Basle
capital-standard compliance, combined with regulatory forbearance that let banks avoid
recognizing losses, created the incentive to evergreen loans to insolvent borrowers
rather than force write-offs (pages 3-4). It also reports (citing Ito and Sasaki, 2002,
and Hoshi and Kashyap 2004, 2005) that when banks raised new capital, most of it came
either from affiliated firms dependent on the banks for financing (notably life insurance
companies) or from government capital injections, rather than from arm's-length investors
(page 3, footnote 2). The paper separately cites Peek and Rosengren (2005) as the most
direct prior evidence that perverse credit allocation to poorly performing firms was more
likely when a bank's own balance sheet was weak or when the borrower was a keiretsu
affiliate, and that non-affiliated banks did not show this pattern (page 6); this is
reported as supporting prior literature, not as a regression run in this paper itself.
The authors' own final assessment is that capital injections given to Japanese banks in
the late 1990s did not recapitalize them sufficiently to remove the incentive to
evergreen (page 38).

### Limitations

The authors flag several caveats themselves. The zombie measure is admittedly imperfect
and cannot detect subsidies delivered through refinancing at par or generous asset
purchases (page 10); a cited follow-up study (Hoshi 2006) finds the crisp measure
occasionally misses well-known troubled firms (a type I error) while only rarely
mislabeling a highly rated (AA or above) healthy firm as a zombie (a type II error, one
misclassification in 26 firms over five years) (pages 13-14, footnotes 11 and 14). The
paper acknowledges an alternative interpretation of the negative firm-level coefficient:
that the industry zombie share could simply proxy for unobserved industry-year
profitability shocks rather than a true congestion effect, and argues (without fully
ruling out) that this would require non-zombies to react more strongly to industry
downturns than zombies do (page 33). Adding firm fixed effects leaves the investment and
employment coefficients negative and significant, but makes the productivity coefficient
statistically insignificant, though still of the predicted sign (page 34, footnote 27).
Controlling for sales growth reduces the employment coefficient's significance to the 20
percent level (page 36). The authors note their model does not distinguish between
capital and labor congestion channels and cannot rule out that subsidized credit biases
zombies toward either more capital-intensive or more labor-intensive technologies (page
32, footnote 23). They explicitly decline to adjudicate whether zombie forbearance was, on
net, welfare-reducing once foregone unemployment costs are weighed against foregone
productivity growth, stating the model "is not structural enough" to assess optimal
regulatory policy (page 38). Cumulative loss estimates in Table 4 are presented as
suggestive extrapolations from linear regression coefficients that ignore
general-equilibrium feedback, not as structural counterfactual simulations (page 36). The
paper also notes its mechanism is not unique to Japan, drawing an explicit parallel to
state-owned-enterprise drag in transitioning socialist economies and to non-liquidation of
unprofitable carriers in the US airline industry (page 38).

### Topic mapping

FA8. FA4. Source list entry 3. Sub-Q 1. This is the red-team companion to FA4's account
of the Japanese main-bank system as a source of patient, monitoring-intensive capital:
the same institutional arrangement (banks willing to keep credit flowing to client firms
based on relationship rather than pure market signal) that FA4 credits as a growth-era
strength is shown here, in the different institutional context of post-1990 regulatory
forbearance and binding Basle capital constraints, becoming the mechanism that prolonged
stagnation. The paper's own numbers give FA8 a base rate and a failure magnitude: roughly
30 percent of publicly traded firms (about 15 percent of assets) on subsidized credit by
the early 2000s, a firm-level interaction coefficient showing non-zombie investment and
employment growth falling as industry zombie share rises, and extrapolated cumulative
investment losses at typical non-zombie firms ranging from roughly 18 to 43 percent of a
year's capital stock (lower-bound to central estimate) in the worst-affected
non-manufacturing industries over 1993-2002. For the lunar-economy caution this source
list entry is meant to support, the mechanism generalizes beyond banking specifics: a
directed-capital institution that is shielded from mark-to-market discipline (here, banks
avoiding capital write-downs) can convert a relationship-based patient-capital advantage
into a congestion externality that starves healthy competitors of the same resource,
once the shielding condition (forbearance) persists past the point the capital provider
can absorb honest losses.
