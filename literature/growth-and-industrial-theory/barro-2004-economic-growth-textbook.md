## Abstract

This is a **scoped reference extract**, not a summary of the full book. The source is a
672-page graduate macroeconomics textbook covering the Solow–Swan model, the Ramsey model, one-
and two-sector endogenous-growth models, R&D-based growth theory, technology diffusion, labor
supply and population, growth accounting, and empirical cross-country and regional growth
regressions.

FA2 uses this book only as the canonical reference for two closed-form growth-rate results and
the concepts that connect them:

1. the Solow–Swan conditional-convergence speed, β, and its formula β = (1 − α)(x + n + δ); and
2. the AK endogenous-growth rate, in both its exogenous-saving form (γ\* = sA − (n + δ),
   chapter 1) and its household-optimization form (g = (1/θ)(A − δ − ρ), chapter 4).

No other content of the book (overlapping-generations models, R&D/quality-ladder models,
migration/fertility models, growth-accounting mechanics, or the cross-country regression
program in full) is summarized here beyond brief pointers needed to locate the convergence-
speed evidence.

## Summary

### Background and objective

*Economic Growth* is Barro and Sala-i-Martin's standard first-year graduate text (MIT Press,
2nd ed. 2004), combining the neoclassical growth model (Solow–Swan, Ramsey/Cass–Koopmans) with
the endogenous-growth literature that emerged after the mid-1980s (AK models, two-sector human-
capital models, R&D/variety and quality-ladder models, technology diffusion). It is written at
the level of first-year economics PhD students and includes a mathematical appendix on
differential equations and dynamic optimization.

This extract covers only the sections needed for FA2 sub-questions 1 and 3, which ask the
literature review to establish (a) what standard growth theory predicts for the *speed* at
which economies close the gap to their steady state, and (b) what growth-theoretic mechanism,
if any, in the standard toolkit can produce a constant, non-decelerating growth rate at all.

### Methods and scope

This entry is a reference extract of two specific closed-form results — the Solow–Swan
conditional-convergence-speed formula and the AK-model constant-growth-rate formula — plus the
surrounding conceptual apparatus (absolute vs. conditional convergence, σ- vs. β-convergence,
level effects vs. rate effects, technology diffusion as a third convergence channel) that FA2
needs to characterize where the thesis's growth-rate claim sits relative to standard growth
theory. It is not a review of the book's empirical methodology, its treatment of R&D-driven
growth, or its full argument structure.

All formulas below are transcribed directly from the book's numbered equations, with page
locations. One illustrative back-of-envelope calculation (clearly marked) applies the book's
own formulas to a 41%/year benchmark; this arithmetic is not performed or endorsed by Barro and
Sala-i-Martin, who do not discuss the thesis or any specific growth-rate claim of that
magnitude.

### Key findings

**1. The Solow–Swan conditional-convergence speed, β.**

For the Cobb–Douglas case, with capital per effective worker k̂, saving rate s, technology
level A, labor-augmenting technical progress rate x, population growth n, and depreciation δ,
the growth rate of k̂ is (eq. 1.41, p. 56):

  k̂̇/k̂ = sA·(k̂)^−(1−α) − (x + n + δ)

The convergence speed is defined as β ≡ −∂(k̂̇/k̂)/∂log(k̂) (eq. 1.42, p. 56). Evaluated in the
neighborhood of the steady state, this reduces to (eq. 1.45, p. 57):

  **β\* = (1 − α)·(x + n + δ)**

where α is capital's income share. The appendix (§1.5.2, p. 78) gives the off-steady-state
form, showing β is not actually constant but declines monotonically as the economy approaches
ŷ\*: β = (1 − α)·(x + n + δ)·(ŷ/ŷ\*)^[−(1−α)/α], which collapses to β\* = (1 − α)(x + n + δ)
exactly at ŷ = ŷ\*. The same coefficient governs convergence of output per effective worker,
since ŷ obeys the log-linear approximation ŷ̇/ŷ ≈ −β\*·log(ŷ/ŷ\*) (eq. 1.47, p. 57). The half-
life of convergence — time for half the initial gap to close — is log(2)/β\* (p. 58, n. 26).

*Calibration (p. 58).* With benchmark values x = 0.02/yr, n = 0.01/yr, δ = 0.05/yr, and a
narrow physical-capital share α = 1/3, equation (1.45) gives β\* = 5.6%/year (half-life ≈ 12.5
years) — a speed the authors describe as too high relative to the data (p. 59). To match the
empirically observed range of β ≈ 1.5–3.0%/year, the model requires a much higher capital
share; α = 0.75 (a broad concept of capital including human capital) yields β\* = 2.0%/year
exactly, with a half-life of about 35 years and roughly 70 years to eliminate three-quarters of
an initial gap (p. 59). An extended Solow–Swan model with physical and human capital (capital
shares α = 1/3 and η = 0.4) gives the analogous formula **β\* = (1 − α − η)·(δ + n + x)** (eq.
1.54, p. 60), which for these parameter values also yields β\* ≈ 2.1%/year (p. 60).

*Empirical estimates confirming the ~2%/year figure:*

- U.S. states, 1880–2000 (ch. 11, p. 467): point estimate β = 0.0172 (s.e. 0.0024) for the
  univariate regression; β = 0.0160 (s.e. 0.0034) with regional dummies; cross-region estimate
  2.1%/year.
- Standard cross-country growth regression, 1965–95 panel, 72–86 countries (ch. 12, p. 521):
  coefficient on log(initial per-capita GDP) = −0.025 (s.e. 0.003), which the text states means
  "convergence occurs at the rate of about 2.5 percent per year."
- **Japanese prefectures, 1930–90** (ch. 11, §11.3.1, pp. 474–476, citing Barro and Sala-i-
  Martin 1992b, 47 prefectures): β = 0.0279 (s.e. 0.0033) for the full period. Splitting the
  sample: β = 0.0358 (s.e. 0.0035) for 1930–55, and β = 0.0191 (s.e. 0.0035) for 1955–90 —
  i.e., during Japan's own high-growth postwar decades, the estimated *cross-prefecture*
  convergence speed (~1.9%/year) sits inside the same ~2%/year band found for the U.S. states
  and the cross-country panel, not at any qualitatively higher rate. Five-year subperiod
  estimates within 1955–90 range from a low of −0.0152 (1955–60, wrong sign, insignificant) to
  a high of 0.0967 (s.e. 0.0100) for 1970–75 — the single fastest five-year window is under
  10%/year, still roughly 4x below a 41%/year benchmark.

**Bottom-line number for FA2:** standard conditional-convergence theory — both the theoretical
formula β\* = (1 − α)(x + n + δ) under a plausible broad-capital calibration, and the empirical
estimates across U.S. states, cross-country panels, and Japanese prefectures specifically —
puts the speed at which a catch-up economy (or catch-up region) closes the gap to its own
steady state at **approximately 2% per year** (empirical range ≈1.5–3%/year; U.S. states
≈1.7–2.1%/year; cross-country basic regression ≈2.5%/year; Japanese prefectures, postwar period
≈1.9%/year, with no five-year subperiod exceeding ~9.7%/year even during the fastest phase of
regional catch-up). At β = 2%/year, roughly 35 years are needed to close half the gap to steady
state, and about 70 years for three-quarters.

Set against a candidate 41%/year growth-rate requirement, this ~2%/year convergence speed is
smaller by a factor of roughly 20 (~1.3 orders of magnitude), and is also on the order of 4x
slower than commonly cited estimates of Japan's own high-growth-era per-capita growth rate (the
book elsewhere reports Japan's per-capita GDP growth as averaging 2.95%/year over the long
1890–1990 span, with the postwar high-growth decades running well above that average, p. 1).
This quantifies how far a 41%/year sustained rate sits outside the parameter space the standard
convergence framework was built to explain: β is a *rate of closing a gap to a level*, not a
sustained per-period growth rate, and even the fastest plausible theoretical calibrations of β
top out around 5–6%/year (the "too high" α = 1/3 case) — and even the single fastest observed
five-year window across 47 Japanese prefectures (1970–75, β ≈ 9.7%/year) remains well under
half of 41%/year.

**2. The AK model growth rate — the one standard mechanism giving constant, non-decelerating
growth.**

*Exogenous-saving version (ch. 1, §1.3.2, pp. 63–65).* If output is linear in capital, Y = AK
(eq. 1.59, p. 63), with A a constant reflecting a broad, reproducible concept of capital
(physical plus human), then average and marginal products of capital are both constant at A,
with no diminishing returns. Substituting into the Solow accumulation equation gives a
*constant* per-capita growth rate independent of the capital stock (eq. 1.60, p. 64):

  **γ\* = sA − (n + δ)**

Because the growth rate does not depend on k, ẏ/y = k̇/k = γ\* at every level of k — there is
no transition, no deceleration, and (equivalently) β\* = 0 for this model, since AK is the α =
1 limiting case of the Cobb-Douglas convergence formula (p. 65: "α = 1 implies β\* = 0").

*Household-optimization version (ch. 4, §4.1, pp. 205–210).* With households maximizing CRRA
utility (coefficient θ, discount rate ρ) subject to the linear technology y = Ak (eq. 4.6, p.
206), the firm's profit condition gives the interest rate r = A − δ (eq. 4.7, p. 206), and the
consumption Euler equation ċ/c = (1/θ)(r − ρ) becomes, in equilibrium (eq. 4.9, p. 207;
restated as eq. 4.16, p. 208):

  **g = ċ/c = k̇/k = ẏ/y = (1/θ)·(A − δ − ρ)**

This is the exact closed-form match to the formula referenced in the FA2 ledger. The model has
*no transitional dynamics at all* (p. 208): consumption, capital, and output all begin at their
period-0 levels and grow at this same constant rate forever from t = 0, because the marginal
product of capital never diminishes (the Inada condition lim(k→∞) f′(k) = 0 is violated by
construction; f′(k) = A for all k, ch. 4 §4.1.2, p. 206). The condition A > ρ + δ is required
for positive growth (eq. 4.12, p. 207). The associated constant gross saving rate is s = [A − ρ
+ θn + (θ−1)δ] / (θA) (eq. 4.17, p. 208).

*Illustrative arithmetic (not stated in the book) — what A or s would a 41%/year band require:*
Using the exogenous-saving form with the book's own benchmark n = 0.01, δ = 0.05, and the "East
Asian miracle" investment ratio the book itself reports (s ≈ 0.25, ch. 1 opening, p. 23),
matching γ\* = 0.41 requires sA − 0.06 = 0.41, i.e., A ≈ 1.88 (a marginal product of capital of
~188%/year). Using the optimizing form with log utility (θ = 1) and ρ ≈ 0.02–0.05, matching g =
0.41 requires A ≈ 0.48–0.51 (a marginal product of capital of ~48–51%/year); with θ = 2, A ≈
0.89–0.92 (~89–92%/year). All of these implied values of A are far above conventional
calibrations of the marginal product of capital (typically single digits to ~15%/year even for
fast-growing economies, since r = A − δ is an observable real return). This is the sense in
which a sustained 41%/year rate would require the *one* standard growth mechanism that admits
constant, non-decelerating growth to operate at a marginal product of capital roughly 3–10x any
empirically plausible value.

**3. Why Solow decelerates and AK does not — the two-line mechanical statement.**

In the Solow–Swan model, the production function has diminishing marginal returns to capital
(f′(k) → 0 as k → ∞, an Inada condition, ch. 1 §1.2.1). As capital accumulates, the average and
marginal products of capital fall toward the effective depreciation rate (x + n + δ), so the
growth rate k̂̇/k̂ = sA·k̂^−(1−α) − (x + n + δ) shrinks monotonically toward zero — this is
what produces both the finite steady state and the convergence coefficient β\* (pp. 56–58).

In the AK model, the production technology is linear in (broadly defined, reproducible)
capital, so the marginal product is the constant A regardless of the capital stock already
accumulated (ch. 1 §1.3.2, p. 63; ch. 4 §4.1.2, p. 206) — the Inada condition is deliberately
violated. Because the average/marginal product of capital never falls, there is nothing for the
growth rate to decelerate toward, and growth remains at the constant rate γ\* (or g)
indefinitely.

Barro and Sala-i-Martin's own generalization (§1.3.3, pp. 66–68, "Endogenous Growth with
Transitional Dynamics," building on Jones and Manuelli 1990) shows the necessary and sufficient
condition for any long-run endogenous (non-vanishing) growth rate is that the marginal product
of capital stay bounded above zero as k → ∞: lim(k→∞) f′(k) > (n + δ)/s > 0 (p. 66) — i.e.,
diminishing returns can operate at low k but must asymptote to a strictly positive floor. A
hybrid technology such as F(K, L) = AK + BK^αL^(1−α) (eq. 1.62, p. 66) satisfies this and
yields both eventual endogenous growth *and* conditional convergence along the way (p. 68) —
the two properties (constant asymptotic growth, and convergence) are not mutually exclusive in
principle, but pure AK (used for the ledger's g formula) has the convergence coefficient at
exactly zero.

**4. Technology diffusion / catch-up to the frontier (brief pointer only).**

Chapter 8 opens by noting that Solow-style convergence rests on diminishing returns to capital,
but that a *second*, independent channel can generate convergence even absent diminishing
returns: if a follower country's economy grows by imitating (at lower cost) product varieties
or quality levels already invented by a technological leader, follower-country catch-up growth
is faster than leader growth because imitation and implementation of discoveries are cheaper
than innovation (p. 349, paraphrased). The book states explicitly that this mechanism gives a
further, independent reason to predict convergence across economies, operating whether or not
diminishing returns to capital apply (p. 349).

The model (built on the chapter 6 variety-expansion framework) is developed for a two-country
leader/follower setting (§8.1–8.2, pp. 351–363) but was not read in detail for this extract;
FA2 should treat this chapter only as confirming that "catch-up to a frontier" is a recognized
third channel in the standard toolkit, distinct from both Solow convergence and AK-type
endogenous growth, and not itself a source of the specific ~2%/year or 41%/year figures used
elsewhere in this extract.

### Limitations

- This extract covers roughly 65 of 672 pages (~10%) of the book, selected specifically for the
  Solow-convergence and AK-growth results FA2 needs; it does not represent the book's full
  argument or its treatment of R&D-based, quality-ladder, or human-capital-based endogenous
  growth models (ch. 5–7), which are the dominant endogenous-growth mechanisms discussed in the
  book but are out of scope here.
- The book is a synthesis/textbook, not primary research; its cited empirical estimates (β ≈
  2–2.5%/year) derive from Barro's own and others' published cross-country and regional
  regressions (Barro 1991; Mankiw, Romer, and Weil 1992; Barro and Sala-i-Martin 1992a, 1992b),
  which are subject to the well-known cross-country-regression robustness debates the book
  itself discusses in ch. 12 (Levine and Renelt 1992; Sala-i-Martin, Doppelhofer, and Miller
  2003) but which are not detailed in this extract.
- The Japanese-prefecture regressions (ch. 11, §11.3.1) measure convergence in per capita
  income *across regions within Japan*, not Japan's aggregate national growth rate relative to
  a world or technology frontier; they are included here because they are the book's own
  closest empirical analogue to "Japan, growth speed," not because they directly estimate
  national-level catch-up to the frontier.
- The "illustrative arithmetic" computing what A or s a 41%/year growth rate would require (Key
  finding 2) is this summary's own application of the book's formulas, not a calculation
  performed or endorsed by Barro and Sala-i-Martin; it is included only to make the order-of-
  magnitude gap concrete and should be labeled as derived, not sourced, if cited elsewhere in
  FA2.
- The diffusion-model discussion (Key finding 4) covers only the chapter's two opening pages;
  the quantitative results of that model (steady-state catch-up growth rates, conditions for
  leapfrogging) were not extracted and would require a separate pass if FA2 needs them.
- No figures/tables required for the extracted results were image-only; all formulas and
  regression coefficients needed were recoverable as machine-readable text via pypdf, so no
  additional page-rendering pass was required.
