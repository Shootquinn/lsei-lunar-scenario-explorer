## Abstract

Taylor's founding statement of scientific management. Diagnoses "systematic soldiering," workers'
deliberate underwork under ordinary management, as the central industrial problem, and proposes
replacing rule-of-thumb methods with a developed science for each job, chosen through time study
and standardization. States four duties of management that make up the new system, and argues the
case through a graduated series of company case studies, pig-iron handling and the worker called
Schmidt, shoveling, machine-shop inspection, and metal cutting, each reporting before-and-after
tonnage, cost, and wage figures. Frames the whole as a route to "maximum prosperity" for employer
and employee together rather than a device for extracting more work at the same pay.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The book opens by citing President Roosevelt's remark that conservation of material resources is
"only preliminary to the larger question of national efficiency," and argues that the country's
daily waste of human effort through inefficient, "blundering, ill-directed" work is a larger loss
than the waste of material resources, though far less visible. Taylor states three purposes for
the book: first, to show through simple illustrations the loss the country suffers from
inefficiency in daily work; second, to convince the reader that the remedy lies in systematic
management rather than in searching for an exceptional man; and third, to prove that the best
management is a true science resting on clearly defined laws, rules, and principles.

The book's diagnostic starting point is "soldiering," workmen deliberately working under their
capacity. Taylor distinguishes "natural soldiering," the ordinary human tendency to take it easy,
from "systematic soldiering," a deliberate, learned practice by which workmen conceal from
employers how fast a job can actually be done, so that employers cannot use that knowledge to
raise output requirements without a corresponding rise in pay. He traces systematic soldiering to
three causes: the fallacy, widespread among workmen, that greater individual output throws men out
of work; defective management systems that make slow work a rational response to employers'
ignorance of true job times; and inefficient rule-of-thumb methods that waste much of the
workman's effort regardless of his intentions. Countering the first cause, Taylor argues from the
history of the shoe trade that cheapening a product through greater output increases demand
enough to employ more men overall, not fewer.

The book's stated central object of management, repeated at several points, is "the maximum
prosperity for the employer, coupled with the maximum prosperity for each employee," understood
for the employer as durable business development and for the employee as both higher wages and
development toward the highest class of work he is suited for. Taylor argues these interests are
not opposed but identical over the long run, since maximum prosperity for both depends on maximum
productivity, and illustrates this early with a simple example: two shoemakers who together, with
better methods, produce two pairs of shoes a day can be paid more, and still leave a larger profit,
than a competitor and his workman producing only one pair. He argues that eliminating soldiering
and substituting scientific for rule-of-thumb methods would, on the evidence he presents later in
the book, come close to doubling the output of the average man and machine.

### Methods and scope

The book has two chapters. Chapter I, "Fundamentals of Scientific Management," lays out the
soldiering diagnosis and the mutual-prosperity thesis described above. Chapter II, "The Principles
of Scientific Management," answers three questions Taylor says are uppermost in readers' minds:
how scientific management differs from ordinary management, why it gets better results, and
whether the whole problem is simply one of finding the right man to head a company. It answers
these by contrasting the "initiative and incentive" management common at the time, in which
workmen supply their own traditional knowledge and effort in exchange for some special incentive,
with scientific management, in which management itself develops the science, and then supports the
argument with a rising sequence of illustrative cases.

The book is not a controlled study with an independent method section; it is built from case
histories Taylor presents as drawn from his own work as a manager and consultant, chiefly at the
Midvale Steel Company, where he began as a laborer and machinist in 1878 and later became gang
boss and chief engineer, and at the Bethlehem Steel Company, where he introduced task work for pig
iron and yard labor. A further case, inspection of bicycle ball bearings by women workers, is
described without the company being named. The illustrations are presented in a deliberately
rising scale, Taylor states, from the simplest labor (pig-iron handling, shoveling) to more
intricate mechanic-arts work (machine-shop inspection, metal cutting), so as to show the same four
underlying elements recurring at every level of skill.

Across the cases, the method combines several recurring elements: stopwatch time study of each
motion in a job; development of a "science" for the job, sometimes expressed as an explicit law
(for example, the load-versus-rest law of heavy laboring derived for pig-iron handling); careful
selection of workmen physically and mentally suited to the task; standardization of tools and
implements to match the science (multiple shovel sizes rather than one shovel per worker, for
instance); setting a daily task tied to the time study; a large wage bonus, paid only when the
task is completed, as the incentive; written instruction cards; a planning department separate
from the shop floor; and, in the machine-shop case, a system of eight functional foremen replacing
the single foreman. Results are reported as company tonnage, cost, and wage figures compared
before and after the change, without a stated sample size beyond the workforce involved in each
case, and without a control group run in parallel.

### Key findings

**Four duties of management.** Taylor states that under scientific management, management takes on
new duties grouped under four heads: first, developing a true science for each element of a man's
work to replace rule-of-thumb methods; second, scientifically selecting the workman, then training,
teaching, and developing him, rather than leaving him to choose and train himself; third, heartily
cooperating with the men so that all work is done in accordance with the science that has been
developed; and fourth, an almost equal division of work and responsibility between management and
workmen, with management taking over work for which it is better fitted rather than leaving nearly
all of the work and responsibility to the men, as under the old system. Near the close of the
book, Taylor restates the same four principles in a shorter footnote form: development of a true
science; scientific selection of the workman; his scientific education and development; and
intimate, friendly cooperation between management and the men. He is explicit that no single one
of these elements, nor the mechanism (time study, functional foremanship, and so on) used to carry
them out, constitutes scientific management by itself; the same mechanism, he writes, can produce
either good or disastrous results depending on the underlying spirit in which it is applied.

**Pig-iron handling and Schmidt.** At Bethlehem Steel, a gang of about seventy-five men loaded pig
iron (each pig about 92 pounds) onto rail cars, averaging 12 and a half long tons per man per day.
Taylor's study concluded a first-class handler ought to load 47 to 48 long tons per day. Of the
gang, only about one man in eight was judged physically capable of this rate. Taylor and his
associates studied the men for several days, selected a workman he calls Schmidt, described as
small, exceptionally frugal, and building his own house before and after work hours, and set him
to work under close direction, being told exactly when to lift and walk and when to sit and rest.
Schmidt loaded 47 and a half tons that first day and, Taylor states, kept close to that rate for
the three years Taylor remained at Bethlehem. His pay rose from the prevailing $1.15 a day to
$1.85 a day, a 60 percent increase, once he worked at the new rate. One man after another was then
selected and trained until the whole 80,000-ton stockpile was handled at the new rate. Taylor
attributes the underlying science to a law developed with Carl G. Barth: for a 92-pound load, a
first-class man can be under load only 43 percent of the day and must rest the remaining 57
percent; lighter loads allow a higher percentage of time under load. He presents this rest law,
not extra strength or speed, as the reason the fourfold increase in tonnage was achievable without
overworking the men.

**Shoveling experiments.** At the same works, Taylor describes experiments varying shovel load to
find the load at which a first-class shoveler does his largest day's work, concluding it was about
21 pounds regardless of material. Because a single shovel gave loads ranging from about 30 pounds
for iron ore to under 4 pounds for light rice coal, Bethlehem built a tool room stocked with eight
to ten shovel types matched to each material, plus standardized picks and crowbars, and instituted
individual daily task assignments (with gangs limited to four men without special permit) in place
of gang labor under yard foremen. Comparing the old plan to task work in the third year of
operation, Taylor reports: the yard-labor force fell from 400 to 600 men down to about 140; average
tons handled per man per day rose from 16 to 59; average daily earnings rose from $1.15 to $1.88;
and the average cost of handling a ton fell from $0.072 to $0.033, a figure Taylor states already
includes office, tool-room, and supervisory wages. Total saving that year was $36,417.69, rising to
an annual rate of $75,000 to $80,000 once the whole yard was on task work. Taylor adds that of the
140 remaining laborers, only two were reported as drinking men, and that Bethlehem declined an
offer from a Pittsburgh competitor to pay its ore handlers more (4.9 cents versus 3.2 cents per
ton), on the stated ground that wage increases above 60 percent above the going local rate tend to
make workmen less thrifty and steady rather than more so.

**Metal-cutting experiments.** Taylor describes experiments, begun at Midvale in 1880 with the
permission of company president William Sellers, to determine the best angles, shapes, and cutting
speeds for machine tools, initially expected to take six months. The experiments in fact continued,
with occasional interruption, over about 26 years, using ten different experimental machines,
between 30,000 and 50,000 recorded trials (with many more unrecorded), more than 800,000 pounds of
steel and iron cut into chips, and an estimated $150,000 to $200,000 spent. In a later demonstration
on a single lathe at a shop Taylor does not name, applying slide-rule-calculated speeds, feeds, and
newly ground high-speed-steel tools to work the shop's own workmen had performed for years produced
gains ranging from two and a half times to nine times the previous speed on individual jobs; over
three years, shop output per man and per machine more than doubled, and average daily earnings rose
about 35 percent, while total wage cost for a given amount of work fell.

**Mechanism: planning, functional foremanship, and the differential rate.** Taylor describes
detailed written instruction cards, prepared in advance in a planning department by specialists in
cutting speeds, motion sequences, and time-study schedules, as central to the method. He states
that under functional management the single foreman is replaced by eight men, each with a specialty
he can perform himself if needed; the text names seven of these functions explicitly: the
inspector (work quality), the gang boss (setup and motions), the speed boss (machine speed and
tooling), the repair boss (machine upkeep), the time clerk (pay and reports), the route clerk (work
sequence and movement), and the disciplinarian. For the ball-bearing inspection case, Taylor
describes "differential rate piece work," under which pay rises both with the quantity of output
and, more steeply, with the accuracy of the work, alongside shortened hours (from about ten and a
half to eight and a half hours) and enforced rest periods, which he reports increased both output
and quality among the selected workers.

**Mutual prosperity and the closing summary.** Taylor closes by rejecting the idea that
antagonism between employer and employee is fundamental, arguing instead that a fair day's work
should become "a question for scientific investigation, instead of a subject to be bargained and
haggled over," so that disputes over output and wages diminish as both sides gain from higher
productivity. He summarizes scientific management, as a whole, in five short statements: science in
place of rule of thumb; harmony in place of discord; cooperation in place of individualism; maximum
output in place of restricted output; and the development of each man to his greatest efficiency
and prosperity. The book's final pages argue the general adoption of scientific management would
roughly double the average industrial worker's productivity, lower production costs, expand
markets and employment, and increase both wages and leisure, while warning that the transition, if
rushed without heeding earlier warnings, has in practice sometimes produced resistance and strikes.

### Limitations

The book is an advocacy and instructional text written by Taylor about his own consulting work,
not an independent or peer-reviewed study; the case figures (tonnage, cost, wages) are presented as
company records narrated by Taylor himself, with no outside audit, no control group run alongside
the changed condition, and no statistical treatment of variability beyond single before-and-after
averages. The Schmidt dialogue is presented as a reconstructed, stylized exchange illustrating how
Taylor believed such a workman should be addressed, not a verbatim transcript, and Taylor's own
description of the ideal pig-iron handler as a man of "the type of the ox," mentally unsuited to
most other work, is his characterization of workman selection criteria rather than a claim this
summary endorses. The reported gains depend on scientific selection of a minority of workers judged
suited to each task, one man in eight for pig-iron handling by Taylor's own figure, so the tonnage
and wage results describe hand-picked workers on a given job, not an unselected workforce. Several
numeric details in the extracted text appear as apparent transcription artifacts (for example, "47,
and 48 long tons" for what is evidently "47 to 48 long tons"), which this summary has resolved
contextually rather than treated as substantive ambiguity. The unnamed ball-bearing company and
some other cited firms are not identified beyond the shop or works named, limiting independent
verification of those figures against outside sources.
