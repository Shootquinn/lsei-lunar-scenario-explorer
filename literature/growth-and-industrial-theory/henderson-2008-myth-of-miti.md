## Abstract

Secondary encyclopedia entry arguing that Japan's postwar growth was driven by high investment, high saving, low taxes, and relatively non-interventionist government, and that the causal role of the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) has been greatly exaggerated. Its value to this review is that it reports, in citable form, the headline growth-accounting decomposition from Denison and Chung (1976) — the source needed to size the labor-reallocation contribution (FA1 mechanism M4) without extracting the Denison monograph itself.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The entry sets out to answer what caused the Japanese "miracle" and to test the popular claim that MITI's industrial policy was central. It reports that from 1952 to 1991 Japan's real GNP grew at an average 6.8% per year, and during the period of greatest growth (1952 to 1971) at 9.6% per year, so that 1991 GNP was over thirteen times its 1952 level, against US real GNP growth of 2.9% per year over the same 1952 to 1991 span. The author's thesis is that free-market factors, not government direction, account for the growth.

### Methods and scope

Narrative synthesis of secondary sources. No original estimation. The quantitative spine is drawn from the Denison and Chung (1976) Brookings growth-accounting study, with supporting figures on saving, investment, and taxation from Pechman, Hayashi, and Hudson Institute material. Scope is the 1952 to 1991 growth era, with the core decomposition covering 1953 to 1971.

### Key findings

**The growth-accounting decomposition (Denison and Chung 1976, as reported here).** Of Japan's 8.77% average annual national income growth from 1953 to 1971, four factors each contributed about two percentage points:

- increases in capital: **2.10** points
- advances in knowledge and factors not elsewhere classified: **1.97** points
- economies of scale: **1.94** points
- increases in labor: **1.85** points

**Most of the remaining growth was accounted for by reallocation of resources away from the inefficient agricultural sector.** This is the M4 figure. The four named factors sum to 7.86 of the 8.77 points, leaving roughly 0.9 points as a residual attributed largely to the agriculture-to-nonagriculture reallocation (plus other unlisted terms). So in the original 1976 accounting, labor reallocation out of agriculture is a real but modest contributor, on the order of well under one percentage point of the 8.77.

**Investment and saving.** Gross private investment rose from 17.2% of GNP (1952 to 1954) to 30.5% (1970 to 1971) — nearly one yen in three invested. Gross private saving rose from 16.5% to 31.9% of GNP over the same span (about half corporate, half household), versus 15.8% US private saving (1961 to 1971).

**Taxation as a saving driver.** Total national and local taxes (excluding social security) fell from 22.4% to 18.9% of national income from 1951 to 1970, while US taxes rose from 28.5% to 31.3%. Tax-exempt treatment of interest income (employer savings plans, postal savings) and untaxed securities capital gains are cited as saving incentives.

**The anti-MITI case.** Six National Economic Plans ran between December 1955 and February 1973; in every case actual growth exceeded the plan targets, which the author reads as evidence the plans were not the cause. MITI initially refused Sony permission to license transistor technology from Western Electric, and tried and failed to force consolidation of the auto industry — two of Japan's most successful industries succeeding against MITI's guidance. 83% of Japan Development Bank financing from 1953 to 1955 went to four industries (electric power, ships, coal, steel), but the entry notes it has not been established that these were good investments. The entry reads Johnson's *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* as containing little evidence that MITI's interventions actually helped.

### Limitations

- Secondary reference-encyclopedia entry with an explicit free-market editorial stance, not a peer-reviewed study; the anti-MITI framing is argued, not tested, within the piece.
- All quantitative content is drawn from other sources (chiefly Denison and Chung 1976); figures should be verified against those primary sources before use as anything but a locator.
- The reallocation contribution is reported only as a residual ("most of the remaining growth"), not as an explicitly estimated coefficient, so its precise magnitude must be read off Denison and Chung directly if a point estimate is required.

### Topic mapping

FA1. Sub-Q 1 (growth accounting), 5 (contingent enablers: taxation, saving), 6 (lunar analogue). Also relevant to the MITI-skeptic thread alongside Beason 1996 and Kiyota 2013.

**Feeds mechanism M4 (labor reallocation out of agriculture) and M5 (saving/investment financing).** Confirms M4 as a modest contributor (well under one point of 8.77) in the original 1976 accounting, and supplies the M5 investment and saving ratios. Note the contested-magnitude caveat carried from the wider literature: under human-capital-adjusted controls (Ye and Robertson; Cao et al., in a China context using the same Denison-Kuznets method), the reallocation effect can shrink toward zero, so M4's band runs from "modest" to "near-zero," not "large."
