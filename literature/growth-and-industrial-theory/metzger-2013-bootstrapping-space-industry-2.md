## Abstract

Modeling argument that advances in robotics and additive manufacturing make it feasible to bootstrap a self-sustaining, self-expanding space industry at low cost. A simple model identifies the main parameters of successful bootstrapping and indicates it can be achieved with as little as 12 metric tons landed on the Moon over about 20 years, using teleoperated equipment that transitions to full autonomy so the industry can spread to the asteroid belt and beyond. The strategy starts from a subreplicating system and evolves toward full self-sustainability (full closure) via an in-situ technology spiral, growing exponentially on the free real estate, energy, and materials of space. End-state industrial mass ranges from 156 metric tons with 60 humanoid robots to as much as 40,000 metric tons with up to 100,000 humanoid robots if faster manufacturing is supported by launching a total of 41 metric tons to the Moon.

---

### Background and objective

- The paper argues the solar system holds billions of times the resources of Earth, but accessing them has been uneconomic because of distance, orbital energy, and launch cost. Earlier analyses (O'Neill 1989; Zubrin 1999) found space manufacturing impractical without very large human populations, but they did not include robotic labor because robotics was then too immature.
- Two changes reframe the problem: robotics and 3D printing (additive manufacturing) as game-changers for space manufacturing, and the discovery of lunar polar ice supplying hydrogen, nitrogen, and carbon. With these, the Moon has every element needed for industry.
- The objective is to reassess and model whether a self-sustaining, self-expanding lunar industry can be bootstrapped affordably and how much landed mass and time it requires.

### Methods and scope

- A simple bootstrapping model is developed to identify the main parameters of success, exploring wide parameter ranges rather than a detailed engineering design.
- The concept begins with a subreplicating system (one that cannot yet make all of its own parts, so it needs imports) and advances toward full closure (full self-sufficiency) through an in-situ technology spiral, where each generation of equipment enables more of the next to be made locally.
- Equipment is teleoperated from Earth initially and transitioned to full autonomy so the network can expand to the asteroid belt and beyond without further Earth expense.

### Key findings

- Bootstrapping can be initiated with as little as 12 metric tons of equipment landed on the Moon over a period of about 20 years.
- The end-of-bootstrapping industrial mass is about 156 metric tons with 60 humanoid robots in the baseline case.
- If faster manufacturing is supported by launching a total of about 41 metric tons to the Moon, the end-state can reach as high as 40,000 metric tons of industrial assets with as many as 100,000 humanoid robots.
- Because of the exponential growth enabled by free space resources, within a few further decades and with no additional investment the industry could reach millions of times the industrial capacity of the United States.
- Modeling across wide parameter ranges indicates these outcomes are reasonable, though the authors state further analysis is needed.

### Limitations

- The results come from a simple parametric model exploring broad ranges, and the authors explicitly caution that further analysis is needed; the outcomes are feasibility estimates, not demonstrated performance.
- The model depends on assumptions about achievable manufacturing closure, robotic autonomy, and additive-manufacturing capability that were not demonstrated at the time of writing.
- The study deliberately excludes potential effects of nanotechnology, and treats humanoid-robot counts and closure fractions as model inputs rather than validated engineering quantities.

### Topic mapping

- The introduction frames resource limits on Earth, the historical O'Neill and Zubrin objections, and the two game-changers (robotics plus additive manufacturing, and lunar polar ice). The modeling sections define subreplication, full closure, and the in-situ technology spiral, and derive the landed-mass and time requirements. The results present the 12 metric ton, roughly 20-year bootstrap, the 156 metric ton and 40,000 metric ton end-states, and the exponential-growth implication.
