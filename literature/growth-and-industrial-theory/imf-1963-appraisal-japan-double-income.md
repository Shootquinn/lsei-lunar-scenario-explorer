## Abstract

Writing roughly a year and a half after Japan's Economic Planning Agency (EPA) adopted the
National Income Doubling Plan in December 1960, IMF economist M. Fujioka lays out the plan's
own targets -- 7.2 percent average annual real GNP growth over 1961-1970, a doubling of
national income, and full employment -- and tests them against the plan's first-year results
and against the structural drivers of Japan's preceding decade of growth. He finds the
numerical target almost certainly attainable, and possibly attainable early, because private
equipment investment had already driven real GNP growth to roughly 10-13 percent in the plan's
opening year, far above the 7.2 percent pace envisaged. But he argues the arithmetic of the
target was never really the risk: the live risks are a chronic balance-of-payments constraint
on how fast Japan can grow without running out of foreign exchange, a self-reinforcing
investment boom that could outrun demand and turn deflationary, and a rising wage-price
friction between large and small enterprises. The paper is a contemporary, insider-adjacent
appraisal of the plan that the FA1 review has designated the Lunar Doubling Plan analogue, not
a retrospective history of it.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The National Income Doubling Plan was adopted by the Japanese government in December 1960 and
took effect from the fiscal year beginning April 1, 1961. Fujioka frames it as the third in a
sequence of comprehensive Japanese long-range economic plans, not the first: the 1955 Five-Year
Plan for Self-Support Economy, built on Gerhard Colm's full-employment planning model, assumed
5 percent annual growth and was overtaken by actual results within its first few years; the
1957 New Long-Range Economic Plan assumed a maximum warranted growth rate of 6.5 percent a year
for balance-of-payments and price-stability equilibrium, and it too proved overcautious, again
with targets reached early. Before drafting the present plan, the EPA had produced a
twenty-year perspective study reviewing both predecessors' merits and defects and had gradually
assembled a working macro-model of the Japanese economy.

The plan's stated purpose is to sustain the high growth of the 1950s in order to raise living
standards and reach full employment, while anticipating three structural shifts over the
decade: technological modernization spreading beyond a few leading industries into small
enterprises; a slackening birth rate that would first swell and then shrink the annual new
labor force (a delayed postwar baby-boom effect); and a deceleration of world trade growth from
6.2 percent a year in 1950-59 to an assumed 4.5 percent in 1961-70.

The plan itself is structured in two parts, and Fujioka is explicit that this division shapes
how much weight the numbers should carry. For the public sector, where government has direct
control, the plan sets out concrete total investment envelopes for named programs -- road
construction, housing, flood control, educational facilities -- though actual annual spending
is left to be decided year by year in the budget process. For the private sector, which forms
the larger part of the economy, the plan supplies only a forecast and minimum guidance, leaving
initiative to individual firms operating in a free market. Because of this, the plan
deliberately sets no intermediate or yearly targets, only the ten-year endpoint, which Fujioka
treats as material to how the plan's early over- and under-shoots should be read.

Fujioka's stated aim, given in his introduction, is to outline the plan and its drafting
background (his Section I), analyze the basic factors behind Japan's past growth and the
outlook for those factors continuing (Section II), examine the emerging change in Japan's price
and cost structure (Section III), assess export and import prospects as the binding external
constraint (Section IV), and discuss the tension between free-market economic planning and the
plan's social-welfare aims (Section V). He explicitly disclaims comprehensiveness, describing
the analysis as confined to the features of recent growth that bear directly on the plan's
future course rather than a full account of all factors behind Japanese growth.

### Methods and scope

This is not an econometric or model-based paper; it is a structured, numerate appraisal built
from official Japanese statistical sources -- EPA White Papers on National Income, Bank of
Japan Economic Statistics of Japan and Monthly Economic Review, and Ministry of International
Trade and Industry (MITI) White Papers on Foreign Trade -- supplemented by IMF International
Financial Statistics and United Nations trade and national-accounts data for cross-country
comparison. Several tables index countries to a common base year (1953 = 100) to make growth
rates comparable across differing national statistical conventions, and the article compares
Japan systematically against the Federal Republic of Germany, France, Italy, the United States,
the United Kingdom, and, in the trade table, Canada.

The centerpiece of the author's own analytical contribution is a capacity-versus-demand
framework he attributes to Dr. Osamu Shimomura, in which a given year's private equipment
investment is treated as roughly equal to the following year's increase in output capacity, net
of a replacement allowance. Fujioka uses this relationship, applied to historical fiscal-year
data from 1952/53 through 1960/61 and projected through 1970/71, to test whether the plan's
assumed pace of private investment is internally consistent with its GNP growth target -- this
is the analytical core of his feasibility critique, distinct from simply restating the plan's
own numbers.

Bibliographically, the article follows the standard IMF Staff Papers convention of the period:
the English text is followed by a French-language Resume and a Spanish-language Resumen of the
same length and content, both present in full in this file (pp. 183-185), which independently
corroborates the IMF Staff Papers venue even though no volume or issue number is printed
anywhere in the supplied pages.

### Key findings

**The plan's own targets.** Real GNP growth of 7.2 percent a year, 1961-1970 (7.8 percent
measured from the 1956/57-1958/59 average base period, chosen by the EPA for statistical
convenience). GNP is to rise from an estimated 13,000 billion yen in fiscal 1960/61 (at fiscal
1958/59 prices) to 26,000 billion yen in fiscal 1970/71 -- exactly double. National income in
the target year is set at 21,300 billion yen ($59.2 billion), with per-capita income of 208,000
yen ($579), versus $335 in 1960 (compared with $1,085 in the United Kingdom, $957 in West
Germany, $954 in France, and $505 in Italy that year).

**Sectoral targets (Table 1, planned annual average change 1961-70, in percent):**
- Primary industry: productivity per unit of labor +5.6, employment -2.8, output (GNP) +2.8
- Secondary industry: productivity +5.5, employment +3.5, output +9.0
- Tertiary industry: productivity +5.5, employment +2.7, output +8.2
- Transportation, communication, and public utilities: productivity +5.6, employment +3.2,
  output +8.8
- Entire economy: productivity +6.6, employment +1.2, output +7.8

The plan's five stated policy elements are: expanding social overhead capital (roads, harbors,
factory sites, water facilities); strengthening the industrial structure toward heavy and
chemical industries; promoting foreign trade and international economic cooperation; improving
labor quality and promoting science and technology; and raising living standards for the
low-income group.

**Investment and savings assumptions.** Gross domestic capital formation is assumed to grow
more than 8 percent a year, employment more than 1 percent a year. Capital formation is to
reach almost 32 percent of aggregate demand in the target year, above the 30 percent base-year
level but below the actual 35 percent recorded in fiscal 1959/60 and 38 percent in fiscal
1960/61. Personal savings are assumed to average 15 percent of disposable income over the
decade -- well below the actual 19 percent recorded in fiscal 1960/61 (and 14 percent in fiscal
1955/56).

**External targets.** The plan projects an overall balance-of-payments surplus of $200 million
in the target year (a $410 million trade surplus, a $230 million deficit on invisibles, and $20
million in net capital receipts), requiring exports to reach $8,490 million against imports of
$8,080 million -- an annual increase of more than 9 percent for both.

**First-year results -- the appraisal's central empirical fact.** GNP growth in fiscal 1961/62,
the plan's first year, was estimated at 10 percent by the official January 1962 estimate; a
preliminary EPA estimate from August 1962 revised this to 13 percent in real terms (footnote
2). Both figures are far above the plan's 7.2 percent average target. By the paper's Section V,
Fujioka treats the 13 percent figure as the first year's outcome, and notes that private
investment in equipment in that single year already exceeded the level the plan had targeted
for ten years out (fiscal 1970/71). Prices rose about 4 percent, and the deficit on current
transactions reached roughly $1,000 million -- the investment-driven boom that the paper's own
opening summary flags as having caused a substantial worsening of the balance of payments.

**Historical growth base cited to justify feasibility.** Real GNP growth averaged 9.6 percent a
year over 1951-60 (9.5 percent for 1950-59; 8.9 percent for 1951-55; 10.2 percent for 1956-60),
against 7.5 percent for West Germany, 5.7 percent for Italy, 4.0 percent for France, 3.3
percent for the United States, and 2.4 percent for the United Kingdom over 1950-59 (Table 3).
The gross fixed capital formation-to-GNP ratio rose from about 18 percent in 1951-55 to 28
percent in 1956-60. (The 9.6 percent figure is the same real-GNP growth statistic the FA1
review already draws from Nakamura 1989 -- this paper supplies the plan's own contemporaneous
framing of that number rather than a later retrospective one.)

**The mechanism Fujioka identifies as driving growth.** Private investment in equipment
expanded 29 percent a year in real terms over 1956-60, against 9 percent a year in 1951-55, and
contributed 36 percent of the total increase in GNP over 1956-60, against only 6 percent in
1951-55. Mining and manufacturing production increased 2.3-fold over 1956-60, of which 41
percent was induced by the rise in private equipment investment. The marginal capital
coefficient (private capital investment divided by the increase in GNP) rose from 1.1 in
1951-55 to 1.9 in 1956-60, which the author attributes to longer-gestation heavy and chemical
industry projects such as port facilities for steel and large-unit petrochemical plants.
Investment for producing capital goods absorbed 24 percent of total investment in 1955 but 42
percent by 1960 -- investment inducing further investment.

**Productivity mechanics.** A Bank of Japan survey of 512 major manufacturing enterprises found
that, over fiscal 1956/57-1960/61, value added rose more than 100 percent while the number of
workers rose only 26 percent, so labor productivity (value added per worker) rose 64 percent;
decomposed further, output per unit of capital actually fell 2 percent while capital per worker
rose 68 percent. Fujioka reads this as showing that the era's productivity gains came from
capital deepening -- heavier capital investment per unit of labor -- rather than from any
improvement in how efficiently capital itself was used.

**The author's central feasibility critique, with numbers.** Applying the Shimomura
capacity-demand method, Fujioka projects that if private equipment investment merely held flat
at its then-current pace of about 3,700 billion yen a year for the coming decade -- a
deliberately conservative assumption, given actual annual increases of 33 percent in fiscal
1959/60, 41 percent in fiscal 1960/61, and 22 percent in fiscal 1961/62 -- cumulative output
capacity would rise by 30,112 billion yen by 1970/71, while the plan's targeted GNP increase
over the same period, restated at 1960 prices, is only 12,791 billion yen. On that comparison,
the increase in GNP would be only 42 percent of the increase in capacity -- a mismatch Fujioka
warns would produce an economy "too deflationary to be endured over a long period of time." His
implied resolution is that if investment does not slow, demand must be expanded faster than the
plan envisages.

**Price and wage risk.** Wholesale prices fell 6 percent between 1953 and 1960 while consumer
prices rose 15 percent over roughly the same span (8 percent during 1956-60 alone, about a
third of it from service charges), even as, over the same period, Japan's wholesale prices fell
while Germany's rose 7 percent, the UK's rose 13 percent, and the US's rose 9 percent (Table
5). Manufacturing wages rose 34 percent during 1955-60 while productivity rose 47 percent -- a
gap Fujioka treats as favorable but fragile, since wage increases running ahead of productivity
gains in low-productivity sectors (small enterprise, personal services, agriculture) could, if
they spread economy-wide, produce genuine cost inflation and erode external competitiveness. He
also cites an EPA estimate that the purchasing-power-parity rate of the yen for labor-intensive
consumer goods and services was about 204 yen per US dollar, against the official exchange rate
of 360 yen per US dollar -- a domestic-price-structure gap, not a trade-competitiveness
verdict, but a striking illustration of how far Japan's internal price structure still diverged
from its trading partners'.

**Trade dependency as the binding external constraint.** The marginal propensity to import is
about 15 percent; every $100 million of exports is estimated to permit roughly 240 billion yen
of additional GNP through its import-purchasing-power effect (a multiplier of about 1.47, built
from marginal saving, tax, and import rates of 0.28, 0.25, and 0.15 respectively). The plan
targets raising Japan's share of world exports from 3.6 percent (1960) to 5.6 percent (fiscal
1970/71), which requires 10 percent annual export growth even as recent growth had been about
18 percent a year and world trade growth is assumed to decelerate. Fujioka states plainly that
reaching the target is conditional on world trade growing at roughly 4 to 5 percent a year --
close to a precondition for the whole plan.

**Discrimination and export-pattern shift.** Fujioka documents specific barriers to Japanese
exports: European countries including the United Kingdom, France, and Italy were invoking
Article 35 of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade to withhold GATT obligations from
Japan, or maintaining discriminatory import restrictions, while exports to the United States
and Canada were subject to voluntary restraint. Japan's exports to North America as a share of
total exports rose from 23 percent in 1937 to 33 percent in 1960, while its export share to
Europe barely moved from prewar levels despite the region's growth. Even so, between 1955 and
1960 thirty-five new manufactured products -- transistor radios, nylon, plastics, and
automobiles among them -- accounted for 22 percent of the total increase in Japan's exports,
evidence of diversification into higher income-elasticity goods.

**Structural shift toward heavy and chemical industry.** The plan assumes the heavy and
chemical share of manufacturing value added rises from 61 percent (fiscal 1959/60) to 73
percent (target year), lifting their share of total exports from 38 percent to 54 percent.
Fujioka notes that by 1961 the domestic heavy/chemical share of manufacturing had already
reached about 70 percent -- matching or exceeding the United States, United Kingdom, Germany,
and France -- while the export share (41 percent in 1960) still lagged those countries' 71-83
percent, which he reads as evidence that much of Japanese heavy industry had not yet achieved
comparative-cost advantage internationally, a discrepancy the plan's export strategy has yet to
close.

**Foreign economic cooperation.** The plan estimates Japan's capital investment abroad at
$3,540 million over the decade, $630 million in the target year alone, plus $1,500 million in
credits to developing countries in that year -- a combined $2,130 million, more than twenty
times the base-year level and equal to 2.9 percent of Japan's target-year GNP.

**Consumption and social change.** Real per-capita consumption rose 25 percent during 1956-60,
accelerating after mid-1958 in what the author terms a consumption revolution: television
ownership rose by 3.5 million sets in 1960 alone (27 percent), washing machines by 1.5 million
(30 percent), and refrigerators by 0.8 million (67 percent); by February 1962, 79 percent of
urban households owned televisions, 58 percent washing machines, and 28 percent refrigerators.
A parallel leisure boom saw 57 percent of households take pleasure trips in 1960 (averaging 2.8
trips a year), and railway round-trip ticket sales rose 41 percent in fiscal 1960/61. Employed
persons are projected to rise by only 7 million against a 16.8 million increase in the
working-age population, cutting the employed-to-working-age-population ratio from 77 percent to
62 percent -- read by the author as progress against underemployment rather than a labor
shortfall.

**Other prospective demand sources.** Fujioka flags residential construction, which he
describes as relatively underdeveloped in Japan, as a plausible offset if private equipment
investment slackens. He also notes that the Japanese government budget had run a surplus every
year of the preceding decade despite periodic tax cuts, which he reads as evidence that
government expenditure -- needed for the social-overhead-capital programs the plan itself
prioritizes -- can be expanded on a basis of sound public finance without threatening the
plan's price-stability assumption.

**Market-economy planning philosophy (Section V).** Fujioka contrasts the plan with those of
centrally planned or developing economies, where government control over the economy is far
broader; the Japanese plan, by contrast, functions mainly as a forecast that private
enterprises can use to inform their own investment plans. He identifies a specific mechanism
for the overinvestment risk he flags elsewhere: because no single body coordinates investment
decisions, and because commercial banks compete with one another to supply investment funds to
large enterprises, competing firms tend to expand capacity ahead of demonstrated demand, each
betting that early movers capture the larger market share -- a dynamic he says the
income-doubling plan's own optimistic growth framing has, if anything, reinforced.

**Author's overall judgment.** Fujioka repeatedly separates two questions: whether the
numerical targets are reachable, and whether the plan manages the process well. On the first,
he is confident, closing the paper with the assessment that achievement of the plan is scarcely
in doubt and that its targets may well be reached one or two years ahead of schedule. On the
second, he is far more guarded, identifying excessive, uncoordinated investment competition
among Japanese firms, the persistence of Japan's dual economic structure of wage and
productivity gaps between large and small enterprise, and the balance-of-payments ceiling as
the live constraints that the plan's own free-market design leaves largely to trial and error
rather than direct policy.

### Limitations

The paper is a single-author professional appraisal, not an econometric or peer-refereed
academic study; its central quantitative tool, the Shimomura capacity-coefficient method, rests
on an assumed one-year lag between investment and capacity that the author acknowledges is
approximate -- he notes that true replacement-ratio statistics are unavailable and substitutes
judgment-based ranges. It is explicitly time-bound: written roughly eighteen months into a
ten-year plan, its first-year GNP figure is revised within the document itself, from a 10
percent official estimate (January 1962) to a 13 percent preliminary estimate (August 1962),
illustrating how provisional the data available to the author were.

The appraisal takes official EPA, MITI, and Bank of Japan statistics essentially at face value;
it critiques the plan's assumptions rather than independently verifying the underlying source
data. It says little that is quantitative about the agricultural labor exodus beyond the
planned -2.8 percent annual change in primary-industry employment, so it does not by itself
resolve the FA1 review's still-open Gap 5 decomposition question. Two of the article's larger
statistical tables (the year-by-year GNP-expenditure-component series in Table 2 and the
year-by-year capacity/demand series in Table 4) extracted from the PDF with column alignment
too ambiguous to reconstruct cell-by-cell with confidence; this summary therefore relies only
on the derived totals and ratios Fujioka states explicitly in his prose, which could be
verified directly, rather than on a full re-tabulation of those two tables.

Finally, and importantly for citation purposes, the document as supplied carries no printed
