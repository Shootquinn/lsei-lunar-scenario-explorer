# R&D-Based Models of Economic Growth

## Citation

Jones, C. I. (1995). R&D-based models of economic growth. *Journal of
Political Economy, 103*(4), 759-784.

## Abstract

Argues that the "scale effects" prediction shared by the R&D-based
endogenous growth models of Romer (1990), Grossman and Helpman
(1991a,1991b,1991c), and Aghion and Howitt (1992) — that a larger level
of resources devoted to R&D should mechanically raise the long-run per
capita growth rate — is contradicted by postwar time-series evidence on
research employment and total factor productivity (TFP) growth in
advanced economies. Proposes a generalized version of the Romer (1990)
model in which the R&D (knowledge-production) equation allows for
diminishing returns to the existing stock of ideas. Under that
generalization a balanced growth path exists in which growth is still
generated endogenously through purposive R&D, but the long-run growth
rate itself is pinned down by the exogenous rate of population growth
rather than by the level of research effort or by policy. Labels this
result "semi-endogenous" growth and works out its welfare and transition
implications.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens by situating itself inside the first wave of R&D-based
endogenous growth theory: Romer (1990), Grossman and Helpman
(1991a,1991b,1991c), and Aghion and Howitt (1992) had each built models
in which sustained per capita growth arises from profit-motivated
research effort, with the important corollary that government R&D
subsidies and related policies could permanently raise the long-run
growth rate. Jones's stated objective is to show that these models share
an unexamined and empirically false prediction — the "scale effects"
prediction — and to ask whether the basic R&D-driven growth story can
survive once that prediction is removed.

Section II states the scale-effects objection; Section III builds a
generalized R&D equation that removes it and derives a new balanced
growth path; Section IV works out the welfare/social-planner comparison;
Section V analyzes transition dynamics after a permanent change in R&D
effort; Section VI asks whether the resulting model is plausible at all,
given that its central prediction (growth tied to population growth)
also looks empirically awkward at first glance; Section VII concludes.
The paper's own framing is that removing scale effects is not a minor
technical fix — it silently converts "endogenous growth" (in the strong
sense that policy can move the long-run growth rate) into what Jones
names "semi-endogenous" growth: growth *generated* by R&D whose
*long-run rate* is set by parameters conventionally treated as
exogenous, principally the population growth rate.

### Methods and scope

The paper is theoretical, not econometric. Its use of data is
descriptive/graphical rather than estimated: two time-series plots
(Figures 1 and 2, built from published U.S. series) motivate and
illustrate the scale-effects rejection; a companion piece — Jones,
"Time Series Tests of Endogenous Growth Models" (cited as forthcoming
in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, May 1995) — is pointed to for
the formal statistical tests. Figure 1 plots the number of U.S.
scientists and engineers engaged in R&D (National Science Foundation,
*Science and Engineering Indicators*, 1989, and the *Statistical
Abstract of the U.S.*) against annual private-business-sector TFP
growth (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 1991), 1950-1988, and references
similar series for France, West Germany, and Japan. Figure 2 plots the
U.S. R&D-employment share of the total labor force (NSF 1989 numerator;
Summers and Heston 1991 Penn World Table denominator) over the same
period.

The theoretical core is a three-sector general-equilibrium growth model
that follows Romer (1990) closely in its final-goods and
intermediate-goods sectors (a competitive final-goods sector combining
labor with a Dixit-Stiglitz/Ethier-style continuum of monopolistically
supplied intermediate producer durables) but generalizes Romer's R&D
("knowledge production") equation. Where the Romer/Grossman-
Helpman/Aghion-Howitt family assumes new ideas arrive in direct
proportion to the number of researchers, with each researcher's
productivity growing in exact proportion to the existing stock of
knowledge, Jones introduces two free parameters into the knowledge-
production function: φ, governing whether the existing stock of
knowledge, A, raises (φ>0), leaves unchanged (φ=0), or lowers (φ<0, "fishing
out") the rate at which new ideas are found; and λ∈(0,1], a
"duplication" parameter allowing less-than-proportional returns to
adding more researchers at a point in time. The Romer/Grossman-
Helpman/Aghion-Howitt models are the special case φ=1, λ=1. The paper
derives the balanced growth path, the decentralized equilibrium R&D
labor share, and the social-planner-optimal R&D labor share in closed
form (with full derivation of the decentralized equilibrium in the
Appendix); it then linearizes the model's transition dynamics around the
steady state and runs a numerical experiment (a permanent doubling of
the R&D labor share, from 1 to 2 percent of the labor force) across
several (φ,λ) combinations consistent with a common 2 percent steady-
state growth rate, calibrated with a labor share of α=2/3, discount rate
ρ=.03, population growth n=.02, and intertemporal elasticity σ=1. A
separate calibration exercise (Figures 3 and 4) computes the gap between
the socially optimal and decentralized R&D labor shares across the
plausible (φ,λ,α) parameter space.

### Key findings

**The scale-effects prediction, and why the standard R&D-based models
share it.** The Romer/Grossman-Helpman/Aghion-Howitt family combines a
production function Y = K^(1-α)(AL)^α with an R&D equation of the form
Ȧ = δL_A: TFP growth is directly proportional to the *number* of workers
engaged in R&D. With a roughly constant share of the labor force devoted
to R&D, this makes the steady-state growth rate proportional to the
*level* of the labor force (and, with a constant R&D-labor share, to the
level of resources devoted to R&D). Doubling the number of researchers
should, at least eventually, double the per capita growth rate. Jones
calls this the "scale effects" prediction and treats it as the paper's
point of departure.

**The empirical rejection (the disciplining evidence).** The paper
treats this prediction as decisively contradicted by postwar time
series. Two versions of the R&D equation are tested against the data,
and both fail:

- *Level version* (Ȧ = δL_A). Figure 1: the number of U.S. scientists
  and engineers engaged in R&D grew by more than a factor of five,
  from about 160,000 in 1950 to nearly 1 million by 1988 (the
  introduction cites closely related NSF figures of under 200,000 in
  1950 rising to nearly 1 million by 1987, also roughly a fivefold
  increase). Comparable growth is reported for France, West Germany,
  and Japan. Over the same window, U.S. private-business-sector TFP
  growth held roughly flat or declined — nothing resembling a fivefold
  acceleration. The same disconnect holds for the labor force overall:
  it grew markedly over the preceding 25, 50, or 100 years, while
  average growth rates stayed roughly constant or fell.
- *Share version* (Ȧ/A = δL_A/L). An alternative specification ties TFP
  growth to the *share*, not the level, of labor in R&D — this removes
  the population-level scale effect but keeps a size-independent version
  of it. Figure 2 shows this version also fails: the R&D share of the
  U.S. labor force rose from about 0.25 percent in 1950 to nearly 0.80
  percent by 1988, over a threefold increase, with a comparable pattern
  in France, Germany, and Japan, again with no corresponding rise in TFP
  growth. The share specification is additionally rejected on
  theoretical grounds: it implies an economy with a single R&D worker
  could generate as much TFP growth as an economy with a million R&D
  workers, contradicting the individuals-discover-ideas microfoundations
  the R&D-based models are built on.

Jones's own summary judgment in Section II is blunt: essentially all the
R&D-based growth models then in the literature are inconsistent with
this basic time-series observation, and the scale-effects prediction is
straightforwardly rejected by the joint behavior of R&D employment and
TFP growth over the postwar period.

**The semi-endogenous alternative and its growth-rate formula.** Section
III generalizes the R&D equation to Ȧ = δ̄ L_A^λ A^φ and shows that a
balanced growth path exists, with population (and hence the R&D labor
force) growing at exogenous rate n, whenever φ<1 — i.e., whenever
returns to the *existing stock of ideas* in the knowledge-production
function are less than one-for-one. Under that condition the steady-
state growth rate of knowledge, output, consumption, and the
capital-labor ratio are all pinned to a single value:

g* = λn / (1 − φ)

Growth is proportional to the exogenous rate of population growth n and
to the R&D-sector returns-to-scale parameters λ and φ — and to nothing
else. In particular, government policy (R&D subsidies, capital-
accumulation subsidies, investment tax credits) has no long-run growth
effect in this model; it can raise or lower the *level* of the growth
path via a transition, but not the eventual growth rate, because tax and
subsidy terms drop out when the balanced-growth-path derivative is
taken. This is the "Solow-like" result the abstract flags: as in Solow
(1956), the long-run growth rate is exogenous to policy; unlike Solow,
growth still arises *endogenously* from purposive R&D, rather than from
an unmodeled exogenous technology trend — hence "semi-endogenous."
Jones traces the general shape of this result (per capita growth
depending on the population growth rate) to Arrow's (1962)
learning-by-doing model and to Nordhaus (1969), and explicitly draws the
parallel to AK-style models in a footnote: the condition for a balanced
growth path with population growth (returns to the accumulable input in
the growth-generating equation below unity) is structurally the same
condition needed in AK-style capital-accumulation models once population
growth is added; without it, population growth would make the AK growth
rate rise without bound, just as it does under φ=1 here.

**Underinvestment, not overinvestment, in R&D.** Comparing the
decentralized equilibrium R&D labor share to the social-planner optimum,
the paper finds that across nearly all plausible (φ,λ) combinations
(swept over φ∈[−1,1), λ∈(0,1] at labor share α=2/3) the decentralized
economy *underinvests* in R&D relative to the social optimum (Figures
3 and 4), even under large negative duplication externalities. The
reason: the monopoly markup charged by the intermediate-goods sector
(1/(1−α), 300 percent at α=2/3) dominates the opposing, growth-
depressing duplication externality across most of the range;
overinvestment appears only at low labor shares (α below roughly .6)
combined with low λ. Because the steady-state growth rate does not
respond to policy in this model, this is a statement about
welfare-improving *levels* of R&D subsidy, not about growth-rate
effects.

**Transition dynamics: long, and shaped by a trade-off between φ and
λ.** Although the long-run growth rate is invariant to policy, a
permanent change in the R&D share still raises growth for a long time
along the transition path. In the paper's numerical experiment — a
permanent increase in the R&D labor share from 1 to 2 percent, at a 2
percent steady-state growth rate, labor share α=2/3, population growth
n=.02, depreciation d=.05 — the transition is markedly long:

| (φ, λ) | Initial growth rate of A | Half-life of A growth (yrs) | Initial growth rate of Y/L | Half-life of Y/L growth (yrs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (0.00, 1.00) | .0333 | 35 | .0331 | 62 |
| (0.50, 0.50) | .0255 | 69 | .0253 | 120 |
| (0.75, 0.25) | .0225 | 139 | .0223 | 242 |
| (0.90, 0.10) | .0210 | 347 | .0208 | 674 |

As φ rises toward one (the Romer/Grossman-Helpman/Aghion-Howitt
benchmark), the transition path lengthens toward infinity, but the
*magnitude* of the initial growth boost shrinks correspondingly (governed
by λ, which must fall as φ rises to keep the steady-state growth rate
fixed at 2 percent): at (φ,λ)=(0,1) doubling the R&D share raises the
initial growth rate of knowledge by a factor of 4/3, but at
(φ,λ)=(.9,.1) the effect on the growth rate is much smaller even though
it persists for over three centuries at half-life. A footnote reports
that solving the underlying differential equation exactly (rather than
via the linear approximation) shortens the two shortest half-lives from
35 and 69 years to 25 and 51 years, without changing "the spirit of the
results."

**Plausibility check and the cross-sectional/intertemporal
distinction.** Section VI confronts the model's headline implication —
that population growth drives per capita output growth — against
cross-country evidence that population growth and per capita growth are
uncorrelated or negatively correlated (De Long and Summers 1991;
Mankiw, Romer, and Weil 1992). Jones argues this does not refute the
model: it applies most naturally to economies pushing out the
technological frontier rather than absorbing ideas from elsewhere;
diffusion between countries (e.g., U.S.-Japan) makes simple
cross-sectional population comparisons uninformative; and the model's
true input is *effective researchers*, which Figure 2 shows has
outpaced population growth in the U.S. postwar era (a rising
R&D-intensity trend the paper's own footnote 17 flags as an unexplained
puzzle within the model, possibly reflecting tax-policy shifts,
property-rights changes, or reclassification of who counts as a
"scientist or engineer"). The model still makes a strong, testable
*cross-sectional* scale-effects prediction distinct from the rejected
*intertemporal* one: two technologically isolated economies starting
from the same knowledge level, one with more researchers, should
diverge for a long time even without a permanent growth-rate
difference. Kremer's (1993) natural experiment on continents isolated
by the post-Ice-Age rise in sea levels (Old World versus the Americas,
Australia, Tasmania, Flinders Island) is cited as broadly consistent
with this cross-sectional prediction.

**The FA2 verdict this paper delivers.** Jones's result is a direct,
formal check on any argument of the shape "add more researchers, more
replication capacity, and the long-run growth rate rises without
bound." That is precisely the "scale effects" prediction built into the
naive R&D equation (Ȧ = δL_A; equivalently, an accumulation equation
with returns-to-scale parameter one on the accumulable input) — and
precisely what 40 years of paired R&D-employment/TFP-growth data
reject: a fivefold rise in the R&D workforce (threefold rise in its
labor-force share) coincided with flat-to-declining productivity
growth, not acceleration. Once the knowledge-production function has
diminishing returns to the existing idea stock (φ<1, the condition
Jones argues the data force on any credible R&D-based model), the
long-run growth rate collapses to g* = λn/(1−φ): a function of the
*population growth rate*, not of the *level* of research or
replication effort. More researchers, more replicating machines, or
more R&D subsidy raise the *level* of the growth path (via a transition
that can last decades to centuries, per the table above) but not the
long-run growth rate itself — that rate is fixed by n. Applied to the
thesis's 41-percent-per-year, 24-month-doubling target: this is exactly
the number a naive scale-effects argument could be used to justify, and
exactly what Jones's model forbids treating as a free engineering
parameter, because the scale effect that would make it available is
empirically absent in every advanced economy tested. One qualification
to carry into Sub-Q 3: Jones's constraint (g* tied to n) applies to
processes whose accumulation equation requires *people* to produce new
designs; it is not automatically a constraint on Rebelo's (1991) pure
AK model, where capital reproduces without a labor-linked generating
equation. Whether physical self-replication behaves like Jones's R&D
sector (population-bottlenecked) or Rebelo's AK capital
(population-free) is exactly the question FA2 and FA6 must settle.

### Limitations

- The paper is theoretical; its empirical content (Figures 1 and 2) is
  descriptive plotting of published series, not a formal statistical
  test. The author points to a companion paper, "Time Series Tests of
  Endogenous Growth Models" (*QJE*, 1995), for the econometric case,
  outside this document's scope. The illustrative data cover the United
  States in detail; France, West Germany, and Japan are asserted to
  show a comparable pattern but are not plotted or tabulated.
- The model omits capital as an input to the R&D (knowledge-production)
  equation; the paper asserts, without demonstrating, that adding it
  would not change the main results.
- The φ (returns to the existing idea stock) and λ (duplication
  externality) parameters are not separately estimated here; the author
  states that identifying both requires detailed industry-level work
  (citing Jones 1994, an unpublished manuscript) left to future research.
- The paper's own footnote flags an unresolved tension: nothing in the
  balanced-growth-path equilibrium generates the rising R&D-intensity
  trend documented in Figure 2, which the model needs as an input but
  does not itself explain; candidate explanations (tax policy,
  property rights, reclassification of "scientist and engineer") are
  offered but not tested.
- The welfare comparison is calibrated at one representative labor
  share, discount rate, and intertemporal elasticity (α=2/3, ρ=.03,
  n=.02, σ=1), with only φ and λ swept across their plausible range.
- The cross-sectional scale-effects prediction the model retains is
  supported mainly by Kremer's (1993) isolated-continents natural
  experiment, which concerns millennia-long, prehistoric-to-early-modern
  technological change, not the postwar dynamics motivating the paper.
- Transition-dynamics results rest on a linearization around the steady
  state; the paper notes, via one exactly solvable case, that this
  somewhat overstates the relevant half-lives, though it states the
  qualitative pattern is unaffected.
