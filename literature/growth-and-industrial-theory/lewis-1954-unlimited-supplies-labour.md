# Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour

## Citation

Lewis, W. A. (1954). Economic development with unlimited supplies of labour. *The
Manchester School, 22*(2), 139-191.

DOI: unconfirmed in FA2 ledger (Wiley; 10.1111/j.1467-9957.1954.tb00021.x to be
verified on the publisher page). Not recorded here per no-unconfirmed-DOI rule.

Publisher URL: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9957.1954.tb00021.x

## Abstract

Revives the classical (Smith-Ricardo-Marx) framework of simultaneously determined
income distribution and growth for economies where an "unlimited" supply of labour is
available at a roughly constant subsistence-related wage, arguing that neoclassical
and Keynesian models — which assume labour is scarce — give the wrong answers for
such economies. The essay splits the economy into a "capitalist" sector, which uses
reproducible capital and pays profit-seeking employers, and a "subsistence" sector,
where output per head is lower and a large fraction of workers (peasant smallholders,
casual labourers, petty traders, domestic retainers, household women) contribute
negligible, zero, or even negative marginal product. Because the capitalist sector can
draw labour from this reservoir at a wage set by subsistence earnings plus a fixed
premium, it can expand without bidding wages up; almost the entire gain from capital
accumulation and technical progress therefore accrues to the profit share, which is
reinvested, further expanding the capitalist sector, in a self-reinforcing cycle. The
closed-economy analysis (Part I) traces this mechanism, the financing of capital
formation via credit as well as profits, and the economic conditions under which the
cycle terminates once surplus labour is exhausted (real wages rise, and the model no
longer applies). The open-economy analysis (Part II) extends the same wage-
determination logic to migration, capital export, and international trade between
labour-surplus and labour-scarce countries, showing that under surplus labour the Law
of Comparative Costs still holds but average (not marginal) product governs wages and
the terms of trade, reversing some conventional free-trade conclusions. The essay is
a qualitative, comparative-statics account of a growth *mechanism*, not a quantitative
growth model; it derives no numerical growth rate, doubling time, or savings-rate
trajectory, only the direction and self-limiting logic of the process.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Lewis states at the outset that the essay is written "in the classical tradition,
making the classical assumption, and asking the classical question": the classical
economists (Smith through Marx) assumed an unlimited supply of labour at subsistence
wages and asked how production grows through capital accumulation, with income
distribution and income growth determined together. Neoclassical economics abandoned
the unlimited-labour assumption once labour became scarce in industrialising Europe,
and Keynes's *General Theory*, though it also assumes labour is elastic in supply at
the current wage, in fact presumes land and capital are the unlimited factors, so that
in Lewis's reading Keynesianism reduces to "a footnote to neo-classicism" for
labour-surplus economies. The stated purpose is to bring the classical
distribution-accumulation-growth framework up to date and apply it first to a closed
economy and then to an open one, in order to understand "the contemporary problems of
large areas of the earth" — explicitly Asia, and by extension other densely
populated, capital-poor regions (Egypt, India, Jamaica are named). Lewis is careful to
scope the assumption: it does not fit the United Kingdom, north-west Europe, or
regions with an acute shortage of male labour (parts of Africa and Latin America are
cited); the essay does not claim to supersede neoclassical economics, only to supply
an alternative framework where its assumptions do not hold.

The paper opens by enumerating where "surplus" labour is found in an over-populated
economy: family farms where a subset of members could leave without reducing output
(disguised unemployment in agriculture); casual dock labour, portering, and jobbing
work, where a large multiple of the needed workforce shares a small aggregate income;
petty retail trading, "enormously expanded" in over-populated economies with output
unaffected if the number of stalls were reduced; and domestic service and commercial
"retainers," inflated by a social convention that it is good form to offer as much
employment as one can (Lewis cites Barbados, where 16 percent of the population was in
domestic service). Three further sources are added: women drawn from household work
into paid employment as opportunities appear (illustrated by contrasting female
employment rates in 1939 Lancashire versus South Wales); natural population increase,
following Ricardo's use of the Malthusian mechanism, with the qualification that a
falling death rate under development is a more reliable dynamic driver than any change
in the birth rate; and Marx's "reserve army" of workers displaced by mechanization and
by larger capitalists driving out smaller ones — a channel Lewis says is empirically
rejected, since capital accumulation historically has shrunk, not enlarged, the
reserve army. Skilled labour is treated as at most a temporary "quasi-bottleneck,"
since capital availability will call forth training; the essay's persistent
bottlenecks are capital and natural resources.

### Methods and scope

The essay is a theoretical, essayistic argument rather than an empirical or
mathematically formalized one; Lewis states explicitly it is "not primarily an essay
in the history of economic doctrine" and does not attempt to adjudicate what earlier
writers "really meant." The analytical apparatus is a simple two-curve diagram
(Figures I and II in the original): the horizontal axis is quantity of labour, the
vertical axis the marginal product of labour in the capitalist sector for a given
capital stock. A horizontal wage line intersects the downward-sloping marginal-product
schedule; the capitalist sector hires labour up to that intersection, wages equal the
area under the wage line up to that point, and the "capitalists' surplus" is the
triangular area between the marginal-product curve and the wage line. Figure II adds
the subsistence wage floor and shows the capitalist wage sitting a fixed premium above
it, a discontinuity Lewis describes with the image of a "cliff" rather than a gradual
"beach" between the two labour markets. The dynamic argument is built by successive
comparative-statics steps: reinvested surplus raises the capital stock, which shifts
the marginal-product schedule outward, which enlarges both employment and the surplus
at the (unchanged) wage, and the process is said to repeat "so long as there is
surplus labour" — there is no formal differential-equation or dynamic-optimization
treatment of the time path.

Beyond the core diagram, the essay develops several supporting analytical excursions:
(1) a discussion, using illustrative round-number arithmetic rather than estimated
parameters, of how credit-financed (as opposed to profit-financed) capital formation
affects prices, distribution, and the length of time before a new price equilibrium is
reached, including a worked numerical example of an inflationary episode converging to
equilibrium over roughly a decade; (2) an analysis of government taxation's role in
"automatically" absorbing part of any monetary expansion, contrasting economies where
output/trade is easily taxed (mines, plantations, foreign trade) against economies
such as India where it is not; and (3) an open-economy section applying the same
wage-determination logic (subsistence/average product, not marginal product, sets the
wage) to migration, capital export, and several stylized trade cases under the
classical Law of Comparative Costs, including cases where the two trading countries
produce different goods, the same goods, or compete in third markets. Throughout, the
essay draws on illustrative, non-systematically-sourced real-world references rather
than a data set: Barbados domestic service ratios, U.K. regional female employment,
Soviet price inflation during forced collectivization (cited as roughly 700 percent
over a decade), the German hyperinflation of 1919, Puerto Rican migration to the
United States, tropical sugar and cocoa pricing, the destruction of the Indian cotton
handicraft industry by cheap imports, and — of direct relevance to FA1 — Japanese
farm rents and taxation between 1880 and 1910. The essay closes with the author's own
15-point numbered summary (Part III), which this review draws on for the recap in Key
findings below.

### Key findings

**The two-sector structure.** The economy is split into a capitalist sector (uses
reproducible capital, pays capitalists for its use, corresponds to Smith's
"productive" workers) and a subsistence sector (everything else, "unfructified" by
capital, lower output per head). Lewis stresses the capitalist sector is typically not
one contiguous zone but a scatter of highly capitalized "islands" (mining, a few
modern plantations, a handful of modern towns) surrounded by "economic darkness,"
held together as a single sector mainly because competition tends to equalize the
marginal (not average) return on capital across the islands.

**Constant-wage labour supply.** Because large parts of the subsistence and casual
sectors show negligible, zero, or negative marginal product, the capitalist sector can
draw additional workers at a wage that does not rise with the quantity hired — Lewis's
"unlimited supply of labour... at the existing wage." The wage floor is set either by
a conventional subsistence-consumption standard or, where peasants own or rent land,
by the average (not marginal) product of the family farm; in practice the capitalist
wage sits about 30 percent or more above subsistence earnings, a premium Lewis
attributes to a mix of higher urban living costs, the psychological cost of
resettlement, higher usefulness of experienced over raw recruits, and the acquisition
of "conventional" urban consumption standards — and this gap persists even without
trade unions.

**The accumulation engine.** "The key to the process is the use which is made of the
capitalist surplus. In so far as this is reinvested in creating new capital, the
capitalist sector expands, taking more people... into capitalist employment out of
the subsistence sector. The surplus is then larger still, capital formation is still
greater, and so the process continues until the labour surplus disappears." Because
the wage stays essentially fixed while output per worker in the capitalist sector
rises with each round of investment, virtually the whole benefit of capital
accumulation and technical progress accrues to profits rather than wages, so the
profit share of national income rises continuously as the capitalist sector expands.
Lewis uses this to answer why savings are low in backward economies: not because
people are poor in general, but because the profit-earning capitalist sector is a
small share of the whole economy; as that sector grows, so does the aggregate savings
rate (he frames the central empirical puzzle of development as the transition from a
4-5 percent to a 12-15 percent net-saving economy, and argues this shift is explained
by the rising share of income captured by the small, high-saving profit-receiving
class, not by any general rise in thrift). Reinvestment can occur out of profits or
out of newly created bank credit; when surplus labour and idle land/resources permit
labour to build capital goods (roads, irrigation works, buildings) without diverting
resources from consumer-goods output, credit-financed capital formation raises prices
temporarily (redistributing, not reducing, consumption) but the inflation is
self-limiting, since rising output from the new capital eventually catches up with and
then can undercut the price rise.

**The Lewis turning point.** The essay is explicit that the process "cannot go on
forever." The underlying condition is that capital accumulation eventually catches up
with the size of the labour-surplus population, after which real wages begin to rise
above the subsistence-plus-premium floor and the constant-wage assumption fails. Lewis
identifies four economic mechanisms by which the wage can start rising even before
literal exhaustion of surplus labour: (1) if capital accumulates faster than
population grows, the shrinking number of people left in the subsistence sector
mechanically raises average (and eventually marginal) product per head there, pulling
the wage floor up; (2) if the capitalist sector does not itself produce food, its
expansion raises the price of food relative to capitalist output (adverse terms of
trade), squeezing profits to protect the real wage; (3) the subsistence sector may
itself become more productive as capitalist techniques (new seeds, fertilizer,
infrastructure spillovers) diffuse outward, raising the wage floor directly unless
offset by a corresponding fall in the price of subsistence output; and (4) capitalist
sector workers may raise their own conventional consumption standard (via union
pressure or employer paternalism), widening what counts as an acceptable wage. Once
one or more of these operate strongly enough, or once the labour surplus is
genuinely exhausted, wages rise above subsistence, the capitalist surplus (and hence
the rate of reinvestment) is squeezed, and — in Lewis's words — "our model of the
closed economy no longer holds," at which point standard marginal-productivity (or
monopoly) wage theory takes over; the essay explicitly declines to pursue that
subsequent regime, calling it "outside the terms of reference of this essay."

**The Japan case, cited by Lewis himself.** In discussing how capitalists can prevent
the turning point from arriving prematurely, Lewis gives Japan as a direct historical
illustration of mechanism (3) being suppressed: agricultural productivity in Japan
roughly doubled between 1880 and 1910, but landlords and the state raised farm rents
and taxes so that "a large part of the rapid increase in productivity... was taken
away from the farmers and used for capital formation," while simultaneously holding
down farm household income kept the capitalist-sector wage floor from rising — to the
advantage of profits and capital accumulation in the modern sector. Lewis draws an
explicit parallel to the U.S.S.R., where farm incomes were similarly held down despite
mechanization, via manufactured-goods/farm-goods price manipulation and heavy taxation
of collective farms. This passage is the essay's own statement of the mechanism that
FA1's M4 (Japan's rural-to-urban structural transition) identifies as operating during
Japan's high-growth period: a subsistence/agricultural sector whose productivity gains
were captured by rent and tax policy rather than passed through as higher rural
incomes, sustaining a roughly constant urban-sector wage floor and channeling the
resulting surplus into capital formation.

**Open economy: exporting the same logic.** Once a country's domestic surplus is
exhausted and wages start to rise, capitalists can restart the constant-wage dynamic
by drawing on surplus labour elsewhere, via mass immigration (which pulls the
receiving country's wage toward the sending country's subsistence level, in contrast
to small-scale migration, which instead pulls the sending country's wage up) or via
capital export to a labour-surplus country (which slows domestic capital formation and
wage growth at home but need not reduce home welfare if the exported capital cheapens
goods the home country imports). Under trade between a labour-scarce and a
labour-surplus country, Lewis shows that wages and the terms of trade are governed by
average product in the surplus country's subsistence sector rather than by marginal
product, which is "the most serious difference which the existence of surplus labour
makes to the neo-classical theory of international trade": the Law of Comparative
Costs remains valid, but money costs computed at going wages give the wrong
specialization signal, and — unlike the neoclassical case — this is a valid analytical
foundation for tariff protection, not free trade, in a labour-surplus economy. A
related result explains persistently cheap tropical export commodities (sugar is the
worked example): because wages in export agriculture are tied to low subsistence food
productivity, gains in export-crop productivity accrue mainly to foreign consumers via
lower prices rather than to local wages, unless subsistence food productivity itself
rises.

**No numerical growth rate.** The essay is a *mechanism* paper, not a growth-rate
model: it never derives, and does not attempt to derive, a specific accumulation
rate, capital-output ratio dynamic, or doubling time. Its content is (a) an
identification of a self-sustaining accumulation feedback loop — reinvested profit
raises capital, which raises the surplus (because the offsetting factor price, the
wage, does not move), which raises reinvestment — and (b) an account of the economic
conditions under which that feedback loop is bounded in time, once the constant-price
supply of the enabling factor (labour, in this essay) runs out.

**Explicit negation for the Moon (FA2 framing).** The dual-economy mechanism has no
direct lunar analogue: the entire argument depends on a subsistence/agrarian sector
that (i) holds a large reservoir of labour at zero or negligible marginal product and
(ii) can release it to a modern sector at a wage that does not rise with the quantity
drawn. A lunar industrial base has no agrarian hinterland and no population reservoir
of this kind to draw on — there is no subsistence sector, no peasant labour supply,
and no "average product of the family farm" setting a wage floor. The mechanism
therefore cannot be imported wholesale as the engine of any lunar growth acceleration.
The open question this review flags for the synthesis is what, if anything, plays the
structural role of Lewis's surplus sector on the Moon — that is, a factor available in
large quantity at a roughly constant "price" that a growing sector can draw on without
bidding that price up, enabling a rising profit/reinvestment share the way surplus
labour did for Lewis's capitalist sector. The ledger's working candidate is *idle
capital capacity* — underutilized reproducible capital (manufacturing, energy,
robotic/automated labour substitutes) rather than surplus human labour — but this
substitution is not something Lewis's own text supports or tests; it is a structural
analogy proposed for the lunar case, flagged here as unresolved. FA2's use of this
paper is accordingly narrow and specific: it is not a source for the 41-percent/year
or 24-month-doubling arithmetic (the paper produces no growth-rate arithmetic at all)
but for the *qualitative form* of a self-sustaining accumulation mechanism — a rising
profit share, driven by an elastically supplied factor whose price does not rise with
quantity used, that funds its own further expansion until the enabling condition is
exhausted. Whatever plays that role for the lunar case (if anything does) would need
to satisfy the same two conditions Lewis's subsistence sector satisfies here: large
quantity relative to near-term demand, and a supply price that stays roughly constant
as it is drawn down. The Moon manifestly fails this in the labour dimension; whether
idle capital capacity satisfies it is a question for FA6, not for this paper.

### Limitations

- The essay is a discursive theoretical argument, not a formal mathematical growth
  model: the core mechanism is illustrated with a static marginal-product diagram
  applied repeatedly, not a differential equation or optimization framework with an
  explicit time path, so it does not itself produce a growth rate, a savings-rate
  trajectory, or a date for the turning point.
- No econometric or empirical testing is performed; supporting facts (Barbados
  domestic-service shares, U.K. regional female employment, Japanese farm rents and
  taxes 1880-1910, Soviet price inflation, German hyperinflation, sugar productivity
  trends) are cited illustratively, without systematic sourcing or a data appendix.
- Key parameters are asserted rather than derived: the roughly 30 percent
  capitalist-wage premium over subsistence earnings is described as a plausible
  observed range, not estimated from data; the assumption that marginal product in the
  subsistence/casual sectors is zero or negligible is adopted as a simplifying
  premise, and Lewis notes it is not universally true even within the paper's own
  scope (he excludes the U.K., north-west Europe, and labour-scarce parts of Africa
  and Latin America).
- The analysis assumes profit-maximizing, "commercially minded" capitalists distinct
  from employers who retain workers as quasi-dependents; Lewis flags this as a
  deliberate simplification needed to make the wage-equals-marginal-product assumption
  usable, acknowledging it does not describe all employers in these economies.
  Similarly, the treatment of capital and technical knowledge as a single undivided
  variable is adopted for analytical convenience, not derived from a production
  function that separates them.
- The credit/inflation and taxation sections use worked illustrative arithmetic
  (specific hypothetical investment and profit figures) rather than an estimated or
  calibrated model, and the author himself notes the "alleged precision" of the
  multiplier arithmetic is "spurious," even while defending the qualitative
  conclusion.
- The open-economy trade analysis relies on stylized two-good, two-country cases
  (e.g., "wheat and peanuts," "steel and rubber") to establish qualitative
  comparative-cost results; it is not calibrated to any actual bilateral trade
  relationship.
- The essay explicitly stops at the turning point: once surplus labour is exhausted
  and wages begin to rise on a sustained basis, Lewis states the resulting wage-setting
  regime is "outside the terms of reference of this essay" and does not analyze it,
  so the paper offers no account of a mature, labour-scarce growth phase.
- As the seminal statement of the dual-economy idea, the paper precedes (and is less
  formally rigorous than) the later two-sector growth models it inspired (e.g.,
  Fei-Ranis), which built explicit dynamic and quantitative versions of the same
  mechanism; this review covers only the 1954 essay itself, not its formalized
  successors.

no growth-rate arithmetic and is not a candidate source for the 41-percent/year or
24-month-doubling verdict; its contribution to the FA2 verdict table is identifying
the qualitative *form* of a self-sustaining accumulation mechanism — an elastically
supplied factor at a roughly constant price funding a rising, reinvested profit share
until that factor is exhausted — and establishing that its enabling condition, a
subsistence/agrarian labour reservoir, has no counterpart on the Moon.
