# Industrialization and the Big Push

## Citation

Murphy, K. M., Shleifer, A., & Vishny, R. W. (1989). Industrialization and the big
push. *Journal of Political Economy, 97*(5), 1003-1026.

## Abstract

The paper formalizes Rosenstein-Rodan's proposition that simultaneous
industrialization across many sectors of an economy can be profitable for all of
them even when no single sector could break even industrializing alone. Working in
an imperfectly competitive setting with aggregate demand spillovers, the authors
recast the big push as a move from a low ("bad") to a high ("good") Nash
equilibrium of the same fixed economy — no change in endowments, technology, or
preferences is required, only coordinated investment. Three distinct mechanisms
capable of generating this multiplicity are presented, each turning on a way in
which a firm's investment can raise other sectors' profits even while losing money
itself, and the paper closes by discussing the mechanisms' relevance to
less-developed economies and to government coordination policy.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens from an empirical observation: essentially every country that
achieved sustained gains in productivity and living standards over the preceding
two centuries did so by industrializing — moving production toward manufactures
that exploit increasing-returns (scale) technologies — yet many countries that had
the same technological opportunity available to them never made the transition.
The question the authors pose is why industrialization is not universal, and
whether government intervention can accelerate it.

Their answer centers on the size of the domestic market as the binding constraint.
When the domestic market is small and trade is neither free nor costless, no single
firm may be able to generate enough sales to cover the fixed costs of adopting an
increasing-returns technology, so industrialization stalls even though it would be
collectively profitable. This is the analytical content the authors attach to
Rosenstein-Rodan's (1943) "big push": if many sectors adopted increasing-returns
technology simultaneously, each would generate income that becomes a source of
demand for the others, expanding every sector's market and making the joint move
self-sustaining even though no sector could justify moving alone. The paper credits
Nurkse (1953), Scitovsky (1954), and Fleming (1955) with developing this into a
"balanced growth" doctrine with two defining features: the same economy must be
capable of both the backward and the industrialized state without any exogenous
improvement in endowments or technology, and the industrialized state must be a
Pareto improvement.

The authors' stated objective is to give this doctrine a precise, model-based
answer to two questions: what does it take for multiple equilibria — one
non-industrialized, one industrialized — to exist in a formal economy, and when is
the industrialized equilibrium actually Pareto-preferred to the alternative. They
locate the source of multiplicity specifically in pecuniary externalities generated
by imperfect competition together with large fixed costs, and they are explicit
that this is a different mechanism from the technological (knowledge) externalities
used in contemporaneous endogenous-growth models such as Romer (1986a) and Lucas
(1988); the two are complementary but conceptually distinct sources of increasing
aggregate returns.

Before building the models, the paper marshals evidence — mainly from Chenery and
Syrquin (1975) and Chenery, Robinson, and Syrquin (1986) — that domestic demand,
not exports, has historically accounted for the bulk of growth in domestic
industrial output. In countries with populations over 20 million, domestic-demand
expansion explains 72-74 percent of the growth in industrial output; even in small,
manufacturing-oriented countries it explains roughly 50-60 percent; and in
Korea — held up as the paradigm export-oriented economy — domestic-demand
expansion still accounted for 53 percent of industrial output growth between 1955
and 1973, with intensive export orientation emerging only after industries were
already established domestically. This evidence is used to justify treating
domestic-market size, rather than the world market, as the operative constraint in
the models that follow.

### Methods and scope

All models are static or two-period general-equilibrium constructions, deliberately
stylized rather than calibrated or estimated. The common skeleton: a representative
consumer with Cobb-Douglas (unit-elastic) preferences defined over a continuum of
goods on the unit interval, inelastically supplying a fixed labor endowment L and
owning all profits. Each good can be produced either by a competitive fringe using
a constant-returns-to-scale ("cottage") technology (one unit of labor to one unit
of output, wage normalized to 1), or by a single potential monopolist in that
sector with access to an increasing-returns-to-scale ("factory"/mass-production)
technology, which requires a fixed setup cost of F units of labor and thereafter
converts each unit of labor into α > 1 units of output. Because the monopolist
would lose the market to the fringe by pricing above 1 and has no incentive to
price below it under unit-elastic demand, price is pinned at 1 whenever a
monopolist operates, and each monopolist's investment decision reduces to a
zero/one choice: industrialize only if expected aggregate income makes the fixed
cost profitable to incur.

Four variants of this skeleton are analyzed in turn:

- **Section III — baseline, unique-equilibrium benchmark.** Profit spillovers exist
  (a monopolist's distributed profits raise other sectors' demand), but this is the
  only channel. Used to establish what is *not* sufficient for a big push.
- **Section IV — factory wage premium.** Factory work carries a disutility v that
  must be compensated with a wage premium over the cottage wage, so factories pay
  w = 1 + v. The extra wage bill, not just distributed profits, is the spillover
  channel.
- **Section V — dynamic (two-period) investment.** The fixed cost F is sunk in
  period 1; the productivity gain α is realized in period 2. An investing firm
  shifts the composition of aggregate demand from period 1 to period 2 regardless
  of its own net present value.
- **Section VI — shared infrastructure (the railroad case).** A fixed-cost piece of
  infrastructure (modeled on a railroad) must be built before industrial goods can
  reach the market; each additional industrializing user helps defray the
  infrastructure's fixed cost R and thereby lowers effective costs for every other
  user.

Across all four, the method is closed-form comparative statics: derive the
condition under which "no sector invests" is self-consistent (income stays at L,
and at that income no monopolist can break even), derive the separate condition
under which "all sectors invest" is self-consistent (income rises enough that every
monopolist's investment is profitable given everyone else's), and ask whether both
conditions can hold simultaneously for a common range of parameters (fixed cost F,
wage premium v, productivity gain α, market size L, infrastructure cost R). Where
both hold, the economy admits two Pareto-rankable Nash equilibria and a "big push"
— coordinated simultaneous investment — is the mechanism that selects the higher
one. The paper does not solve for a transition path or a growth rate between the
two equilibria; each model is a comparison of two static (or two-period) resting
points of the same economy, not a dynamical system with a computed growth rate.
Footnote citations throughout connect the pecuniary-externality mechanism to
related macro/IO literature (Hart 1982; Weitzman 1982; Kiyotaki 1988) and
distinguish it from the technological-externality growth models of Romer and
Lucas. Section VII draws out policy implications, with South Korea's coordinated
industrial-investment programs cited as a real-world illustration.

### Key findings

**The central formal result.** Industrialization can be a Pareto-preferred Nash
equilibrium of an economy with unchanged technology, endowments, and preferences,
coexisting with a Pareto-inferior equilibrium in which no sector industrializes —
provided there exists a channel through which one sector's investment raises the
profitability of investment in other sectors even when that investment is
individually unprofitable. This is the paper's formalization of Rosenstein-Rodan's
big push: not a technology shock, but a coordination problem with two stable
resting points for the same primitives.

**Why a demand-spillover story is not automatically enough (Section III).** The
paper first shows a negative/benchmark result: if a firm's only effect on other
sectors' markets runs through its own distributed profits, the economy has a
*unique* equilibrium — either every sector industrializes or none does. The logic:
if a single firm cannot break even at the low, non-industrialized income level, its
investment (were it to occur) would only reduce aggregate income, which makes every
other sector's investment even less attractive, ruling out a second equilibrium
entirely. The authors state explicitly that the conditions required for genuinely
multiple equilibria are "more stringent than those loosely expressed" in the
1940s-50s big-push literature — profit spillovers alone, without a wedge between a
firm's own profitability and its contribution to others' demand, cannot produce a
big push.

**Mechanism 1 — the factory wage premium (Section IV).** To draw workers out of
cottage production, factories must pay a compensating wage w = 1 + v. Because that
extra wage income is spent on goods across the economy regardless of whether the
paying firm itself turns a profit, an unprofitable factory can still raise
aggregate demand for other sectors' output. A necessary condition for the model to
be interesting at all is that the productivity gain exceed the wage premium,
α − 1 > v (eq. 8); otherwise no factory could break even at any income level. Given
that, the no-industrialization equilibrium requires that a lone factory could not
break even at income L (eq. 9), while the industrialized equilibrium requires that
every factory earns a positive profit once all sectors have industrialized and
income has risen accordingly (eq. 10). The paper shows that for a nonempty range of
the fixed cost F, both conditions hold simultaneously — a genuine big push — and
that the industrialized equilibrium is strictly Pareto superior: prices are
unchanged, workers are no worse off as wage earners, and the population additionally
shares in higher aggregate profits.

**Mechanism 2 — intertemporal demand-composition shift (Section V).** In a
two-period version, the fixed cost F is paid in period 1 and the productivity gain
α is realized in period 2. Even an investment with negative net present value
transfers spending power from period 1 (irrelevant to other firms' period-2
decisions) to period 2 (which is exactly what raises the profitability of other
firms selling in period 2). The no-industrialization equilibrium requires that a
lone firm's expected period-2 income (still just L) leaves it unable to break even
(eq. 15); the industrialized equilibrium requires that, given the higher period-2
income generated by everyone investing together (income rises to αL, eq. 17) and
the associated equilibrium interest-rate adjustment, the discounted investment is
worthwhile (eq. 19). The authors connect this mechanism to a historical account of
"bullish" nineteenth-century American entrepreneurs (Sawyer 1954, describing an
"economics of euphoria") but show explicitly that self-fulfilling growth
expectations of this kind do not require any entrepreneurial irrationality — fully
rational agents coordinating on the high-investment equilibrium reproduce the same
outcome. A further, more technical point: the paper shows multiplicity and Pareto
ranking are driven by distinct features of the model (gross cash flows across
periods versus the relative size of markups/multipliers in each period), so a
second, less efficient high-investment equilibrium is possible in principle if
first-period markups are large enough (Section V, footnote 10) — multiplicity does
not automatically imply the industrialized equilibrium is the better one, although
it is in the paper's baseline dynamic model.

**Mechanism 3 — shared infrastructure (Section VI, the railroad case).** Here the
spillover runs through a fixed-cost piece of shared infrastructure (modeled as a
railroad) that all industrializing sectors must use to reach the market. Because
its cost is fixed, each additional industrializing user helps defray it and lowers
the effective cost of using it for everyone else — a second channel, distinct from
demand spillovers, through which one sector's industrialization can make another
sector's industrialization more attractive. This section also isolates two
*additional*, non-coordination reasons an efficient infrastructure project can
still fail to be built: (1) if the infrastructure operator cannot price-discriminate
across users with heterogeneous fixed costs, it can extract only part of the social
surplus its investment generates, so a project that would be efficient overall
(condition 22) may still not be privately profitable to build (condition 21 fails)
— a classic natural-monopoly underinvestment problem layered on top of the
coordination problem; and (2) even when both conditions could in principle be
satisfied once the infrastructure exists, its financiers may simply refuse to build
it for fear of landing in the bad (no-industrialization) equilibrium and getting no
customers — a pure equilibrium-selection risk, illustrated by the "white elephant"
outcome. The paper extends the logic to other lumpy infrastructure — power
stations, roads, airports, and (citing Rosenstein-Rodan 1961) training facilities —
arguing that training facilities in particular are underprovided both because firms
cannot prevent trained workers from being poached and because workers cannot
discover their comparative advantage without a broad range of industries already
present to employ them.

**The common wedge.** Across all three generating mechanisms, the necessary
ingredient is the same: a firm's own profit must understate its true contribution
to the demand (or cost reduction) available to other sectors — via extra wages
paid, via future cash flow that lifts next period's spending, or via fixed costs
defrayed for shared infrastructure. Whenever that wedge collapses to zero (as in
the Section III benchmark, where a firm's only channel is its own profit), the
equilibrium is unique and no big push is possible.

**Welfare and policy.** In every generating model, the industrialized equilibrium
is shown to be Pareto-preferred to the non-industrialized one, though the analysis
is explicitly second-best: monopoly pricing distortions in the industrial sectors
are taken as given throughout, and the paper's claim is only that coordinated
investment reaches the better of two feasible equilibria, not a first-best outcome.
The policy implication drawn is that government's most useful role is coordination
of investment across sectors — persuasion, or investment subsidies spread widely
enough to reach a critical mass — rather than picking individually profitable
projects; South Korea's coordinated industrial-investment programs are cited as
having achieved industrialization at a lower explicit subsidy cost than piecemeal
industrialization elsewhere, because large *implicit* subsidies flow across sectors
through the demand spillovers once coordination succeeds.

**What this paper is not.** It is not a growth-rate model and produces no
computed annual growth rate, doubling time, or transition-path dynamics; each
mechanism is a comparison between two static or two-period equilibria of a fixed
economy, not a trajectory. Its contribution is a precondition result — the
algebraic conditions (eqs. 8-10, 15-19, 20-23) under which industrialization
*requires* coordinated, simultaneous investment across sectors rather than being
achievable by any single firm acting alone, and the identification of the specific
channels (wage premium, intertemporal cash flow, shared fixed-cost infrastructure)
through which that coordination requirement arises.

**FA2/thesis framing.** The Moon is close to a textbook illustration of the
condition this paper formalizes. No market exists for lunar power, propellant,
structural material, or transport services in isolation: a propellant-production
firm has no customers until a transport operator exists to buy propellant, the
transport operator has no business case until structural-material and power
producers exist to ship for, and none of them can break even alone at the small,
near-zero starting scale of demand, in the same structural sense that a lone
factory cannot break even in Section III's baseline or Section IV's wage-premium
model when aggregate income is stuck at L. The paper's formal apparatus — the
demonstration that a Pareto-preferred "all sectors invest" equilibrium can coexist
with a "no sector invests" equilibrium, and that reaching the better one requires
simultaneous rather than sequential investment — is the theoretical argument for
why a lunar industrial economy needs a coordinated, multi-sector investment launch
rather than an organic, one-firm-at-a-time emergence of the kind that characterizes
terrestrial market entry into an already-thick economy. The specific conditions the
paper derives (the productivity-gain-versus-wage-premium condition of eq. 8, the
market-size thresholds of eqs. 9-10, and the infrastructure cost-recovery
conditions of eqs. 20-22) supply a template for the kind of threshold conditions a
lunar techno-economic model would need to derive for its own candidate sectors —
though translating them requires substituting the Moon's absent labor market and
wage mechanism with whatever plays the analogous coordinating role (see Limitations
below, and FA2 Sub-Q 4 on the missing labor-supply term). This paper supplies the
coordination-precondition row of the FA2 verdict table, not a growth-rate row: it
answers "does industrialization here require a big push," not "how fast could it
grow once launched."

### Limitations

- All models are highly stylized comparative-statics exercises with closed-form
  algebraic conditions; none is calibrated or econometrically estimated against
  real economies, and the authors state their own caution that "policies suggested
  by these models should be interpreted with caution."
- Demand is unit-elastic (Cobb-Douglas) throughout the core models; the authors
  note in a footnote that price-elastic demand for manufactures has historically
  been important to industrial growth (citing Deane 1979) and would add a further,
  unmodeled consumer-surplus channel reinforcing the big push.
- The externalities modeled are exclusively pecuniary (operating through market
  size and prices under fixed costs and imperfect competition); technological
  externalities such as learning or knowledge spillovers (the Romer 1986a/Lucas
  1988 family) are explicitly set aside as a distinct mechanism, so the paper does
  not adjudicate between the two as explanations of actual industrialization
  episodes.
- Section V's own footnote 10 shows that multiplicity of equilibria and their
  Pareto ranking are driven by different features of the model (gross
  intertemporal cash flows versus relative markups/multipliers across periods), so
  the result that the industrialized equilibrium is always the better one is not
  fully general even within the paper's own dynamic variant.
- Monopoly pricing distortion in the industrial sector is taken as given
  throughout; all welfare comparisons are explicitly second-best (industrialized
  vs. non-industrialized equilibrium), not benchmarked against a first-best
  allocation.
- The railroad/infrastructure model relies on strong informational and commitment
  assumptions — the infrastructure operator cannot observe individual firms' fixed
  costs but can credibly commit to a second-period price before firms invest —
  which may not hold for real infrastructure financing.
- The paper's own empirical content is limited to citing Chenery-Syrquin stylized
  facts on the domestic-demand share of industrial-output growth to motivate the
  domestic-market-size framing; it does not test the equilibrium-selection
  predictions of its own models against data, and does not claim to.
- No labor-market or population dynamics are modeled beyond a fixed endowment L
  supplied inelastically each period; the mechanisms (especially the wage-premium
  model of Section IV) are built around a rural-urban/cottage-factory wage
  differential that presupposes an existing labor force choosing between two
  employment modes — a structure with no direct analogue on the Moon, where there
  is no comparable labor supply to draw from or wage-differential mechanism to
  operate.
- A companion paper by the same authors (Murphy, Shleifer, and Vishny, "Income
  Distribution, Market Size, and Industrialization," *Quarterly Journal of
  Economics*, 1989) is cited for extensions, including an agricultural sector, that
  are not developed in this paper.
- The paper offers no numerical estimate of how large a "big push" investment
  program would need to be in absolute terms for any real economy; all results are
  qualitative threshold conditions on ratios of parameters (F, v, α, L, R), not
  point estimates.
