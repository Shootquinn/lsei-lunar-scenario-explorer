## Abstract

First-person account by W. Edwards Deming of the introduction of statistical quality control
(SQC) methods into Japanese industry beginning in 1950, presented as a retrospective explanation
of Japanese manufacturers' rapid gains in product quality and productivity.

Covers Deming's 1950 lectures and eight-day courses to Japanese management, engineers, and
statisticians; enumerates nine factors he attributes to the speed of adoption; defines the
"statistical control of quality" and its design-make-sell-test improvement cycle; distinguishes
special (assignable, local) from common (general, management-level) causes of variation; and
reports illustrative early productivity results from named-industry, unidentified-company
Japanese firms.

Method is practitioner testimony and case narrative, not a data study. Scope is limited to
quality-control methodology and its management/educational diffusion, not macroeconomic or
industrial-policy mechanisms.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The article is authored by W. Edwards Deming, identified as a consultant in statistical surveys
based in Washington, D.C. It is dedicated to Walter A. Shewhart (1891-1967), originator of the
statistical control chart, whom Deming credits as the source of the broad definition of
statistical quality control used throughout.

Deming states the piece draws in part on an earlier article he published in the Indian
statistics journal Sankhya (Calcutta), series B, volume 28, 1966.

His stated objective is to offer observations, from the standpoint of statistical control of
quality, on the causes of the competitive success of Japanese manufactured products, in the hope
that similar application of statistical technique elsewhere — explicitly including the United
States — might have a comparably healthy impact. He further suggests that appreciation of what
happened in Japan should be taken seriously by scientific and professional societies concerned
with statistical methods in production.

Deming frames the account around his own direct role: he was invited to lecture to Japanese top
management and to teach statistical methods to engineers and statisticians beginning in 1950, and
the article is built around what he personally observed and taught in that capacity.

### Methods and scope

The article is a first-person narrative and methodological exposition, not an empirical or
comparative study. There is no dataset, survey instrument, or systematic measurement described.
Evidence takes three forms:

- (a) Deming's own recollection of lectures and eight-day courses delivered in Japan starting in
  1950, including a specific prediction he made at a Tokyo assembly of manufacturers in July 1950;
- (b) a small number of named-industry (but not named-company) production results reported to him
  after roughly one year of application, covering steel, pharmaceuticals, and cable manufacturing;
- (c) exposition of the underlying statistical concepts themselves — control charts, acceptance
  sampling, common/special-cause analysis, and consumer-research sampling.

The scope is confined to statistical quality control as a management and engineering discipline
and its transmission into Japanese industry via private lectures and courses. It does not address
government industrial policy, sector financing arrangements, trade policy, or macroeconomic growth
data of the kind found in the broader postwar-Japan economic literature.

The intended audience is readers of Industrial Quality Control, the journal in which the article
appeared (reprint of Vol. 24, No. 2, August 1967, pp. 89-93) — a professional/technical quality-
engineering audience rather than an economics readership. Deming explicitly disclaims subject-
matter authority outside statistics, stating he is "not an economist, nor a business-man, only a
statistician."

### Key findings

**Nine reasons Deming gives for the speed and success of statistical quality control's adoption
in Japan:**

1. Genuine and resolute determination on the part of management to improve quality.
2. Confidence — in the ability to lead Japanese industry forth from a prior bad reputation for
   quality, confidence in Japanese scientific ability, confidence in Japanese skills, and
   confidence in statistical methods themselves.
3. Workers and managers were Japanese, with existing industrial experience and, in Deming's
   phrasing, an inbred pride of workmanship.
4. Japanese top management, statisticians, and engineers learned the statistical control of
   quality in the broad Shewhart sense, not merely as an isolated technique.
5. Management took immediate interest, learned the techniques, their likely results, and —
   notably — their own management responsibilities; early direct contact between the training and
   top management is cited as one of the fortunate features of the Japanese program.
6. Statistical education became a continuing process rather than a one-time installation. Deming
   identifies the "eight-day courses that began in 1950" as the primary vehicle, and states their
   main accomplishment was instilling inspiration to keep learning rather than delivering any
   single fixed body of technical content.
7. Japanese practitioners learned to distinguish a statistical problem from a problem of
   engineering, chemistry, management, or marketing — i.e., statistical knowledge was not treated
   as a substitute for subject-matter knowledge, nor vice versa.
8. Japanese manufacturers financed and organized the effort themselves, without seeking help from
   their own government or from the U.S. government. Consultation was arranged and paid for
   privately ("they sent a ticket and a cheque"), with financial and moral support for statistical
   education channeled mainly through the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE).
9. Suggestions and technical information moved through a comparatively clear channel from lower to
   higher levels of supervision and management; Deming states a Japanese executive was "never too
   old or too successful" to consider a better way of doing something.

**Recognition mechanism.** An annual prize — named, per an editor's footnote, after Deming
himself, though the body text describes it only as a prize offered "in the name of an American
statistician" — was awarded to the Japanese manufacturer judged by a Committee on Awards to have
made the greatest advance in product quality during the preceding calendar year. Deming states
many companies competed for the prize, sometimes planning years in advance, and that the
continual competition among many firms — not solely the one or two annual winners — had an
important leavening effect on quality broadly.

**1950 prediction and its stated fulfillment.** At an assembly of Japanese manufacturers in Tokyo
in July 1950, Deming predicted that within five years manufacturers in other industrial nations
would be placed on the defensive by Japanese competition, and that within ten years a reputation
for top-quality Japanese products would be firmly established the world over. The article asserts
this timetable was met, stating "the whole world knows how well Japanese manufacturers met the
predicted time-table."

**Early productivity/quality results** (reported as achieved after only about one year of
applying the methods, in unnamed companies within named industries):

- A steel company reduced coal consumption per ton of steel produced by 28 percent.
- A large pharmaceutical company achieved three times as much finished product per unit of
  raw-material input.
- A large cable company substantially reduced the amount of paper used and the amount of rework
  required on insulated wire and cable.
- Multiple companies reduced workplace accidents to a permanently lower level.

Deming characterizes these gains as achieved largely "with little new machinery" — i.e.,
attributed to management and process improvement rather than new capital investment.

**Definition and improvement cycle.** The 1950 definition of "the statistical control of quality,"
which Deming states was displayed in large letters and never departed from in the Japanese
program, reads: statistical control of quality is the application of statistical principles and
techniques in all stages of production, directed toward the economic manufacture of a product
that is maximally useful and has a market.

This was operationalized as a continuing cycle taught in Japan: (1) design a product, (2) make
it, (3) try to sell it, (4) test it in service, then (5)-(7) repeat steps 1 through 3, with the
product redesigned on the basis of the service tests. A simple accompanying diagram depicts this
as a closed loop among "Design," "Make," "Sell," and "Test/Service" stages, and is fully described
in the surrounding prose; no quantitative content appears in the figure beyond what the text
states, so no separate visual analysis was required for this summary.

The definition was also translated into eight concrete actions taught as part of the program:
statistical construction of specifications for materials and finished product; assistance to
suppliers/vendors on incoming-material quality; statistical control of process (via X- and
R-charts, run charts, and design of experiments), with responsibility for special causes assigned
locally and responsibility for common causes assigned to upper management; use of acceptance
sampling where appropriate; consumer research and in-service testing of product; product
redesign; laboratory and in-service testing of new product; and use of inventory/distribution
theory to find economical levels of stock.

**Special versus common causes of variation.** The article restates the Shewhart-derived
distinction central to the training: special (assignable) causes of variability are typically
local and correctable by an operator or foreman, whereas common (general) causes are systemic and
correctable only by higher management. Examples of common causes listed include poor light,
unsuitable humidity, vibration, poor instruction or supervision, lack of management interest in a
quality program, poor food in the cafeteria, inept management, raw materials/procedures/machines
not suited to requirements, and mixing of production streams with differing underlying levels.

Deming argues that confusing the two — blaming a specific worker or machine for a problem that is
actually systemic — was, and in his view remained, one of the more serious and costly mistakes of
industrial (and public) administration, and that Japanese management was specifically trained to
route responsibility correctly between local and higher levels rather than defaulting to blaming
the local operator.

**Analogy outside manufacturing.** As an example of common-cause improvement outside industrial
production, Deming cites the U.S. Census Bureau in Washington, stating that sustained, long-term
attack on common causes of error and cost (alongside elimination of special causes) had produced
statistical series whose quality, reliability, and speed were, by the time of writing, "the envy
of other statistical organizations in the U.S. and abroad," achieved at a cost he estimates at
about a third of what private industry in the United States pays for comparable consumer-research
surveys.

**Consumer research component.** Small sample surveys of household inventories and requirements —
covering pharmaceuticals, sewing machines, bicycles, and similar goods — were conducted in the
summer of 1951 as part of the sampling course. Deming states the Japanese themselves designated
these as the first studies in consumer research carried out by Japanese companies with the aid of
modern sampling methods.

**Acceptance sampling.** Presented as a scheme of protection, contingent on genuine enforcement
(rejecting and screening a lot when the sample contains more than the allowable number of
defects), requiring as part of any workable contract between vendor and purchaser agreed
specifications for lot size and disposition of defective units. The article notes acceptance
sampling was frequently confused, especially early on in America, with process control, with some
practitioners treating it mistakenly as a detector of special causes and others treating it as an
estimator of overall lot quality or as a means of separating good lots from bad.

**Statistical estimation.** Deming distinguishes problems of estimation (e.g., determining whether
one machine or process is different enough from another to justify a cost, or determining the
iron content of a shipload of ore) from problems of detecting a special cause, noting that
estimation problems concern the size of a known difference rather than whether a difference
exists at all, with risk of error weighed against the economic cost of unwarranted versus
foregone changes.

### Limitations

The account is a first-person, non-empirical narrative by the practitioner who both delivered the
training being described and — per the editorial footnote — is the "American statistician" for
whom the described quality prize was named. The source is therefore a direct participant's own
retrospective assessment of his program's effects, rather than an independent evaluation.

Quantitative results are limited to a handful of illustrative, unnamed-company figures (the 28
percent coal-consumption saving, the threefold raw-material output ratio, and the paper/rework
reduction), reported without disclosed methodology, verification, comparison baselines, or
sourcing, and are explicitly framed by the author as "spectacular" outcomes visible after "only
one year," rather than as findings from a systematic before/after study.

The article contains no data on economy-wide output, trade, employment, or aggregate growth, and
does not address financing, government coordination, or industrial-policy instruments of the kind
central to the broader postwar-Japan growth literature; its scope is restricted to statistical/
quality-control methodology and its diffusion through privately organized education (chiefly via
JUSE), which Deming explicitly and repeatedly contrasts with the absence of government
involvement. Deming disclaims economic or business expertise.

As a scanned reprint, portions of the source text contain OCR-related irregularities (garbled
characters in isolated words, occasional non-sequential page-number stamps). Figures and named
results reported in this summary were checked against duplicate OCR renderings of the same pages
where available and are reported as they appear in the source.
