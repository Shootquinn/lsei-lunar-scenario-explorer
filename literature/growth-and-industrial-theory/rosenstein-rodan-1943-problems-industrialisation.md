# Problems of Industrialisation of Eastern and South-Eastern Europe

## Citation

Rosenstein-Rodan, P. N. (1943). Problems of industrialisation of Eastern and
South-Eastern Europe. *The Economic Journal, 53*(210/211), 202-211.

DOI: unconfirmed in FA2 ledger (OUP/JSTOR; 10.2307/2226317 to be verified on

Publisher URL: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2226317

## Abstract

A policy essay, originally a chapter of a Royal Institute of International
Affairs Committee on Reconstruction report, on how to industrialize Eastern
and South-Eastern Europe's "agrarian excess population" after the Second
World War. Rosenstein-Rodan argues that piecemeal, firm-by-firm private
investment cannot industrialize a backward region, because a single new
factory typically has no market for its output until enough other factories
exist to pay wages that create demand for one another's products. He argues
investment must instead be planned and financed as one large, coordinated
program spanning complementary industries simultaneously - the whole
prospective industry treated and planned like a single firm - financed
through a proposed joint creditor-debtor holding company (the "Eastern
European Industrial Trust"), and sized against a rough ten-year capital
budget for absorbing the region's surplus rural population into industrial
employment.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Written as a chapter of the RIIA Economic Group's Committee on Reconstruction
report (per the article's opening footnote), the essay addresses postwar
planning for "international depressed areas," specifically the region
between Germany, Russia, and Italy, treated as a single economic unit with a
national income of about £2,000 million - roughly 40% of Great Britain's
despite comparable population. The starting assumption is that 20-25 million
of the region's 100-110 million people constitute an "agrarian excess
population," about 25% totally or partially unemployed ("disguised
unemployment"). Industrialization is framed as transporting capital to labor,
the counterpart to emigration (transporting labor to capital); emigration is
judged infeasible at the necessary scale, so industrialization must carry
most of the burden. The objective is prescriptive: which path of
industrialization the region should take, and what institutional and
financial arrangement could make it work in the decade-plus after the war.

Two paths are weighed. Path (i), self-sufficient "Russian model" vertical
industrialization financed by internal saving alone, is rejected: it would
demand prolonged sacrifice of an already low standard of living, would cut
the region off from the gains of international division of labor, and in
heavy industry specifically would only add to existing world excess
capacity. Path (ii), integration into the world economy through
labor-intensive light industry financed substantially by international
investment, is preferred because it could proceed faster and at much less
domestic sacrifice - the author frames the stakes partly in terms of
postwar political stability, contrasting 50% versus 20% of the excess
population profitably employed within ten years. The rest of the essay
argues why path (ii) has no clean nineteenth-century precedent and what
institutional form it needs.

### Methods and scope

The essay is not empirical or econometric; it is a discursive argument built
on two devices. First, hypothetical numerical illustrations - a 20,000-worker
shoe factory contrasted with a 1,000,000-worker multi-industry program, and
Allyn Young's example of a London Underground line - used to make the
external-economies argument concrete. Second, a rough capital-budgeting
exercise sizing the ten-year (de facto twelve-year, since the first two
postwar transition years are excluded from the count) financing need. Six
reasons are given for why the nineteenth-century pattern of international
investment cannot simply be repeated: self-liquidation of investment is no
longer automatic; existing investment institutions operate at too small a
scale and are blind to external economies; technology now mostly diffuses
rather than being newly discovered; overhead costs and firm size have risen,
raising capital risk and lowering flexibility; political risk now requires
state guarantees; and social tolerance for hardship has fallen since the
nineteenth century.

The institutional proposal this motivates is an "Eastern European Industrial
Trust" (E.E.I.T., explicitly marked as a provisional name) - a joint holding
company in which creditor and debtor countries each supply 50% of capital
and sit on its board, with shares redeemable after twenty years at a 10%
premium contingent on an average dividend of at least 4.5%, and a 3%
dividend floor guaranteed by each subscribing government. The capital
estimate covers roughly 12 million new industrial workers (the existing
agrarian excess plus 40-50% of a decade's population growth, net of workers
absorbable in idle capacity), costed at stated capital-per-worker ranges for
light, medium, and heavy industry (£100-400 / £400-800 / £800-1,500),
yielding a total requirement of about £4,800 million including maintenance,
of which the region itself would need to supply about £3,600 million.

### Key findings

The central argument is that industrializing a backward region cannot work
as a sequence of independently financed factories, because complementarity
of demand means a single new enterprise usually has no market for its own
output. This is illustrated with the shoe-factory example: putting 20,000
unemployed agrarian workers into one large shoe factory raises their cash
wages, but the workers will not spend all of those wages on shoes, so the
factory does not generate a market equal to its own output. If, instead,
1,000,000 workers are placed simultaneously across a whole system of
industries producing the bulk of the goods wage-earners spend their income
on, the industries collectively create their own market for one another's
output. Industries producing the bulk of wage goods are, in this sense,
complementary, and planning their creation together reduces the risk of not
being able to sell - a special case of external economies.

Two further categories of external economies are named as arguments for
coordinated investment: ordinary Marshallian economies external to a firm
but internal to a growing industry, and economies external to one industry
that arise from the growth of other industries - framed as a Pigovian gap
between "private" and "social" marginal net product that the essay argues is
too large to ignore in a region undergoing structural change, unlike in an
already-mature competitive economy.

Allyn Young's example of a London Underground line is used to sharpen the
point: the line fails an ordinary profitability test on fares alone but
becomes viable once the developer also captures the resulting land-value
appreciation along the route, and Young cautioned against counting that
appreciation in a national calculus because it has an uncompensated
mirror-image loss in the districts people left. Rosenstein-Rodan argues the
East European case differs: because agricultural land does not depreciate
when underemployed rural population moves off it, there is no offsetting
loss, so external economies of this kind should be counted in the calculus
of profitability - a further argument for large-unit, planned investment
able to internalize gains an individual private investor cannot capture.

Because private entrepreneurs price risk from past experience and lack the
aggregated market knowledge a planning board would have, their subjective
risk estimate for entering a not-yet-existing regional market runs higher
than the true, objective risk; reliance on ordinary private capital markets
would therefore under-invest, misallocate capital across industries relative
to the coordinated optimum, and proceed too slowly. The conclusion is that
"the whole of the industry to be created is to be treated and planned like
one huge firm or trust" - a single investment decision spanning all the
complementary industries, financed and owned as a unit (the proposed
E.E.I.T.), rather than assembled piecemeal.

The essay also weighs and partly rejects the alternative "basic industries
first" program - build railways, roads, and hydro-power and let the rest
follow automatically through the normal investment multiplier - arguing
this fits regions where transport scarcity is the binding constraint (China
and parts of Latin America are named) but not Eastern Europe, whose rail
mileage per unit of national income was already comparatively high; the
complementarity argument instead implies that, in this setting, all the
wage-goods industries are "basic," not just infrastructure. Labor training
(with associated transport and housing) is treated as a distinct necessary
form of overhead investment that private markets systematically
underprovide, since a firm that trains workers has no claim on them if they
leave for a rival employer - a poor private investment but a good one for
the state or the trust as a whole, again framed as a Pigovian divergence
between private and social return.

The capital-budgeting exercise concludes that even an "optimistic" ten-year
program, requiring an investment rate of about 18% of income (matched, the
author notes, to Russia's own rate), could still absorb only 70-80% of the
target population within the decade against a realistically achievable
domestic savings path (8% rising to 15%), so industrialization would need to
be supplemented by continued emigration and by capital transfers such as
German reparations paid in kind. This is a one-time capital-requirements
estimate for a labor-absorption plan, not a claim about a sustained annual
output growth rate: the essay contains no production function, no
rate-of-growth equation, and no analog to a Solow- or AK-style dynamic path.

Framing for FA2 / the thesis: this essay is the historical and conceptual
origin of the "big push" argument later formalized by Murphy, Shleifer, and
Vishny (1989) - that industrialization requires large, coordinated,
simultaneous investment across complementary sectors because no sector has a
viable market in isolation. Its relevance to a lunar economy is structural
rather than quantitative: a lunar production system has no market for any
single output (power, propellant, structural material, transport capacity)
until the complementary outputs exist simultaneously, mirroring the
shoe-factory-versus-many-industries argument directly. This source therefore
contributes the coordination precondition to FA2's verdict table - the case
that a lunar buildout must be planned and launched as a single multi-sector
investment program rather than emerge factory-by-factory - and not a
growth-rate figure. It produces no analog to the thesis's 41%-per-year
doubling requirement; its own headline number, an 18% investment rate over a
ten-year absorption plan, describes a one-time capital-mobilization pace for
absorbing surplus labor, not a sustained output growth rate.

### Limitations

- A discursive, verbal argument rather than a formal model: "complementarity"
  and "external economies" are asserted and illustrated, not derived from an
  explicit production or general-equilibrium structure. That formalization
  comes only later, in Murphy, Shleifer, and Vishny (1989) (FA2 source 9).
- The numerical illustrations (the shoe factory, the Tube-line example) are
  stylized devices to carry the argument, not estimates from data.
- The capital-budget figures (£4,800 million total, £3,600 million internal,
  an 18% investment rate against an 8-15% feasible savings path) rest on
  rough per-worker capital-intensity assumptions the author himself
  describes as based on "such statistics as are available," not on
  a documented data source.
- Historically specific to postwar Eastern and South-Eastern European
  reconstruction planning and the 1940s political economy of creditor/debtor
  relations and German reparations, not framed as a general growth theory.
- Contains no mechanism for a sustained per-period growth rate and no
  treatment of production functions, capital productivity, or diminishing
  returns; the "one huge firm or trust" planning-board device assumes away,
  rather than models, the coordination problem it identifies.
