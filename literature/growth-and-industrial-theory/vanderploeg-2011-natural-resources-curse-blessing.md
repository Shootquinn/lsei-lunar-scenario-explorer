## Abstract

Survey of the theory and evidence on whether natural resource wealth helps or harms economic
development. Lays out the heterogeneous country record, then evaluates eight candidate explanations for
a resource curse, Dutch disease, lost learning by doing in traded sectors, weak institutions,
presidential political systems, corruption, revenue volatility, rent seeking and civil conflict, and
unsustainable policy, against cross-country, panel and quasi-experimental evidence. A separate section
asks why resource rich developing countries so often fail to reinvest resource rents (negative genuine
saving) and proposes fiscal rules, informed by the permanent income and Hartwick rules, for saving and
spending windfalls in developed and developing economies alike.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens with the puzzle that motivates the whole survey: some resource rich economies, Botswana
and Norway are its own examples, prosper while others with comparable endowments perform badly (page 1).
It asks whether the mechanism is Dutch disease driven de-industrialization, insufficient learning-by-doing
spillovers in traded sectors, a shift from productive entrepreneurship to rent seeking, weak institutions
and rule of law, plundering by corruption and civil war, persistence of bad policy, or
under-reinvestment of depleting resource wealth. The stated plan is to present stylized facts (section
2), test eight resource-curse hypotheses against the best available evidence (section 3), examine why
resource rich developing economies under-reinvest rents via the "anticipation of better times" and
"voracious rent seeking" hypotheses (section 4), and derive fiscal rules for harnessing windfalls given
capital scarcity and volatile revenue (section 5), before concluding (section 6).

### Methods and scope

This is a literature survey, not a single new empirical study, though the author works through his own
theoretical models (a Salter-Swan two-sector model of Dutch disease, rent-seeking and voracity models,
Hotelling and Hartwick depletion rules, and fiscal-rule derivations) alongside reporting others' results.
Evidence surveyed spans cross-country growth regressions (notably Sachs and Warner, 1995, 1997a, 1997b,
and Mehlum, Moene and Torvik, 2006, reproduced in the paper's own Table 1); panel-data studies adding
fixed effects and institutional-quality controls; quasi-experimental, within-country studies exploiting
subnational variation (Brazilian municipal oil dependence, Caselli and Michaels 2009, Brollo et al.
2010; the paired comparison of oil-discovering São Tomé against non-discovering Cape Verde, Vicente
2010; Colombian municipalities across commodity price cycles, Dube and Vargas 2008); and World Bank
(2006) wealth-accounting data on produced, natural and intangible capital for close to 120 countries.
Country illustrations are drawn from Nigeria, Botswana, Norway, the United Arab Emirates, South Africa,
Chile and Peru, among others. No new dataset is assembled; each cited estimate carries its own sample,
period and specification, which the paper repeatedly flags as varying.

### Key findings

**Heterogeneous stylized facts (pages 2-7).** Nigeria's oil revenue per capita rose from US$33 in 1965 to
US$325 in 2000, yet income per capita stagnated near US$1,100 (PPP) since 1960; the population share
living on under US$1 a day rose from 26 to almost 70 percent between 1970 and 2000, and the income share
of the top 2 percent rose from equalling the bottom 17 percent (1970) to equalling the bottom 55 percent
(2000). By contrast Botswana, with 40 percent of GDP from diamonds, has the world's highest growth rate
since 1965 and GDP per capita at least ten times Nigeria's; only four of 65 resource rich developing
countries (Botswana, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand) achieved both investment above 25 percent of GDP and
growth above 4 percent (Gylfason, 2001). Norway, the world's third largest petroleum exporter, shows
strong non-oil growth alongside low corruption; the United Arab Emirates, with close to 10 percent of
world crude oil reserves, is described as having "turned its resource curse into a blessing" through
diversification and low debt. World Bank (2006) wealth accounts show global wealth per capita of
$96,000, but poorer countries hold over two thirds of their national wealth in land and natural capital
versus a much higher intangible-capital share in rich countries; Nigeria, Venezuela and Algeria show
negative shares of intangible capital in total wealth. A negative cross-country correlation between
growth and resource export share is also reported (Fig. 1), alongside partial correlations across
samples of 85 to 87 countries linking resource wealth to less trade openness, less foreign direct
investment, lower investment and financial development (M2 to GDP), lower school enrolment, and higher
macroeconomic volatility, each in turn correlated with lower growth; the paper is explicit these are
correlations, not evidence of causation.

**The Dutch disease mechanism and its evidence (pages 8-12).** A Salter-Swan two-sector model shows a
resource windfall raising national income and demand for non-traded goods, appreciating their relative
price, drawing labour out of the traded sector and contracting it while the non-traded sector expands;
longer-run outcomes depend on capital mobility and factor intensities, and can in some parameterizations
even reverse into pro-industrialization (Corden and Neary, 1982). Empirically, for 135 countries over
1975-2007 a resource windfall is associated with saving about 30 percent of it, decreasing non-resource
exports by 35 to 70 percent, and increasing non-resource imports by 0 to 35 percent (Harding and
Venables, 2010), robust across cross-sections and panels. A 10.0 percent oil windfall is associated with
a 3.4 percent average fall in manufacturing value added, smaller where capital-account restrictions exist
(Ismail, 2010); where the resource sector exceeds 30 percent of GDP, tradables run 15 percentage points
below the Chenery-Syrquin norm (Brahmbhatt et al., 2010). A quasi-experimental study of Brazilian
municipalities, exploiting the geographic concentration of oil fields and Petrobras's exogenous
allocation decisions (Caselli and Michaels, 2009), finds oil discoveries barely raise non-oil GDP and
shift some activity from industry to services, consistent with Dutch disease, but mostly boost local
public revenue without much household income gain or in-migration, suggesting local waste and corruption
rather than pure Dutch disease. Early evidence on manufacturing decline following terms-of-trade shocks
had been "mixed" (Sala-i-Martin and Subramanian, 2003, page 11).

**Learning by doing and the growth channel (pages 11-13).** A separate channel holds that if the traded
sector generates most learning-by-doing or human-capital spillovers, a resource-driven labour shift out
of it permanently lowers technical progress and growth (Sachs and Warner, 1995; Gylfason et al., 1999).
The pioneering cross-country regression finds resource rich countries grew about one percentage point
less per year over 1970-89, controlling for initial income, investment, openness and rule of law (Sachs
and Warner, 1995, page 13). The paper's own Table 1 reproduces three versions of this regression (Sachs
and Warner 1997a, 1997b, and Mehlum, Moene and Torvik 2005a): the coefficient on resource dependence is
negative and significant in all three
(-10.57, -6.69 and -14.34, with t-statistics in parentheses of 7.01, 5.43 and 4.21 respectively), while
initial income is consistently negative (conditional convergence) and openness and investment are
consistently positive; institutional quality alone is not significant at the 5 percent level in the
second specification.

**Critiques of the basic growth regressions (pages 14-15).** These cross-country results are contested.
If resource dependence (a flow measure) is instrumented, it does not significantly affect growth, while
subsoil resource wealth (a stock measure of abundance) has a significant positive effect (Brunnschweiler
and Bulte, 2008); instrumenting instead with economically recoverable reserves finds no evidence for
curse or blessing unless an indirect volatility channel is allowed (van der Ploeg and Poelhekke, 2010).
The paper flags omitted-variable bias, sensitivity to sample period and variable definitions, and the
case for panel over cross-section data to avoid conflating dependence with unobserved initial
productivity (Parente and Prescott, 1994; Islam, 1995). One 91-country panel (1970-2000) finds the
resource effect on growth disappears once fixed effects are added (Manzano and Rigobon, 2001), while
another, on the same countries and period, finds point-source resources (minerals, coffee, coca)
specifically retard democratic and institutional development (Mavrotas et al., 2006).

**Institutions turn the curse into a blessing; the point-source versus diffuse distinction (pages
16-18).** The paper's central conditional finding is that countries with sufficiently good institutions
escape the curse. In the third regression of Table 1, an institutional-quality index above 14.34/15.4 =
0.93 implies no curse; this holds for 15 of the 87 sample countries, including the US, Canada, Norway,
the Netherlands, New Zealand and Australia (page 17). The adverse effect is described as particularly
strong for "point-source" resources (oil, diamonds, minerals, plantation crops), whose production and
revenue are geographically concentrated and easily appropriated by the state, versus more diffusely
produced agricultural commodities (rice, wheat, animals) whose rents disperse through the economy (Auty,
1997, 2001b; Isham et al., 2003; Boschini et al., 2007; Mavrotas et al., 2006, page 17). Boschini et al.
(2007) separate "institutional appropriability" (dependence only hurts under bad institutions) from
"technical appropriability" (bad institutions bite harder the more technically appropriable the resource
is); the paper's Table 2 reports marginal growth effects for 1975-88 across institutional quality for
four resource measures, and for each the sign flips from negative under the worst institutions to
positive under the best: primary exports as a share of GDP run from -0.548 to -0.228; ores and metals
exports from -0.946 to +1.629; mineral production from -1.127 to +1.560; gold, silver and diamond
production from -1.145 to +1.776, the swing being largest for the most technically appropriable resources
(page 18). Related cross-country evidence attributes the curse mainly to resources' corrosive effect on
institutional quality rather than to lost export competitiveness (Sala-i-Martin and Subramanian, 2003,
page 17); institutions are described elsewhere as possibly more powerful than geography, trade or policy
in explaining cross-country income (North, 1990; Acemoglu et al., 2001, 2003), a view not universally
shared (page 18; see Limitations).

**Presidential democracies and corruption (pages 18-20).** Using a cross-country sample of 90 countries,
the resource curse is found to occur in presidential rather than parliamentary democracies, since
presidential systems are less accountable and more exposed to rent extraction (Andersen and Aslaksen,
2008); constitutional structure is reported as more important empirically than democracy per se.
Cross-country regressions on 55 countries link resource dependence to a worse Transparency International
corruption score, in turn predicting lower growth (Mauro, 1995), and other work finds resource wealth
stimulates bureaucratic and political corruption (Ades and Di Tella, 1999). A 99-country panel (1980-2004)
finds resources induce corruption specifically in states non-democratic for more than 60 percent of years
since 1956 (Bhattacharyya and Hodler, 2009). Two quasi-experiments are highlighted as the paper's best
evidence on this channel (page 20): comparing São Tomé, where an oil discovery was announced in 1997-99,
against similar, non-discovering Cape Verde, perceived corruption rose close to 10 percent after the
announcement, easing after 2004 (Vicente, 2010); and Brazilian municipal data with a
regression-discontinuity design, where a 10 percent municipal revenue windfall is associated with a 17
to 24 percent rise in corruption, a 7 percentage point rise in incumbent re-election, and a 7 percentage
point fall in challengers holding a college degree (Brollo et al., 2010).

**Volatility as the "quintessence" of the curse (pages 20-22).** During the 1970s commodity price boom,
resource rich countries borrowed against high prices as collateral; the 1980s price collapse then drove
many into debt crises, and once debt is included as a regressor the direct effect of resource dependence
on growth disappears in panel estimation (page 20). Resource revenues are far more volatile than GDP
because of low supply price elasticity, and Dutch disease can itself generate real exchange rate
volatility that discourages investment and learning (Gylfason et al., 1999). Real exchange rate
volatility is found to significantly harm long-run productivity growth, especially where financial
development is low (Aghion et al., 2009, page 20), and historical evidence for 1870-1939 likewise finds
volatility harmed growth in commodity-dependent "periphery" nations but not in Europe or the US (Blattman
et al., 2007). Building on this, van der Ploeg and Poelhekke (2009) find the adverse growth effect of
resources works mainly through commodity price volatility, worst for point-source resources (oil,
diamonds) and in landlocked, ethnically polarized economies with weak financial institutions (page 21).
Instrumenting resource exports with subsoil resource stocks, van der Ploeg and Poelhekke (2010) report an
insignificant direct coefficient on point-source resource exports for mean growth, but a significant
coefficient of -0.394 (1 percent level) for the standard deviation of unanticipated growth, and
significant coefficients of 11.8 and 5.3 (1 percent level) for point-source and diffuse resource
dependence, respectively, on the variance of unanticipated growth (footnote 11, page 21). A nonlinear
specification finds the curse operative only where volatility of unanticipated growth exceeds 2.45
percent per annum, a threshold crossed by Bolivia but not Norway despite both having roughly 15 percent
point-source resource export dependence over the sample (page 21). Financial development offsets the
volatility effect somewhat; specialization away from the non-resource traded sector, driven by bankruptcy
costs and interest-rate responses to relative-price volatility, can be self-reinforcing until that sector
vanishes (Hausmann and Rigobon, 2002, page 21). Volatility is also linked to worse investment, income
distribution, poverty and educational attainment (Aizenman and Marion, 1999; Flug et al., 1999, page 22);
diversification is offered as a partial remedy, more relevant for Nigeria or Venezuela than for
diversified Mexico or naturally specialized Gulf states (page 22). The paper's own closing assessment is
that volatility, not the level of dependence alone, "may be the quintessence of the resource curse" once
the endogeneity of resource dependence is taken into account (page 41).

**Rent seeking, the voracity effect, and armed conflict (pages 22-25).** Massive, badly governed resource
rents combined with weak property rights push entrepreneurs from productive activity into rent seeking,
with self-reinforcing, multiple-equilibrium dynamics (Murphy et al., 1993; Acemoglu, 1995); the "voracity
effect" explains drag on growth after oil windfalls in Nigeria, Venezuela and Mexico as a commons problem
of rapacious rent grabbing (Lane and Tornell, 1996; Tornell and Lane, 1999). Higher production income
makes war less attractive while higher resource income makes it more attractive: a country with no
resources has an estimated 0.5 percent probability of civil conflict, rising to 23 percent where
resources are a quarter of GDP (Collier and Hoeffler, 2004, page 23), and primary-export share is
described as the single largest influence on conflict risk. Diamonds, oil and narcotics carry the
highest risk of civil war onset (Lujala, 2010; Fearon and Laitin, 2003; Ross, 2004; Angrist and Kugler,
2008), with onshore oil more conflict-prone than offshore. A quasi-experiment on Colombian municipalities
(Dube and Vargas, 2008) finds the 1990s coffee price collapse increased violence in coffee regions by
cutting wages and the opportunity cost of fighting, while rising oil prices fuelled conflict in oil
regions by raising capturable municipal revenue: conflict intensifies when labour-intensive commodity
prices fall but weakens when capital-intensive prices fall (page 24). The paper cautions that
cross-country conflict estimates may be confounded, since fighting affects resource dependence itself,
which can remove the correlation entirely once treated as endogenous (Brunnschweiler and Bulte, 2009,
page 24-25); ethnic and religious fractionalization is reported to worsen the curse (Hodler, 2006).

**Unsustainable policy (pages 26-27).** Resource wealth is linked to excessive government borrowing,
which depreciates the real exchange rate over the long run (Mansoorian, 1991; Manzano and Rigobon,
2001), and to eroded political discipline generally. The paper's case is the Netherlands after the
Slochteren gas discovery and the 1970s oil shocks: successive governments expanded public employment,
made benefits more generous, weakened eligibility conditions and raised the minimum wage, a path it took
more than twenty years, starting in 1989, to put back on a sustainable footing (page 27). Many developing
countries similarly pursued import-substitution industrialization anticipating secular commodity price
decline (the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis).

**Negative genuine saving and the Hartwick rule puzzle (pages 31-38).** World Bank (2006) genuine saving
estimates, roughly net saving minus resource and pollution depletion, show resource rich countries
typically saving less: Nigeria and Angola have genuine saving rates near minus 30 percent (page 32), and
Congo and Nigeria show saving gaps, relative to what full Hartwick-rule reinvestment would require, of
110 and 71 percent of GNP respectively (page 33). Even where roughly half of 1970s oil windfalls was
invested domestically (Algeria, Ecuador, Indonesia, Nigeria, Trinidad and Tobago, Venezuela), all six
still saw prolonged real appreciation and negative growth, partly attributed to "white elephant" public
investment (Gelb, 1988, page 33). The paper offers two of its own explanations rather than treating this
purely as mismanagement. First, "anticipation of better times": if a country expects rising resource
prices or improving extraction technology, optimal saving falls below the Hartwick benchmark since it is
efficient to defer extraction and borrow against future rents, so negative saving need not reflect poor
institutions (pages 33-35). Second, "voracious rent seeking": rival factions competing over resource
stocks subject to seepage or weak property rights use an effective discount rate exceeding the world
interest rate, accelerating extraction; more fractionalized societies save a larger revenue share yet end
up with less accumulated sovereign wealth in the long run (pages 37-38).

**Fiscal rules for harnessing windfalls (pages 39-41).** For temporary, anticipated windfalls the
benchmark is the permanent income hypothesis: borrow ahead of the windfall, repay debt and build
sovereign wealth during it, then finance a permanent consumption rise from the fund's interest once it
ends (the IMF has often recommended sovereign wealth funds along these lines, Davis et al., 2002). Norway
follows a more conservative "bird-in-hand" rule, restricting spending to interest already earned; its
fiscal reactions are forward-looking with respect to pension liabilities but not hydrocarbon revenue
itself (Harding and van der Ploeg, 2009, page 39-40). For capital-scarce developing economies
facing high interest rates and imperfect capital markets, the paper argues the permanent income
benchmark is inappropriate: it is instead optimal to skew consumption toward the present, pay down debt
and build domestic capital, parking the windfall abroad only until absorption constraints (infrastructure,
teachers, nurses) ease (van der Ploeg and Venables, 2010a, page 40). The paper flags political-economy
risks that can defeat any of these rules: incumbents may avoid liquid funds rivals could raid, biasing
spending toward illiquid, partisan "white elephant" projects (Collier et al., 2010; Robinson and Torvik,
2005, page 40); and given persistent, high-variance price shocks it can be optimal to extract faster than
the certainty-equivalent Hotelling path while building precautionary liquidity buffers (van der Ploeg,
2010b, page 40-41).

### Limitations

The paper is a synthesis of others' estimates rather than a single unified empirical exercise, so its
headline figures come from studies with differing samples, periods, instruments and definitions of
"resource dependence" versus "resource abundance," a distinction it shows can reverse a result's sign
(Brunnschweiler and Bulte, 2008, page 14). It repeatedly flags cross-country regressions as vulnerable to
omitted-variable bias, reverse causality and sample sensitivity, and explicitly favours panel and
quasi-experimental designs over the cross-sections producing several headline coefficients (pages 14-15).
Some hypotheses rest on thin quasi-experimental evidence, one paired-country comparison (São Tomé versus
Cape Verde) plus Brazilian and Colombian subnational studies, rather than replicated bodies of work. Two
of the paper's own proposals, the anticipation-of-better-times and voracious-rent-seeking hypotheses for
negative genuine saving, are offered as unresolved theory with "no studies available yet" formally
testing them (page 37). It does not resolve competing accounts of institutional primacy, noting "not
everybody agrees fully" that institutions dominate geography, trade and policy (Glaeser et al., 2004,
page 18). A funding disclosure states the work was supported by "the BP funded Oxford Centre for Analysis
of Research Rich Economies." Its closing section leaves several questions open: the historical reversal
of the curse (resources aided US growth before roughly the 1970s but not since), incentive-compatible
government-exploration-firm contracts, and whether private versus public ownership changes saving
behaviour (pages 42-43).

### Topic mapping

FA8. Source list entry 5. Sub-Q 4. A lunar economy built on a single extracted commodity (PGM,
propellant, water) is structurally a point-source, capital-intensive resource economy in this paper's own
taxonomy: concentrated production, massive appropriable rents, weak initial institutions. The paper's
central finding is that such profiles carry the base-rate expectation of a curse: the marginal growth
effect of point-source, technically appropriable resources runs from strongly negative under weak
institutions to positive only under the best (Table 2, page 18), and the curse is "particularly severe"
for easily appropriable point-source resources with concentrated rents (page 17). For mechanism, the
paper supplies channels a red team could map onto a lunar program: real exchange rate appreciation and
crowding out of a nascent tradable sector (Dutch disease, pages 8-12); loss of learning-by-doing if
extraction draws effort from the growth-generating sector (pages 11-13); rent seeking, corruption and
elite capture where institutions are weak or a single actor controls appropriation (pages 16-25); and,
argued as the channel with the largest identified effect, revenue volatility itself, significant and
negative on growth even where the level of dependence is not (footnote 11, page 21), with a nonlinear
threshold (2.45 percent volatility of unanticipated growth) separating cursed from uncursed outcomes at
comparable dependence levels (Bolivia versus Norway, page 21). The paper's conditions for flipping curse
to blessing, institutional quality above a threshold, financial development, trade openness, and saving
rents via sovereign wealth funds or, for capital-scarce entities, paying down debt instead (pages 39-40),
plus its caution that political actors resist locking rents into raidable liquid funds (page 40), together
describe both the base-rate risk and the governance conditions under which such an economy has
historically escaped it. The paper's own debate between the original Sachs and Warner (1995) finding and
its critics (Brunnschweiler and Bulte, 2008; Manzano and Rigobon, 2001; van der Ploeg and Poelhekke, 2010)
is reported above rather than resolved, consistent with its conclusion that the curse is real on average
but "not cast in stone" (page 41-42).
