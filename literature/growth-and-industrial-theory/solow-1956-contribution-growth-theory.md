# A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth

## Citation

Solow, R. M. (1956). A contribution to the theory of economic growth. *The Quarterly
Journal of Economics, 70*(1), 65-94.

DOI: 10.2307/1884513 (confirmed in FA2 ledger)

Publisher URL: https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/70/1/65/1903777 (JSTOR
stable: https://www.jstor.org/stable/1884513)

## Abstract

The 1956 QJE printing carries no author abstract (the journal did not use them at the
time); the following is a descriptive summary written for this review. The paper
replaces the fixed-proportions production technology of the Harrod-Domar growth model
with a neoclassical production function exhibiting constant returns to scale and smooth
capital-labor substitution, then asks whether Harrod's "knife-edge" instability survives.
It derives a single differential equation governing the capital-labor ratio, shows
graphically and through three worked examples (fixed proportions, Cobb-Douglas, and a
constant-elasticity-of-substitution case) that the economy typically converges to a
stable balanced-growth path in which capital, labor, and output all expand at the
exogenous rate of labor-force growth, works out the associated competitive factor-price
paths, and then extends the model to neutral technological change, an elastic labor
supply, a variable saving ratio, taxation, and endogenous population growth. A closing
section relaxes the full-employment assumptions to sketch how rigid wages and liquidity
preference could reintroduce Keynesian-style unemployment.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens as a direct critique of the Harrod-Domar model. Solow's target is its
central and, in his telling, powerful conclusion that even in the long run a growing
economy is balanced precariously, on a knife-edge of equilibrium growth: any departure
of the savings ratio, capital-output ratio, or labor-force growth rate from the exact
values needed to equate Harrod's warranted and natural growth rates produces either
chronic unemployment or unbounded inflation. Solow's diagnostic claim is that this
instability is not a generic feature of growth but an artifact of one crucial assumption
buried in the Harrod-Domar setup: production under fixed proportions, with no
possibility of substituting labor for capital. He opens with a methodological remark on
theorizing worth quoting directly: "All theory depends on assumptions which are not
quite true." A crucial assumption, in his terms, is one the conclusions depend on
sensitively, and he sets out to test whether
Harrod's instability is such a case.

The stated objective is therefore narrow and constructive: retain every other
Harrod-Domar assumption (a single composite good, a constant exogenous saving rate,
exogenous exponential labor-force growth) but replace fixed proportions with a standard
neoclassical production function allowing continuous substitution between capital and
labor, and trace out the resulting long-run adjustment path. The paper's own summary of
its plan (Introduction, p. 66) is to work out in detail how the system adapts to an
exogenously given rate of labor-force increase, checking whether Harrod's instability
appears, then analyze the associated price-wage-interest behavior, then relax
several of the remaining rigid assumptions (adding neutral technological change and an
interest-elastic savings schedule) to see what changes, before briefly considering
Keynesian-style rigidities.

### Methods and scope

The model has one composite commodity Y(t), consumed or saved at a constant fraction s,
so that net investment equals net saving: K̇ = sY (eq. 1). Output is produced by capital
K(t) and labor L(t) through a production function Y = F(K,L) (eq. 2) that is homogeneous
of degree one (constant returns to scale) — explicitly ruling out any fixed,
non-augmentable factor such as land. Output is defined net of capital depreciation from
the outset, so no separate depreciation term appears in the accumulation identity (unlike
later textbook expositions of the "Solow model," which typically work with gross output
and an explicit −δK term). The labor force grows exogenously and inelastically at a
constant relative rate n: L(t) = L₀eⁿᵗ (eq. 4). Substituting gives the basic accumulation
equation K̇ = sF(K, L₀eⁿᵗ) (eq. 5), a differential equation in K(t) alone.

Solow then converts this into an equation in a single normalized variable, the
capital-labor ratio r = K/L (his notation; later literature typically writes k). Using
K = rL and constant returns to scale to divide through by L, he arrives at what this
review treats as the paper's fundamental equation of motion:

  ṙ = sF(r,1) − nr  (eq. 6)

where F(r,1) is output per worker as a function of capital per worker (i.e., f(r) in
later notation). The right-hand side is the difference between actual investment per
worker, sF(r,1), and the "capital widening" per worker, nr, required merely to equip the
growing labor force at the existing ratio. Solow analyzes this equation almost entirely
graphically: plotting the curve sF(r,1) against the ray nr and reading off intersections,
where ṙ = 0, as candidate balanced-growth (steady-state) capital-labor ratios (Figures
I-III). He shows that whether an equilibrium r* exists, and whether it is stable, depends
entirely on the shape of F(r,1) — that is, on how the marginal product of capital behaves
as r varies — not on any special coincidence among s, n, and a fixed capital-output
coefficient.

Three worked examples make the qualitative analysis explicit and solvable in closed form:
(1) Fixed proportions, Y = min(K/a, L/b) — the Harrod-Domar case recovered as a limiting,
zero-substitution special case of Solow's own framework; (2) the Cobb-Douglas function,
Y = KᵃL¹⁻ᵃ; and (3) a constant-elasticity family, Y = (α√K + √L)², a special case (ρ=1/2)
of the CES form Y=(αKᵖ+Lᵖ)^(1/ρ) with an elasticity of substitution greater than the
Cobb-Douglas benchmark of one. Section V works out the competitive factor-price behavior
consistent with each growth path: real wage w/p and real capital rental q/p are set by
marginal-productivity conditions (eqs. 10-11), and an arbitrage condition (eq. 12) ties
the money rate of interest i(t) to the commodity own-rate q/p and the rate of price
change. Section VI relaxes the model piece by piece — neutral (Hicks-type) technological
change Y = A(t)F(K,L), an elastic labor-supply schedule, a saving ratio that responds to
the return on capital, proportional income taxation, and an endogenous, income-dependent
rate of population growth. Section VII, "Qualifications," steps outside the full-
employment neoclassical frame to note, briefly and non-formally, how rigid real wages or
a liquidity trap could reproduce Keynesian unemployment or excess capacity within the
same capital-labor-ratio bookkeeping.

Throughout, the maintained assumptions are: a single homogeneous capital good;
production with constant returns to scale and smooth (continuously variable) factor
proportions in the base cases; perfectly competitive factor markets with instantaneous
market clearing, so real factor prices equal marginal products; a constant, exogenously
given saving rate s (not derived from any intertemporal optimization problem); and an
exogenously given, inelastically supplied labor force growing at constant rate n (later
relaxed to an elastic or endogenous supply as an extension, not the baseline case).

### Key findings

- **Central verdict for FA2: the model does not admit a constant 24-month doubling (a
  sustained ~41 percent-per-year growth rate) of aggregate or per-capita output. The
  answer is NO.** Every closed-form solution Solow derives is built to show the opposite
  property — that a capital stock (or capital-labor ratio) growing faster than its
  equilibrium value decelerates smoothly back toward a low, exogenously fixed long-run
  rate, rather than compounding indefinitely at an elevated rate.
- **Mechanics of the deceleration.** In the fundamental equation ṙ = sF(r,1) − nr, a
  balanced-growth ratio r* satisfies sF(r*,1) = nr* (investment per worker exactly
  matches the widening needed for the growing labor force). For the well-behaved
  production functions Solow treats as the generic case (Cobb-Douglas; fixed proportions
  within its two linear branches), the marginal product of capital is unbounded as r → 0
  and falls to zero as r → ∞ — the property later formalized (Inada, 1963, a term Solow
  does not use) as the Inada conditions. This forces the sF(r,1) curve to cross the nr
  ray from above exactly once, producing a unique, globally stable r*: whenever r < r*,
  ṙ > 0 but shrinks as r approaches r*; whenever r > r*, ṙ < 0. The text states this
  directly: whatever the initial capital-labor ratio, the system develops toward a state
  of balanced growth at the natural rate, with the time path not exactly exponential
  except asymptotically. A capital-deepening episode — e.g., an initially
  low capital-labor ratio — makes capital and output grow temporarily faster than labor,
  but that excess growth rate decays continuously as r* is approached, rather than
  persisting.
- **Long-run growth rate is pinned to exogenous n, independent of the saving rate.** In
  the Cobb-Douglas example, Solow gives the closed-form solution for K(t) and shows that
  for large t, K(t) grows essentially in proportion to L₀eⁿᵗ — that is, at the same
  asymptotic rate n as the labor force, regardless of s. The saving rate enters only
  through the level: the equilibrium ratio is r* = (s/n)^(1/(1-a)), so a higher s (or
  lower n) raises the steady-state capital-per-worker and output-per-worker level, but
  not the long-run growth rate, which stays fixed at n. Solow makes the same point via
  the capital-output ratio: at equilibrium, K/Y = s/n, which he treats not as a
  coincidence but as the necessary outcome of demand-supply adjustment — Harrod's
  warranted rate (s/C) and natural rate (n) are reconciled at the level of C = s/n, not by
  forcing an accelerated growth rate.
- **With exogenous technical progress, the long-run per-capita growth rate is set by the
  rate of technical progress, not by the saving rate.** Extending the Cobb-Douglas case
  with neutral technical change A(t) = eᵍᵗ, Solow finds the capital stock grows
  asymptotically at rate n + g/b (b = 1−a) and states that real output grows
  asymptotically at rate n + ag/b — in both cases a fixed rate set only by the exogenous
  parameters n, g, and the capital share a, never by s. Per-capita output growth is then
  ag/b, a constant determined purely by the technology parameters. (Because this
  particular case uses Hicks-neutral rather than labor-augmenting technical progress, the
  capital-output ratio itself does not settle to a constant — Solow notes it grows without
  bound, at rate g — but the growth *rates* of the aggregates remain fixed exogenous
  constants throughout; there is no channel by which raising s raises the long-run growth
  rate.) This is the formal content of the modern statement that in the Solow model the
  saving rate affects levels, not the long-run per-capita growth rate, which is instead
  tied to the exogenous rate of technical progress.
- **The Harrod-Domar knife-edge is recovered only as the zero-substitution limit.**
  Example 1 (fixed proportions, Y = min(K/a, L/b)) reproduces Harrod's instability
  exactly: if the natural rate n exceeds the warranted rate s/a, capital-labor ratio falls
  without limit and unemployment grows without bound; if s/a exceeds n, capital becomes
  permanently redundant and excess capacity grows without bound; only at the exact
  knife-edge n = s/a does neutral balance hold. Solow's point is that this instability is
  a consequence specifically of rigid (right-angle) isoquants, not of growth theory in
  general: once smooth substitution is restored (Examples 2 and 3), the knife-edge
  need not appear at all, and Solow notes that with the Cobb-Douglas function it
  provably never can.
- **A load-bearing internal caveat: the deceleration result depends on diminishing
  returns actually biting, and Solow's own third example shows a case where it does not.**
  In Example 3 (Y = (α√K+√L)², an elasticity-of-substitution-greater-than-one CES case),
  Solow notes that if sα² > n, no balanced-growth equilibrium exists at all: the
  capital-labor ratio and real output per head both increase without limit, because the
  underlying system is productive enough to sustain it. Because the marginal product of
  capital in this function does
  not fall to zero as r grows (violating the upper Inada condition that the other
  examples satisfy), the fundamental equation's dynamics for large r reduce approximately
  to ṙ ≈ (sα² − n)r — implying, by the paper's own equation (8), sustained exponential
  growth of the capital-labor ratio, and hence of per-capita output, at the constant rate
  (sα² − n), driven by capital accumulation alone with no technical progress required.
  Solow treats this as one of two illustrative possibilities in a short passage, not as a
  central result, and gives no reason to think sα² − n could plausibly reach anything near
  41 percent per year; but the case is the earliest formal demonstration, within the
  neoclassical apparatus itself, that unbounded capital-driven per-capita growth is not
  logically excluded by the model's structure — only by the specific (empirically
  motivated) Inada-type assumption on the production function that Solow adopts as the
  default case. This is the formal seed later made rigorous by the AK/broad-capital
  literature (Rebelo 1991 in this ledger).
- **Multiple equilibria are possible, but each is still a decelerating balanced-growth
  path.** Figure II shows a production function for which sF(r,1) crosses nr three times,
  giving two stable equilibria (r₁, r₃) separated by an unstable one (r₂); depending on
  initial conditions the system settles at one or the other, with a permanently higher
  output-per-head level around r₃. In every stable case, however, capital, labor, and
  output still converge to expanding in the same proportion at rate n — multiplicity
  changes the long-run level, not the long-run growth rate.
- **Factor-price behavior is smooth, not catastrophic, once substitution is restored.**
  Under competitive pricing (eqs. 10-12), as r converges to a stable r*, the real
  wage-rental ratio w/q converges to a finite equilibrium value; Solow notes this directly
  contradicts Harrod's claim that a perpetually falling interest rate is needed to sustain
  equilibrium growth. By contrast, in the fixed-proportions case, factor prices are
  degenerate almost everywhere: capital's return is zero wherever labor is the binding
  factor, labor's wage is zero wherever capital is binding, and prices are indeterminate
  only exactly at the knife-edge ratio r = a/b.
- **Endogenous population growth produces a low-level trap with an escape threshold.**
  In the extension where the population growth rate depends on the capital-labor ratio
  (Figure IX), Solow sketches a case with a low stable equilibrium r₁, an unstable
  threshold r₂, and describes how a sufficiently large, discrete burst of investment can
  lift the system past that threshold into a self-sustaining rise in income and capital
  per head — a coordination/big-push-flavored escape valve (relevant to Sub-Q 6),
  though the destination is still a new balanced-growth path growing at a fixed rate, not
  unbounded acceleration.
- **A variable, income-responsive saving ratio and proportional taxation are shown to
  shift the equilibrium level (up or down) without altering the qualitative conclusion**
  that a stable balanced-growth path, once reached, expands at the exogenous rate of
  labor-force (and, if present, technical-progress) growth.

### Limitations

- The paper is explicitly and self-consciously full-employment economics, in the dual
  sense of an equilibrium condition and a frictionless, competitive, causal system;
  Solow states plainly in the Qualifications section that Keynesian problems of
  deficient aggregate demand are set aside only within the scope of this exercise, not
  denied to exist in general.
- Technical progress, once introduced, is a bare exogenous scale factor A(t) = eᵍᵗ with no
  underlying model of what generates it or how it interacts with investment decisions — it
  is simply assumed, not explained, which is precisely the "residual" character later
  identified with Solow's own 1957 growth-accounting paper.
- The saving rate s is a fixed behavioral constant, not derived from any optimizing
  household problem; the model therefore has nothing to say about how saving responds to
  the return to capital except in the separate, briefly sketched s(r) extension (eq. 6c).
- Capital is a single homogeneous, infinitely malleable composite good; there is no
  vintage structure, no capital heterogeneity, and no adjustment costs to reallocating it.
- Labor supply is exogenous and (in the baseline case) completely wage-inelastic; the
  economy is closed, with no international trade or capital flows, and there is no money,
  risk, or uncertainty in the core analysis — points Solow himself flags in the closing
  Qualifications section, where he states outright that no credible investment theory
  can rest on an assumption of perfect foresight and arbitrage over time.
- Existence and uniqueness of a stable balanced-growth equilibrium are not guaranteed in
  general; Solow shows by construction (Figures II and III, and Example 3) that
  multiple equilibria, no equilibrium, or unbounded per-capita growth are all
  mathematically possible depending on the shape of the production function, so the
  benign convergence result is a property of the specific (Inada-satisfying) examples
  treated as the default case, not a theorem proved for all constant-returns production
  functions.
- The Hicks-neutral form of technical progress used in the worked extension (Section VI)
  is shown, by the paper's own algebra, to leave the capital-output ratio drifting upward
  without bound rather than settling to a constant — a feature later resolved in the
  literature by restricting steady-state-compatible technical progress to the
  labor-augmenting (Harrod-neutral) form, a refinement this 1956 paper does not make.
- Monopolistic competition, non-proportional taxation, and a fully worked-out monetary
  sector are each raised as extensions the paper does not carry out, with Solow noting
  explicitly that considerably more experimentation on these fronts remains to be done.

### Topic mapping

FA2. Serves Sub-Q 1 (the convergence bound). This is the priority-tier baseline paper for
the whole focus area: Section III (the fundamental equation ṙ = sF(r,1) − nr and its
graphical stability analysis), Section IV (the Cobb-Douglas and fixed-proportions
examples that pin the long-run growth rate to n, and to n plus a function of the
exogenous technical-progress rate once Section VI's extension is added), and Section VII
(the qualifications limiting the result's domain) together supply the mechanics — the
deceleration property, the Inada-type condition it rests on, and the result that the
saving rate sets levels rather than long-run growth rates — that the FA2 synthesis needs
to name the "wall" any faster-growth argument must get over. The paper also touches
Sub-Q 3 (reproducible capital without diminishing returns) at one remove: Example 3's
sα² > n case is the model's own demonstration that relaxing the upper Inada condition
reopens unbounded capital-driven per-capita growth, which is the conceptual bridge to the
AK-type argument formalized later by Rebelo (1991). The low-level-trap extension in
Section VI (endogenous population growth, Figure IX) touches Sub-Q 6 (big-push /
coordination failure) in passing, as an early formal illustration of a threshold effect,
though it is not this paper's focus and is not developed as a coordination-failure
argument in the way Rosenstein-Rodan or Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny later formalize it.
