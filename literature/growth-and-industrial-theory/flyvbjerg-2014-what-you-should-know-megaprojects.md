## Abstract

Overview of megaproject management: how large megaprojects have become (the "tera era" of
trillion-dollar projects), how much is spent on them globally, the "four sublimes" that drive
their proliferation, the "iron law of megaprojects" (over budget, over time, over and over again)
and the "break-fix model" that explains it, a critique of Hirschman's Hiding Hand theory, the
"survival of the unfittest" mechanism by which the worst-forecast projects get built, and a
closing assessment that conventional megaproject management has reached a "tension point" where
reform is emerging.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper takes stock of megaproject management as a field of study. It first establishes scale:
megaprojects are defined as large-scale, complex ventures that typically cost a billion dollars
or more, take many years to develop and build, involve multiple public and private stakeholders,
and impact millions of people (page 3). The paper traces the escalation of project cost units
from "mega" (millions, the term's literal Greek-derived meaning) through "giga" (billions, a term
that never caught on despite being technically correct once megaprojects exceeded a billion
dollars) to "tera" (trillions), arguing that stimulus packages and defense acquisition portfolios
of the scale seen after 2008 mark entry into a new "tera era" of trillion-dollar projects or
programs (pages 3-5). The stated aims are to document how big and how frequent megaprojects are,
identify what drives their proliferation (the "four sublimes"), lay out and document the "iron
law of megaprojects," introduce the "break-fix model" as an explanation for that law, revisit and
critique Albert O. Hirschman's Hiding Hand theory, explain "survival of the unfittest" (why the
worst projects tend to get built instead of the best), and assess whether conventional megaproject
management is beginning to reform.

### Methods and scope

This is a synthesis and position paper, not a new empirical study. It draws on the author's prior
peer-reviewed research and the author's megaprojects database, on named external sources (the
McKinsey Global Institute, The Economist, the UK Government Accountability Office, the Standish
Group), and on named case histories (the Channel tunnel, Sydney Opera House, Boston's Big Dig,
Denver International Airport, the UK National Health Service IT system, among others). Scope
spans megaprojects across infrastructure, oil and gas, mining, aerospace, defense, information and
communication technology (ICT), supply chains, and mega-events. Some statistics are presented as
the author's own established findings with citations to earlier Flyvbjerg-authored studies (for
example Flyvbjerg, Holm, and Buhl, 2002, 2004, 2005; Flyvbjerg and Budzier, 2011), while others are
explicitly flagged in the text as preliminary or still in progress, most notably a dam-delay
statistic described as "preliminary results from a study undertaken at Oxford University" (page
10). No new dataset, sample size, or methodology is presented in this paper itself; the reader is
referred to the cited studies for underlying data and methods.

### Key findings

**The iron law of megaprojects.** The paper states the law in the abstract and again at the close
of its central section: "Over budget, over time, over and over again" (pages 2, 11), attributed to
Flyvbjerg (2011). The paper explicitly extends this beyond infrastructure: a footnote notes that
The Economist (March 10, 2012) called the same phenomenon "the iron law of infrastructure
projects," but the author's data are said to show the law "is not limited to infrastructure; it
applies to megaprojects in general and covers benefit shortfalls in addition to cost overruns and
delays" (page 30, note 5).

**Headline overrun statistics (page 9).** "Nine out of ten such projects have cost overruns."
Overruns "of up to 50 percent in real terms are common, over 50 percent not uncommon." The paper
states overrun performance "has stayed high and constant for the 70-year period for which
comparable data exist," across all countries and continents for which data are available. Benefit
shortfalls "of up to 50 percent are also common, and above 50 percent not uncommon, again with no
signs of improvements over time and geography," citing Flyvbjerg et al. (2002, 2005).

**Sector-specific averages (page 9).** For rail projects, an average cost overrun of 44.7 percent
combines with an average demand shortfall of 51.4 percent. For roads, an average cost overrun of
20.4 percent combines with a "fifty-fifty risk that demand is also wrong by more than 20 percent."

**Named example overruns cited in the running text (page 9).** Channel tunnel, 80 percent (real
terms); Denver International Airport, 200 percent; Boston's Big Dig, 220 percent; UK National
Health Service IT system, 400 to 700 percent; Sydney Opera House, 1,400 percent. Table 2 (page 29,
titled "Large-scale projects have a calamitous history of cost overrun") separately tabulates 34
named projects with a single overrun percentage each, ranging from 50 percent (Great Belt east
bridge, Denmark) up to 1,900 percent (Suez Canal, Egypt). Selected entries from that table: Suez
Canal 1,900 percent; Scottish Parliament Building 1,600 percent; Sydney Opera House 1,400 percent;
Montreal Summer Olympics 1,300 percent; Concorde 1,100 percent; Canadian Firearms Registry 590
percent; National Health Service IT system, UK, 550 percent; Boston's Big Dig 220 percent; Denver
International Airport 200 percent; Channel tunnel 80 percent. Note an internal inconsistency: the
running text gives the NHS IT system overrun as "400-700 percent" (page 9), while Table 2 lists it
as a single figure of 550 percent (page 29); both figures appear in this paper as published, and
the summary reports both rather than resolving the discrepancy.

**Channel tunnel case study (pages 9-10).** At its initial public offering, Eurotunnel told
investors that 10 percent "would be a reasonable allowance for the possible impact of unforeseen
circumstances on construction costs." Actual outcomes: 80 percent over budget for construction,
140 percent over budget for financing, revenues at half of forecast, an internal rate of return of
minus 14.5 percent, and a total loss to the British economy of 17.8 billion US dollars. The paper
quotes an ex post economic evaluation concluding "the British Economy would have been better off
had the Tunnel never been constructed" (Anguera, 2006, cited at page 10). Other named non-viable
megaprojects: Sydney's Lane Cove tunnel, the high-speed rail links to Stockholm and Oslo airports,
the Copenhagen metro, and Denmark's Great Belt tunnel.

**ICT project overruns (page 10).** "One in six such projects become a statistical outlier in
terms of cost overrun with an average overrun for outliers of 200 percent in real terms." This is
described as a 2,000 percent overincidence of outliers compared to normal, and a 200 percent
overincidence compared to large construction projects (citing Flyvbjerg and Budzier, 2011). Total
US ICT project waste from failed and underperforming projects is estimated at 55 billion dollars
annually (Standish Group, 2009).

**Delay statistics (page 10).** "Preliminary results" from an Oxford University study, described
as based on "the largest database of its kind," suggest dam delays average 45 percent (a
10-year-planned dam takes 14.5 years on average). Separately, Flyvbjerg et al. (2004) modeled that
a one-year delay in the implementation phase correlates with an average 4.64 percentage-point
increase in cost overrun. Applied illustratively to London's 26-billion-dollar Crossrail project,
a one-year delay would cost 1.2 billion dollars extra, or 3.3 million dollars per day (this
Crossrail figure is a derived illustration, not a reported outcome).

**Success rate and the specific 47.9/8.5/0.5 verification (page 11).** The paper does NOT contain
the cascade "47.9% on budget, 8.5% on budget and on time, 0.5% on budget, time, and benefits."
Those exact figures were checked against the full extracted text (including notes and references)
and do not appear anywhere in this document. What the paper does contain, in its place, is a
cruder illustrative approximation: "If, as the evidence indicates, approximately one out of ten
megaprojects is on budget, one out of ten is on schedule, and one out of ten is on benefits, then
approximately one in a thousand projects is a success, defined as on target for all three." The
paper immediately adds a robustness check on this same estimate: "Even if the numbers were wrong
by a factor two - so that two, instead of one, out of ten projects were on target for cost,
schedule, and benefits, respectively - the success rate would still be dismal, now eight in a
thousand." This one-in-ten-per-dimension, one-in-a-thousand (or, under the factor-two check,
eight-in-a-thousand) framing is presented as an illustration of the iron law rather than as a
measured statistic with a named sample size; no dataset, project count, or citation-specific
percentage breakdown accompanies it. It is a distinct and much rounder set of figures than the
47.9/8.5/0.5 cascade the verification task asked about, and the paper gives no indication that a
more precise breakdown of this kind exists within it.

**Rare successes named (pages 10-11, 18).** The Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (part of the Basque
Abandoibarra urban regeneration project) is named as built on time, on budget, and delivering
promised benefits. Recent Madrid metro extensions (Flyvbjerg, 2005) and a number of industrial
projects (Merrow, 2011) are also named as on-budget, on-time examples. The Paris-Lyon high-speed
rail line and the London Docklands light railway extension are named as built to budget. The
paper states that, as part of ongoing research, the author and associates have tried and so far
failed to assemble a large enough sample of successful megaprojects to permit statistically valid
analysis of success, because "success is so rare in megaproject management that at present it can
be studied only as small-sample research, whereas failure may be studied with large samples of
projects" (page 11).

**Global scale and spending.** The combined cost of the Joint Strike Fighter program and China's
high-speed rail project is put at 700 billion dollars, over half of the roughly one-trillion-dollar
US debt to China (page 3). Global megaproject spending across infrastructure, oil and gas, mining,
aerospace, defense, ICT, supply chains, and mega-events is estimated at 6-9 trillion US dollars
annually, approximately 8 percent of total global GDP (pages 5-6), on top of the McKinsey Global
Institute's narrower estimate of 3.4 trillion dollars per year for global infrastructure spending
alone, 2013-2030 (page 5).

**The four sublimes (Table 1, pages 6-8).** Four drivers are named for the increasing size and
frequency of megaprojects: the political sublime (the rapture politicians get from building
monuments to themselves and their causes, and the public and media visibility this generates); the
technological sublime (a term the paper attributes to Frick, 2008, building on Miller, 1965, and
Marx, 1967: the excitement engineers and technologists get from pushing the envelope of what is
possible); the economic sublime (the delight business people and trade unions get from the money
and jobs megaprojects generate for contractors, workers, consultants, bankers, investors,
landowners, lawyers, and developers); and the aesthetic sublime (the pleasure designers and
design-lovers get from something very large that is also iconic and beautiful, such as the Golden
Gate Bridge or the Sydney Opera House).

**The break-fix model (page 12).** The paper describes a "megaprojects paradox": demand for
megaprojects and their size and frequency have never been greater, while performance on cost,
schedule, and benefits has not improved over the 70-year period for which comparable data exist.
The paper attributes this persistence to a "break-fix model" of megaproject delivery (a term
credited to Dr. Patrick O'Connell, page 30, note 6): planners and managers generally do not know
how, or lack incentive, to deliver megaprojects successfully, so projects "break" when reality
catches up with optimistic or manipulated estimates, and are then paused, reorganized, and
sometimes refinanced in an attempt to "fix" them and salvage some version of the original plan.
Lock-in and escalation of commitment make outright cancellation politically and financially
difficult, which the paper says is why megaprojects have been called the "Vietnams" of policy and
management, "easy to begin and difficult and expensive to stop" (citing White, 2012, page 12).

**Hirschman's Hiding Hand, revisited and critiqued (pages 13-17).** The paper summarizes
Hirschman's (1967a) Principle of the Hiding Hand, under which underestimating a project's
difficulty is offset by an equal and opposite underestimate of human creativity in overcoming that
difficulty, so that ignorance of true costs is, in Hirschman's account, functionally necessary to
get worthwhile projects started at all. The paper also summarizes Sawyer's (1952) closely related
concept of "creative error." The paper's critique is that both theories rest on small, biased
samples: "Hirschman studied only 11 projects, or a few more if we count subprojects, Sawyer ten to
15" (page 16), and that Sawyer himself warned his study was a "marginal and distinctly limited
note" that deliberately excluded failed projects. The paper further argues the theories'
asymmetrical assumption, that cost estimates are pessimistic while benefit estimates are optimistic
so that errors cancel, is "solidly disproved" by Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory and later
behavioral-economics work, which instead finds optimism bias applies to both cost and benefit
estimates simultaneously, so errors reinforce rather than cancel each other (page 16). The Sydney
Opera House is used at length (pages 14-15) as an illustration of the human cost of relying on the
Hiding Hand: the paper recounts that the cost overrun and resulting controversy drove architect
Jørn Utzon from Australia and from major commissions for the rest of his career, making him "that
most tragic figure in architecture, the one-building-architect" (page 15). The paper concludes that
"the iron law of megaprojects... trumps Hirschman's Hiding Hand at a high level of statistical
significance," and that the theories with genuine explanatory power are instead "optimism bias,
the planning fallacy, strategic misrepresentation, and principal-agent behavior" (page 17).

**Survival of the unfittest (pages 17-19).** The paper argues that cost underestimation and
benefit overestimation, rather than being a necessary and benign "creative error," produce an
inverted Darwinism: the projects that look best on paper (because they carry the largest cost
underestimates and benefit overestimates) are systematically the projects that will perform worst
in reality, since the size of the underestimate on paper predicts the size of the overrun in
practice. The paper terms this "survival of the unfittest" (citing Flyvbjerg, 2009), meaning the
worst-forecast projects, not the best projects, are the ones that get approved and built. The
paper rejects the "nothing-would-ever-get-built" justification for deliberate misrepresentation on
three grounds: it produces economically inefficient (Pareto-inefficient) resource allocation; it is
frequently unlawful or unethical under legal "obligation to truth" requirements in democracies and
under corporate-governance legislation such as Sarbanes-Oxley; and it is empirically unnecessary,
since sufficiently good projects (the paper again names the Guggenheim Bilbao, the Paris-Lyon
high-speed line, and the London Docklands light railway extension) exist and can be delivered on
budget without misrepresentation.

**Signs of reform ("Light at the End of the Tunnel?", pages 19-21).** The paper's own proposed
mitigation is reference-class forecasting, cited alongside front-end management and institutional
design for accountability: "with a better understanding of causes has followed a better grasp of
cures, from front-end management (Williams and Samset, 2010) to reference class forecasting
(Kahneman, 2011: 243-254; Flyvbjerg, 2006) to institutional design for better accountability (Scott,
2012; Bruzelius et al., 1998)" (page 21). The paper also documents institutional developments it
treats as reform signals: the UK Treasury's Green Book process and requirement that ministries
account for "optimism bias" in project appraisal (page 21); the 2011 creation of the UK Major
Projects Authority and, in 2012, the Major Projects Leadership Academy with Oxford University
(page 20); parliamentary inquiries in Victoria, Australia, and the Netherlands (page 21); and cases
where megaproject failure visibly cost executives their jobs or damaged national economies,
including the Airbus A380, BP's Deepwater Horizon spill, Kmart's ICT system failure, China's
high-speed rail corruption scandal, Hong Kong's new airport opening problems, the 2004 Athens
Olympics' contribution to Greece's 2011 debt default, and the Fukushima nuclear accident's impact
on Japan's national economy (pages 19-20).

### Limitations

The paper is explicitly a synthesis and position piece rather than a study reporting a new,
fully-specified dataset; most headline statistics are attributed to earlier Flyvbjerg-authored
studies cited by year rather than re-derived here, so sample sizes, time windows, and methods for
those figures must be checked in the underlying sources, not in this paper. The paper itself flags
the dam-delay statistic (45 percent average delay) as "preliminary results" from a study still in
progress (page 10). The one-in-ten-per-dimension, one-in-a-thousand success-rate framing (page 11)
is presented by the paper as an illustrative deduction from the roughly "one in ten" overrun
frequency asserted elsewhere in the same paper, not as a measured statistic drawn from a specific
sample with a stated project count; the paper gives no citation or database reference specific to
that one-in-a-thousand figure itself. As addressed above under Key findings, the widely cited
"47.9% on budget, 8.5% on budget and on time, 0.5% on budget, time, and benefits" cascade does NOT
appear in this 2014 paper; it is not a statistic this document reports, in any form, exact or
approximate, and any attribution of that specific cascade to this source should be treated as
incorrect. Table 2's cost-overrun figures are given without listed sample dates or currencies of
measurement (real versus nominal terms) for the table as a whole, and the paper does not resolve
the internal inconsistency between its own text figure (400-700 percent) and table figure (550
percent) for the UK National Health Service IT system. Success-rate figures for on-budget,
on-schedule, and on-benefit delivery, taken individually, are asserted in prose without an
accompanying citation to a specific dataset in this paper. No funding source or conflict-of-interest
statement was found in the extracted text; the author does disclose (page 30, note 9) personal
involvement in planning and delivering the UK Major Projects Leadership Academy discussed favorably
in the paper.

### Topic mapping

FA8. Source list entry 1. Sub-Q 3. This paper is the base-rate source for the red team's
disconfirmation case against treating a lunar industrial program as a conventional large
engineering effort: it establishes, in the author's own words, that megaprojects in general (not
only infrastructure) are subject to an "iron law" of chronic cost overrun, schedule overrun, and
benefit shortfall, with a rough one-in-a-thousand (or, on a looser check, eight-in-a-thousand)
estimated rate of full success across all three dimensions simultaneously (page 11). For Sub-Q 3's
concern with realistic cost and schedule expectations for unprecedented-scale programs, the
paper's named examples (the Channel tunnel, Sydney Opera House, Boston's Big Dig, Denver
International Airport, the UK NHS IT system) and its sector-level averages for rail and road
projects supply concrete base rates and named precedents, while its explanatory apparatus
(optimism bias, strategic misrepresentation, principal-agent problems, the break-fix model, and
"survival of the unfittest") supplies mechanisms a red team could argue would apply with at least
equal force to a program of unprecedented scale and novelty. The paper's own proposed mitigation,
reference-class forecasting, together with front-end management and institutional accountability
design, is the countermeasure this paper itself proposes rather than an external rebuttal, and is
reported here as such. As established above, the specific 47.9/8.5/0.5 cascade attributed
elsewhere to this source is not present in this paper and should not be cited to it; the paper's
own comparable statistic is the cruder one-in-ten-per-dimension, one-in-a-thousand approximation
on page 11.
