# Endogenous Technological Change

## Citation

Romer, P. M. (1990). Endogenous technological change. *Journal of Political Economy, 98*(5, Part 2), S71-S102.

DOI: 10.1086/261725 (confirmed in FA2 ledger)

Publisher URL: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261725

## Abstract

This paper models economic growth as the output of deliberate, profit-seeking R&D investment rather than an exogenous process. Technology occupies an unusual category in the model: unlike a conventional good it can be used by everyone at once (it is nonrival), yet unlike a pure public good its use can still be partially fenced off through patents (partially excludable). That combination breaks the convexity assumptions behind price-taking markets, so the model instead clears through monopolistic competition among firms holding exclusive rights to their designs. Working through the resulting equilibrium, growth turns out to track the economy's stock of human capital, and the market allocates less human capital to research than a social planner would choose. Trade integration that pools human capital across countries speeds up growth, while population size on its own does nothing for it.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens with a scale-setting empirical observation: output per hour worked in the United States is roughly 10 times as valuable as it was 100 years ago (citing Maddison 1982), a gain 1950s growth accountants (Abramovitz 1956; Kendrick 1956; Solow 1957) attributed almost entirely to technological change.

Romer's illustrative example for what "technological change" means concretely: the raw material iron oxide is unchanged over that century, but a hundred years ago the only use anyone had for it was as a pigment, whereas today it coats plastic tape and stores videocassette recordings — the change lies entirely in the instructions for combining raw materials, not in the materials themselves.

Romer sets out to build a growth model resting on three premises stated explicitly in the introduction.

1. Technological change — improved instructions for combining raw materials — is the engine of growth, so the model nests inside a Solow-type framework.
2. Technological change is *endogenous*, arising from intentional, profit-motivated investment by firms and individuals, not handed down exogenously.
3. The instructions (designs, blueprints, formulas) that constitute "technology" are inherently different from conventional goods: once the fixed cost of creating them is paid, they can be reused at zero additional cost.

The paper's organizing claim is that these three premises, taken together, are jointly inconsistent with price-taking equilibrium.

Section II proves this formally via a replication argument, which is why the model must be built on monopolistic competition (Dixit-Stiglitz style) rather than the perfectly competitive apparatus used by Solow (1956) and by the "learning-by-doing" line (Arrow 1962a; Romer 1986) that preceded it.

The paper is explicitly positioned as a successor to Romer's own 1986 and 1987 papers, correcting what Romer identifies as their central flaw: those models made knowledge accumulation either purely incidental (tied in fixed proportion to physical capital, as in learning-by-doing) or driven by an ad hoc "scale" variable (population, in Romer 1987) without a coherent research technology.

This paper's stated innovation is to make human capital, not population, the scale variable that drives growth, and to model research and development as an explicit, patent-generating economic sector with its own production function.

**Provenance.** The paper was prepared for a 1988 conference at the State University of New York at Buffalo ("The Problem of Economic Development: Exploring Economic Development through Free Enterprise"), with discussants Rob Vishny and Dale Jorgenson, and was supported by two National Science Foundation grants. It was published in a special *JPE* supplement issue (vol. 98, no. 5, pt. 2, 1990) alongside the companion Becker, Murphy, and Tamura (1990) paper it repeatedly cross-references.

### Methods and scope

This is a pure theory paper: an analytically solved general-equilibrium growth model, not an empirical study.

It contains no original data. The one empirical citation offered as corroborating evidence (Sokoloff 1988; Sokoloff & Khan 1989, discussed in Section VII, on U.S. county-level patenting and access to navigable waterways) is used narratively, not re-estimated.

**Rivalry/excludability taxonomy (Section II).** Romer classifies goods along two independent dimensions:

- *Rivalry* — a purely technological attribute: does one agent's use of the good preclude another's use?
- *Excludability* — a function of technology and law: can the owner prevent others from using it?

Four cells follow from crossing these dimensions:

- Rival + excludable: conventional economic goods, privately provided and competitively traded.
- Nonrival + nonexcludable: public goods (e.g., basic scientific research), which cannot be privately provided.
- Rival + excludable: human capital — skills tied to a physical body, lost when the person is unavailable or dies.
- Nonrival + partially excludable: the paper's subject. A "design" for a producer durable can be copied and used without limit (nonrival), but the designer holds a patent restricting its manufacturing use (excludable), while other researchers remain free to study and learn from the disclosed design (nonexcludable in the research use).

This last cell is what separates Romer's formulation from Lucas (1988) and from pure human-capital growth models (King & Rebelo 1987; Rebelo 1988; Becker, Murphy & Tamura 1990), all of which treat intangible knowledge as rivalrous, like ordinary human capital.

**The nonconvexity argument.** If output F(A, X) depends on a nonrival input A together with an exhaustive list of rival inputs X, the standard replication argument gives:

F(A, λX) = λF(A, X)

for any λ, because only the rival inputs need to be replicated to scale up production. If A is also productive, it follows that:

F(λA, λX) > λF(A, X)

The production function cannot be homogeneous of degree one, hence cannot be concave, hence cannot support price-taking: paying every input its marginal product would bankrupt the firm.

Romer traces how earlier models dodged this conclusion:

- Solow (1956) and Shell (1966, 1967) make A an exogenous or government-provided public input that earns no compensation.
- Arrow's (1962a) learning-by-doing makes A a pure, nonexcludable byproduct of capital accumulation.
- Romer's own 1986 paper tied the excludable component of knowledge to capital in fixed proportion — a formulation Dasgupta and Stiglitz (1988) show is not robust once the input is even partially excludable.

The only way to keep all three premises, Romer argues, is to introduce explicit market power — hence monopolistic competition with free entry, building on Dixit and Stiglitz (1977), Judd (1985), and Grossman and Helpman (1989c).

Romer positions Lucas (1988) — FA2 ledger entry 5 — as occupying the same public-good corner of the taxonomy as Arrow's learning-by-doing: Lucas assumes, in effect, that it is the production of human capital rather than physical capital that generates the nonrival, nonexcludable good driving growth, but the byproduct is still unintentional and uncompensated, exactly like learning-by-doing. Both Arrow-style and Lucas-style formulations therefore satisfy premises one and three (technology drives growth; technology is nonrival) while violating premise two, since neither gives an intentional, profit-seeking research decision a role. This is the specific gap Romer's monopolistic-competition apparatus is built to close.

The continuum-of-goods formulation used here is close to Judd's (1985) treatment of patents, and Romer identifies his own model as corresponding to Judd's case of infinitely lived patents. The stated differences: Judd treats the differentiated goods as consumption goods rather than producer durables, and Judd's model still relies on an exogenous source of technological change to generate productivity growth *within* the research sector, whereas here growth in A raises the productivity of human capital in research directly and endogenously, with no outside driver.

**Formal structure — three sectors (Section III).** The economy has a fixed population, fixed aggregate labor supply L, and a fixed aggregate human-capital endowment H, both assumptions imposed "largely for technical reasons" to keep the model on a constant-growth-rate balanced path.

There are four basic inputs: capital (measured in units of consumption goods), labor L (physical skills such as eye-hand coordination, measured by headcount), human capital H (the cumulative effect of formal education and on-the-job training, distinct from labor), and the technology index A. Romer is deliberately narrow about what "human capital" means here: it follows the labor-market and growth-accounting usage (Heckman 1976; Rosen 1976; Gollop & Jorgenson 1980), person-specific years of education and experience — a *more limited* concept than the "unlimited human capital accumulation" used in Lucas (1988), King and Rebelo (1987), and Becker, Murphy, and Tamura (1990), which implicitly blend rival, person-specific human capital together with a nonrival, immortal knowledge component. Romer's model deliberately separates the two: H is the rival component, and A is split off as the separate, nonrival, unbounded-growth component.

*Research sector.* Human capital devoted to research, H_A, combines with the existing stock of designs A to produce new designs. The core law of motion (equation 3):

Ȧ = δ · H_A · A

where δ is a research-productivity parameter. Any researcher has free, uncompensated access to the *entire* existing stock of knowledge A — nonrivalry in use — which is why an engineer today is more productive than one 100 years ago holding human capital fixed. Output of new designs is linear in H_A (a functional-form convenience) and linear in A (the assumption that carries the paper's dynamics; see Key findings below).

*Intermediate/producer-durables sector.* A distinct monopolist firm exists for every invented design i. Owning a patent, it converts η units of forgone final output into one unit of durable i and rents it to final-goods producers. It faces a downward-sloping demand curve (equation 4) and sets a constant monopoly markup over marginal cost:

p(i) = [1 / (1 − α − β)] × marginal cost

Free entry into design-creation drives the price of a new design, P_A, up to the present discounted value of the resulting monopoly-profit stream. The zero-profit, no-arbitrage condition (equation 6′):

π(t) = r(t) · P_A(t)

says instantaneous excess revenue over marginal cost must just cover the interest cost of the original design investment.

Working backward from that condition, the monopolist's underlying pricing problem (equation 5) is a firm with constant marginal cost facing a constant-elasticity demand curve: it chooses output x to maximize p(x)·x − r·η·x at every date. Because the model assumes capital is "putty-putty" (durables can be costlessly reconverted back into general capital), the firm can resolve this problem afresh at each instant, so the demand for durables is stationary in equilibrium and no disinvestment ever occurs. The only sunk cost in the whole model is the original expenditure on the design itself.

*Final-goods sector.* A competitive, constant-returns aggregate (equation 1/1′):

Y(H_Y, L, x) = H_Y^α · L^β · ∫ x(i)^(1−α−β) di

a Cobb-Douglas-type function extended to a continuum of *additively separable* differentiated durables x(i) — no two durables are close substitutes (the "no-neighboring-goods" property, Pascoa 1986). This is the one competitive, price-taking sector; its homogeneity of degree one lets it be represented by a single aggregate firm even though the underlying capital stock is infinitely disaggregated across durable types.

Human capital allocates between the two using sectors according to H_Y + H_A = H, with a market-clearing condition requiring the wage of human capital (its marginal product in final goods) to equal its earnings in research (P_A · δ · A). Consumers are Ramsey optimizers with constant relative risk aversion σ and discount rate ρ, giving the standard Euler relation:

g_C = (r − ρ) / σ

The paper solves only for the *balanced growth path* — A, K, and Y all growing at the same constant exponential rate. Transition dynamics from an arbitrary initial capital/knowledge ratio are explicitly left unsolved: "this analysis is not attempted here."

**Equilibrium definition.** An equilibrium is a set of price and quantity paths such that, simultaneously:

1. Consumers choose savings and consumption optimally, taking interest rates as given.
2. Holders of human capital choose between the research sector and the manufacturing sector, taking as given the stock of knowledge A, the price of designs P_A, and the manufacturing wage.
3. Final-goods producers choose labor, human capital, and the full list of differentiated durables, taking all prices as given.
4. Each firm owning a design and manufacturing a durable maximizes profit, taking the interest rate as given and setting prices along the downward-sloping demand curve it faces.
5. Firms contemplating entry into durable-good production take the price of designs as given.
6. Supply equals demand for every good.

A terminological note carried from the paper's first footnote: an earlier draft used "embodied" versus "disembodied" to distinguish human capital (tied to a person) from a design (not tied to any physical object); Romer dropped that language in the published version because "embodiment" already carries a different meaning elsewhere in growth theory, in favor of the public-finance vocabulary of rivalry.

The consumer's integral of discounted utility also imposes a technical restriction on the parameter space: for the value of the objective to remain finite, the rate of growth of current utility, (1 − σ)g, must be strictly less than the discount rate ρ. If this fails, Romer notes, the integral can diverge and an overtaking criterion would be needed instead — a boundary case the paper does not pursue further.

**Intuition via Solow and Uzawa (Section IV).** Before the formal solution, Romer frames the model's behavior by reference to two earlier benchmarks. For a fixed stock of designs A, the model reduces to the Solow (1956) model exactly: by symmetry, every invented durable is supplied at the same level x, so output can be rewritten (equation 7) as

Y = (H_Y·A)^α · (L·A)^β · K^(1−α−β) · η^(−α+β,1)

which is the familiar neoclassical form with labor- and human-capital-augmenting technology, exhibiting ordinary diminishing returns to K alone. If A instead grew at an exogenously fixed exponential rate, the economy would converge to a Solow-style path with K growing at that same rate. What is new is the Uzawa (1965) piece: because equation (3) is linear in A, A itself can grow at a constant endogenous exponential rate whenever H_A is held constant, so the whole system can sit on a balanced growth path without any exogenously imposed rate of technical change.

Figure 1 in the source illustrates the resulting steady state directly: a rectangle whose height is the constant per-durable usage level x and whose width is the (growing) range of invented durables A(t); the rectangle's area, A(t)·x, is total capital divided by η. Over time the rectangle only widens (A grows) while its height (x) stays fixed — the diagram's entire content is that composition of capital shifts toward more distinct durable types rather than more of each type, which is why it is treated here as a supporting illustration rather than a load-bearing numerical result.

### Key findings

**The growth-rate expression.** Combining the free-entry/no-arbitrage condition on the research sector with the labor-allocation condition yields (equation 11′):

g = δ · H_A = δH − Λr

where:

Λ = (1 − α − β) / (α + β)   (equation 12)

is a constant built from the final-goods technology's factor shares and the monopoly markup, 1/(1 − α − β), attached to intermediate goods. Substituting the consumer Euler equation (r = ρ + σg) to close the model gives the balanced-growth-path solution (equation 13):

**g = (δH − Λρ) / (Λσ + 1)**

This single equation is described by Romer as containing "almost all the content of the model." Its properties:

- Increasing in **H**, total human capital — the paper's central positive result: "the stock of human capital determines the rate of growth."
- Increasing in **δ**, the productivity of research.
- Decreasing in **ρ** (impatience) and, algebraically, in Λ (a lower monopoly markup, or higher intermediate-goods cost share, raises growth by raising the return researchers can extract).
- **Independent of L** (population/labor) and of **η** (the unit cost of converting output into a durable).

This independence from L is flagged explicitly as *not* a general property of the functional forms chosen. For this specification, an increase in L raises the market faced by every monopolist and simultaneously raises the manufacturing wage by exactly enough to leave the research/manufacturing allocation of human capital unchanged — the two effects cancel exactly.

Consequence, stated by Romer directly: "having a large population is not sufficient to generate growth." Scale operates through H, not through population size. This is offered as the explanation for why large-population, low-human-capital economies (Romer's own examples: China, India) do not automatically grow fast, and why market integration — effectively pooling H across countries — raises growth even without population growth. Section VII works this through explicitly: two identical closed economies each growing at g(H) grow faster once integrated and jointly facing g(2H).

**Historical corroboration cited (Section VII).** Romer leans on two studies by Sokoloff as the closest available natural experiment. Sokoloff (1988) finds that U.S. counties with access to navigable waterways in the early nineteenth century had higher rates of patenting than counties without such access, and — more persuasively for identifying a causal channel — that the opening of a new canal or the dredging of a river was followed by a sharp increase in patenting in the adjacent counties. Sokoloff and Khan (1989) add that a substantial group of broadly skilled individuals moved in and out of research activity in response to aggregate disturbances (business cycles, a British trade embargo), consistent with the model's premise that human capital used in research has an active outside option in manufacturing/commerce, and that the short-run supply of human capital to research was fairly elastic in that period. Romer treats this as supportive but limited: it corroborates that market size and integration move research effort, not that the specific functional form or the resulting growth-rate magnitude is correct.

A footnote in Section VII also flags that, between the paper's first draft and its final revision, Grossman and Helpman (1989a, 1989b) had independently worked out detailed trade-and-growth models built on the same underlying nonrival-technology structure, which Romer cites as parallel confirmation that the framework generalizes to explicit multi-country trade settings rather than remaining a two-region thought experiment.

**Escaping Solow deceleration — and why.** With A fixed, the model collapses algebraically to a standard neoclassical (Solow-type) production function in K with diminishing returns (equation 7), reproducing ordinary transitional dynamics and convergence to a stationary K/A ratio.

What prevents the whole economy from decelerating à la Solow is that A itself does *not* face diminishing returns: equation (3) is linear in A, so the marginal productivity of a researcher's human capital keeps growing in proportion to the existing stock of knowledge rather than flattening out.

Romer is explicit that this is a modeling choice, not a derived result: linearity in A is "more like an assumption than a result of the model," in his own words — it is what makes unbounded growth possible at all. He defends the choice only on the grounds that there was, as of 1990, "no evidence from recent history" that research opportunities were diminishing — explicitly conceding that whether this holds indefinitely "is an empirical question that this kind of theory cannot resolve."

**Nonrivalry, increasing returns, and the scale effect.** Because doubling both human capital and the existing knowledge stock more than doubles the productivity of a researcher — equation (4) shows the research sector's constant-elasticity demand curve exhibits genuine increasing returns, not constant returns — a permanent increase in the *total* stock of human capital produces a more-than-proportional increase in the share of human capital devoted to research. This is illustrated in Figure 2, a plot of g and H_A against total H (drawn for δ = 1).

This is the scale-effect property built into the model: more researchers (more H_A, driven by more H) mechanically imply a permanently faster growth rate, with no ceiling supplied by the model's own mathematics — g in equation (13) rises linearly and without bound as H or δ is increased.

This is exactly the prediction later targeted by Jones (1995) (FA2 ledger entry 10), who argues that sustained twentieth-century increases in the number of researchers in advanced economies were not accompanied by a corresponding permanent acceleration of growth rates, empirically undermining the strong scale-effect class of models to which Romer (1990) belongs.

**The bridge to reproducible-capital (AK) models.** Structurally, equation (3) — Ȧ = δ·H_A·A — is an AK-type accumulation equation transplanted into idea-space rather than physical-capital space: the "stock" being accumulated (A) is a direct, linear input into its own rate of production, exactly the mechanism that gives Rebelo's (1991) literal AK model (FA2 ledger entry 3) its undecelerating constant growth rate. The difference is what plays the role of the reproducing stock. In Rebelo's AK model, broadly defined physical/human capital reproduces itself directly, with no separate research sector. In Romer (1990), it is *disembodied knowledge* — nonrival and only partially excludable — that reproduces without diminishing returns, while physical capital K continues to face the ordinary diminishing returns of equation (7). This is the sense in which Romer (1990) is a narrower, more mechanism-explicit cousin of the AK class: it locates the undiminished-returns engine specifically in the technology stock rather than in capital broadly defined, and it prices that engine's output through a market (patents, monopoly rents) rather than leaving it as an unpriced externality.

**Stagnation/threshold result.** Because H_A cannot be negative, if total human capital H is too low, the nonnegativity constraint binds: H_A = 0 and g = 0. Every feasible growth rate for A is too low relative to the discount rate ρ to justify sacrificing current consumption.

Romer offers this as a candidate explanation for cross-country growth variation, and for the historical absence of growth before human capital could be "spared" from subsistence production — while noting the model cannot fully explain this since H itself is exogenous, not accumulated within the model.

Figure 2 therefore carries two results at once, both driven by the same underlying increasing-returns mechanism: below a threshold level of H, the curve for g sits at zero (the stagnation region); above that threshold, g rises and, because H_A rises more than proportionally with H, does so at an increasing rate. Romer reads the figure as consistent with two cross-cutting observations — that both the level of human capital and the research share of human capital are higher today than at any point in the past, and that the research share is apparently highest in the most developed economies.

**Underinvestment in research (welfare result, Section VI).** The decentralized equilibrium growth rate g (equation 13) is below the social planner's optimum g\* (equation 14, derived in the Appendix via a current-value Hamiltonian). Two distortions drive the gap:

1. New designs raise the productivity of *future* researchers through the nonexcludable spillover embedded in equation (3) — a benefit the market price of a design cannot capture.
2. The intermediate-goods monopolist appropriates only a fraction (1 − α − β) of the social value a new design creates, because the price of durables exceeds marginal cost.

On the second point, Romer is quantitatively explicit: equation (7) shows a new design (one additional unit of A) raises output at every future date by H_Y·L·x^(1−α−β), while the calculation underlying the monopoly-pricing problem (equation 5) shows the design's producer captures only the fraction (1 − α − β) of that social benefit as private profit. The remainder accrues to final-goods producers and consumers as a pure externality the inventor cannot price.

The optimal solution replaces the markup-inflated constant Λ with its markup-free analogue θ = α/(α + β) in the growth-rate formula, which raises the optimal research share of human capital and yields g\* > g at every admissible parameter value.

Implied policy conclusion: a subsidy to *physical* capital accumulation (equivalent to reducing η) is "a weak, and possibly counterproductive" lever for growth, since η does not appear in the growth-rate equation at all. What does raise growth: a lower effective interest rate faced by research, a direct subsidy to research employment (equivalent to raising δ), or — second-best, since the model treats H as fixed — a subsidy to human-capital accumulation itself.

Romer contrasts this with his own earlier learning-by-doing-style model (1986) and with Arrow (1962a): in both, the rate of growth of A was mechanically tied to the rate of growth of K, so an investment tax credit for physical capital automatically sped up A as well. Decoupling the research decision from the physical-capital decision, as this paper does, breaks that equivalence.

The Appendix derives g\* by constructing a current-value Hamiltonian over consumption C and the research-allocation control H_A, subject to the capital-accumulation constraint, the design-accumulation constraint (equation 3), and H_Y + H_A = H. The first-order conditions (equations A1-A3) characterize the co-state variables on capital and knowledge; imposing the balanced-growth requirements that the growth rates of the co-state on capital and of consumption coincide reduces the system to a single equation (A4) in H, H_A, and the technology parameters, from which equation (14) follows algebraically. Romer does not carry out this derivation for the transition path, only for the balanced-growth point.

**FA2 verdict — does the model admit a constant 24-month doubling (~41 percent per year)?**

Answer: mechanically yes, but only on an assumption the model does not discipline; empirically no for any economy resembling an actual national population.

A 24-month doubling implies, on a discrete annual-compounding convention, g ≈ 2^(1/2) − 1 ≈ 0.414/year (about 41.4 percent per year), the figure carried in the FA2 ledger. Because Romer's model is stated in continuous time, the more natural benchmark is the instantaneous exponential rate satisfying e^(2g) = 2, i.e., g = ln(2)/2 ≈ 0.347/year (about 34.7 percent per year continuously compounded). The two figures describe the same doubling time under different compounding conventions.

The reduced-form balanced-growth equation, g = (δH − Λρ)/(Λσ + 1), places **no intrinsic ceiling** on g: it is linear and increasing without bound in both H (total human capital) and δ (research productivity). This is exactly what the nonrivalry/increasing-returns mechanism is built to deliver — since equation (3) is *linear* in the existing stock of ideas A, there is no analogue of Solow's diminishing marginal product of capital to force deceleration as the "capital" being accumulated (designs) grows. In that sense the model's functional form cannot rule out a constant 41-percent-per-year (or any other constant) growth path.

But hitting that number is not free. Since g = δ · H_A identically — true at every point in time, not only along a balanced-growth path, from equations (3), (10), and (11) — a continuously-compounded g ≈ 0.35/year (or ≈ 0.41/year under the discrete convention) requires:

δ · H_A ≈ 0.35 to 0.41 per year

That is, the flow of new designs generated per unit time would need to equal roughly a third to two-fifths of the *entire existing stock of designs*, every year, indefinitely.

Romer's own model further constrains this. Total human capital H is *exogenously fixed* in this paper ("the total stock of human capital in the population is fixed," Section III), and H_A ≤ H with H_Y = H − H_A required to be strictly positive — otherwise final output, and hence all consumption, is zero, which is inconsistent with the Ramsey households the model assumes. So for any *given*, finite H, the feasible growth rate is bounded above by δH, approached only in the economically infeasible limit H_Y → 0.

The paper supplies no calibrated values for δ or H, and offers no argument for why either should be bounded to realistic magnitudes — the result is a purely qualitative, comparative-statics one. For context (not drawn from the paper itself, since Romer performs no calibration here): historically observed aggregate productivity/knowledge growth in advanced economies runs at roughly one to a few percent per year — one to two orders of magnitude below the δ·H_A ≈ 0.35-0.41/year the 24-month-doubling band would require.

Reaching that band under this model's mechanics would demand either a research-productivity parameter δ or an aggregate research-employment stock H_A far outside any historically observed range. The model does not forbid this by construction, but it supplies no economic mechanism that would produce it for a population-scale economy — and its own scale-effect property (more H implies proportionally faster g, with no saturation) is precisely the feature later shown empirically implausible by Jones (1995).

**Verdict for the FA2 arithmetic table:** admits a constant 24-month doubling in principle, on the assumption of an unrealistically large δ·H_A product with no in-model bound to rule it out; it is not a mechanism that plausibly delivers that rate for anything resembling a national population's human-capital endowment.

**Romer's own summary (Section VIII, Conclusions).** Romer describes the model as, in essence, the one-sector neoclassical growth model with an endogenous explanation added for the source of technological change. He restates the most robust welfare conclusion as the sensitivity of research investment to the interest rate — since research trades a current cost for a future stream of benefits — and restates the central positive claim: an economy with a larger total stock of human capital experiences faster growth, which he offers as a candidate explanation both for why free trade can speed up growth and for why growth accelerated to historically unprecedented rates in developed twentieth-century economies. He closes by flagging, not resolving, the question of where the underlying stock of human capital itself comes from.

### Limitations

- **No calibration.** The paper is purely theoretical; it never assigns numerical values to δ, H, α, β, σ, or ρ, so no quantitative growth-rate prediction is directly testable from the text itself.
- **Unbounded growth is an assumed functional form, not a derived result.** Romer states this openly: linearity of equation (3) in A is "convenient analytically" and chosen because there was, as of 1990, no evidence that research opportunities were diminishing — a judgment call the model cannot itself verify.
- **Fixed population and fixed human capital.** L and H are exogenous constants throughout; the model has nothing to say about how H is produced, why it might grow, or what determines its historical increase. Romer notes this leaves observed cross-country and historical variation only partially explained.
- **Ambiguity flagged by the author himself.** The independence of g from L and η is explicitly not robust to the functional forms chosen. Romer notes a companion paper ("Capital, Labor, and Productivity," cited as in press) shows an increase in L can instead *reduce* research effort under slightly different assumptions — "something that a priori theorizing cannot resolve."
- **No obsolescence or product replacement.** The additively separable, "no-neighboring-goods" structure of the final-goods production function rules out competition between old and new designs by construction — a substantive simplification relative to the Schumpeterian/creative-destruction alternative (Aghion & Howitt 1992, FA2 ledger entry 6).
- **Balanced-growth-path analysis only.** Transition dynamics starting from an arbitrary initial K/A ratio are explicitly not solved in this paper; only the constant-growth-rate equilibrium is characterized.
- **Scale effect not stress-tested against data.** The paper does not address whether the strong, unbounded scale effect it builds — more human capital in research mechanically implies permanently faster growth — survives contact with long-run data. That critique arrives five years later, in Jones (1995).
- **Stationary-population assumption is acknowledged as restrictive.** Romer notes explicitly that in a stationary population with finite lives, zero is the only feasible constant growth rate for total years of education/experience, so H must ultimately approach an upper bound in any more realistic dynamic extension.
- **Extreme factor-intensity assumptions in the research technology.** Only human capital and existing knowledge enter the production of new designs (equation 3); labor and physical capital play no role at all in research, a simplification Romer describes as "the plausible assertion that research is relatively human capital- and knowledge-intensive" pushed to an extreme functional form for tractability, not derived from data.
- **The empirical corroboration offered is indirect.** The Sokoloff evidence (Section VII) speaks to whether market size/integration moves patenting activity at all; it says nothing about whether the specific linear research technology of equation (3), or the specific closed-form growth rate of equation (13), matches observed magnitudes.
- **No treatment of uncertainty or indivisibility in research output.** Romer explicitly notes that treating the production of designs as a continuous, deterministic function (rather than a discrete, uncertain process) is a simplifying assumption for aggregate-level, first-pass analysis, while acknowledging that both factors matter "at the micro level and over short periods of time."
- **Product variety, not product quality.** Growth in this model takes the form of an ever-widening range of distinct producer durables, each supplied at a constant level x along the balanced growth path; there is no mechanism by which existing goods improve in quality over time, which is a different channel from the quality-ladder/creative-destruction growth engine developed shortly afterward (Aghion & Howitt 1992; also Grossman & Helpman's contemporaneous quality-ladder models, cited only in passing here).

Touches: the rivalry/excludability taxonomy and the nonconvexity/replication argument against price-taking equilibrium (Section II); the three-sector model — research (Ȧ = δH_A·A), monopolistically competitive intermediate/producer-durables production, and competitive final-goods production (Section III); the Solow/Uzawa intuition and Figure 1's balanced-growth diagram (Section IV); the balanced-growth solution and the closed-form growth-rate equation g = (δH − Λρ)/(Λσ + 1) and its independence from population/labor (Section V); the welfare comparison showing equilibrium research investment below the social optimum, and the policy contrast between subsidizing physical capital versus subsidizing research or human capital (Section VI); and the trade/integration result that pooling human capital across economies raises growth even without population growth (Section VII) — relevant to Sub-Q 4's question of what, if anything, substitutes for a large population in a market with no indigenous labor force.

The model's unbounded, human-capital-driven scale effect is the direct antecedent to the critique in Jones (1995) (FA2 ledger entry 10).

Its idea-space accumulation mechanic (equation 3) is also the direct conceptual link to the AK/reproducible-capital family (Rebelo 1991, FA2 ledger entry 3), making this paper the load-bearing hinge between Sub-Q 2 and Sub-Q 3 that the ledger's framing calls for.

For the FA2 model-by-model verdict table specifically, this entry contributes the row: constant 24-month doubling admitted in the model's reduced-form mathematics, with no in-model ceiling, but only by positing a δ·H_A product roughly one to two orders of magnitude above any historically observed research-productivity/human-capital combination — and the scale effect that makes this possible is the same feature the FA2 review must weigh against Jones's (1995) negative empirical finding before treating any endogenous-growth model as a candidate mechanism for the thesis's target growth rate.
