## Abstract

Formulates space logistics as a generalized multicommodity network flow (GMCNF) problem and solves it as a linear program. Locations are nodes, transport options are parallel arcs, and resource processing is a self-loop; commodities interact through three matrix multiplications that carry flow gain and loss, commodity transformation, and concurrency requirements. Demonstrated on a single round-trip human Mars mission whose logistical demand is taken from NASA Mars Design Reference Architecture 5.0, over a 16-node, 598-arc Earth-Moon-Mars network with 20 commodities, minimizing total launch mass to low Earth orbit. Reports a validation against DRA 5.0, a baseline optimum, eight variant cases on propulsion and in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) availability, and a sweep of ISRU productivity from 0 to 12 kg per year per kilogram of plant mass.

### Background and objective

The paper opens by classifying past human spaceflight logistics into two paradigms: the Apollo carry-along approach, in which all vehicles and resources travel with the crew, and the International Space Station resupply approach, which suits long-duration operations conducted near a resupply source (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 26). The stated research question is what the next-generation paradigm should be once multiple destinations and optional ISRU are in play, and the stated purpose is a graph-theoretic framework that can evaluate and optimize exploration logistics with optional ISRU from a network perspective rather than from an architecture perspective.

The literature review is where the paper positions its contribution, and it is organized around a single recurring complaint. Taylor et al. solved a concurrent vehicle-design and routing problem with combinatorial integer programming, which the present authors report was limited by computational complexity to a two-week lunar sortie over a time-expanded six-node Earth-moon network with only two commodity types, cargo and vehicles (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 26). SpaceNet is characterized as a discrete-event simulation that requires a predefined transportation network and mission sequence as inputs and can then determine feasibility and optimal manifesting for that given campaign. Object process networks are described as representing information flows rather than physical locations, with paths fixed per architecture. The EXAMINE framework is described as requiring the user to define the concept of operations and waypoints arbitrarily at the outset, with waypoint selection not optimizable within it. Arney et al.'s graph-theoretic framework is credited with expressing locations as nodes and transport modes as arcs and with allowing asset reuse over time, but is described as still requiring manual specification and as coupling architecture definition to transportation strategy, which forecloses some logistics strategies from the start (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 27).

The common gap the paper claims is that every one of these frameworks requires the user to predetermine the logistics network as an input. The stated objective follows directly: produce a method that selects the network itself, at a size and speed that suits long-duration campaigns, and that can therefore act as a front end providing network autogeneration to the existing tools rather than replacing them.

### Methods and scope

The formulation starts from the classical minimum-cost flow problem, in which a directed graph carries a supply or demand at each node, a unit cost on each arc, and arc flows as the decision variables, subject to mass balance at nodes and upper and lower bounds on arc flows (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, pp. 27-28). The paper then applies two standard generalizations and one of its own. Generalized flows attach a positive multiplier to each arc so that flow may be consumed or generated in transit rather than conserved. Multicommodity flows let several commodities share the network. The paper's own step is to replace the scalar multiplier with matrices, so that commodities may transform into one another and may impose requirements on one another, not merely share arc capacity.

The decision variables are the arc flows, and each arc carries two of them rather than one. Flow on arc (i, j) is split into an outflow vector leaving the tail node and an inflow vector arriving at the head node, each a k-dimensional vector over the k commodities, with its own unit-cost vector. The objective function is the sum over all arcs of the dot products of those two cost vectors with those two flow vectors (Eq. 3). Splitting the arc is what allows a transformation to be located on the arc itself, between the two endpoints.

Four constraint families act on those variables (Eqs. 4a to 4d). The flow equilibrium constraint sums outflows premultiplied by a matrix A, subtracts inflows premultiplied by a matrix A, and requires the result to respect the node's net supply or demand; A carries commodities that are consumed or generated at a node in order to dispatch or receive a flow without themselves travelling. The flow transformation constraint sets the inflow equal to a matrix B times the outflow, and this is where conservation is broken on purpose: burns, boiloff, crew consumption, waste generation, and ISRU production all live in B. The flow concurrency constraint requires the dot product of a matrix C with the flow to stay within a constant vector, and this is where one commodity compels another to travel alongside it, such as an aeroshell sized against the mass being braked, or structure sized against propellant. The fourth family is the ordinary lower and upper bound on each of the two flow vectors. For k commodities, A and B are k-by-k and C has one row per concurrency constraint on that arc; interaction between commodities appears exactly in the off-diagonal entries of A and B and the nonzero entries of C.

Two graph-theoretic devices carry the rest of the modeling load. A graph loop, an arc from a node to itself, represents a processing facility, so an ISRU plant is an arc rather than a node and inherits the same A, B, and C machinery. Parallel arcs between the same pair of nodes represent competing transport options, so propulsion choice, aerocapture versus propulsive capture, and delta-V against time-of-flight trades are all embedded as alternative arcs and are resolved by the optimizer rather than by the analyst. When several transformations occur on one arc, B is the left-multiplied product of the individual matrices, and the paper notes that because these matrices are generally noncommutative the multiplication order must match the physical sequence of events (Eq. 5).

Because every relation above is linear, the whole model is a linear program. The paper is explicit that this is the point: an LP solves quickly and, when it solves, returns a provable optimum, which permits the many runs that the integer-programming predecessor could not afford.

The case study instantiates this on a 16-node, 598-arc network including 20 self-loops (Fig. 5). The nodes are Kennedy Space Center, Pacific Ocean splashdown, low Earth orbit, geostationary transfer orbit, geostationary orbit, Earth-moon Lagrange points 1, 2, and 4/5, low lunar orbit, the lunar south pole, Deimos capture orbit, Deimos, Phobos capture orbit, Phobos, low Mars orbit, and Gale Crater; this expansion of the abbreviations is printed only inside Fig. 2 (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 27) and appears nowhere in the running text. Twenty commodities are carried (Table 1), all measured in kilograms of mass, in four groups: crew and returning crew; hydrogen, oxygen, water, methane, carbon dioxide, food, and waste; habitat, ISRU plant, and ISRU spares; and vehicle, three inert-mass classes, three tank classes, and aeroshell. Returning crew is a separate commodity from outbound crew, and a self-loop at Gale Crater transforms one into the other, which the paper describes as the device that forces a round trip in a flow formulation.

The objective is total launch mass to low Earth orbit, abbreviated TLMLEO, implemented by setting every arc's unit cost to zero except a unit cost of one on the inflow of the arc from Kennedy Space Center to low Earth orbit. The paper distinguishes this from initial mass in LEO on the grounds that IMLEO is assessed mission by mission, whereas TLMLEO is taken to include everything that must be launched to enable the resulting architecture, including predeployed ISRU systems and their spares. Demand is set as a habitat mass of 51,700 kg at Gale Crater and returning crew at the splashdown point, which the paper states is what makes it a round trip rather than a one-way mission; provisions and spares are not set explicitly because the three matrix multiplications demand them implicitly. Orbital and Lagrange nodes carry zero net supply and demand and act as transshipment points. Raw material at ISRU nodes is assumed unlimited, with production limited only by plant capacity, and only resource production is modeled, not surface manufacturing of spares or infrastructure.

One relation in the case study is genuinely derived and the rest are inputs. The propellant mass fraction is derived from the rocket equation as phi = 1 - exp(-dV / (Isp * g0)), and the burn is then written as a two-by-two B matrix acting on propellant and dry mass (Eqs. 7 and 8). Everything else that sets a number is an assumed input, and the paper collects the inputs in one table (Table 2):

- Specific impulse: 450 s for LOX/LH2, 369 s for LOX/LCH4, 900 s for nuclear thermal. Mixture ratios 5.88 for LOX/LH2 and 3.5 for LOX/LCH4.
- Inert mass fraction: 0.10 for both chemical systems and 0.30 for nuclear thermal in space; 0.30 for chemical descent stages; 0.24 for chemical ascent stages.
- Tank mass fraction: 0.18 for LH2, 0.02 for LOX, 0.04 for LCH4.
- Boiloff rate: 0.127 percent per day for LH2, 0.016 percent per day for LOX and for LCH4.
- Crew life support: consumption of 0.84 kg/day of oxygen, 2.90 kg/day of water, and 2.45 kg/day of food per person; output of 1.00 kg/day of carbon dioxide and 5.19 kg/day of waste. Oxygen leaks from vehicle or habitat at 0.0005 of pressurized volume per day, printed in the source as 0.05 percent.
- Aeroshell mass fraction: 0.37 of the total mass being braked, printed in the source as 37 percent.
- Mission data: crew of six at 100 kg each, one crew exploration vehicle at 10,000 kg dry, one Mars transfer habitat at 27,540 kg, one surface habitat at 51,700 kg.
- Arc delta-V and time of flight: imported from four cited references, assuming high-thrust propulsion, an Oberth maneuver at Earth flyby, and departure in appropriate launch windows. Mars transfers are fixed at a 180-day fast trajectory rather than optimized.

The two ISRU constants deserve separating from the rest because the paper's headline result is a sensitivity on one of them. Maintenance requirement and productivity are both assumed linearly proportional to plant mass, so a plant of mass m operated for a duration t requires alpha * t * m of spares and produces beta * t * m of resources, implemented as a three-by-three A and B pair on the self-loop (Eq. 6). The baseline values are beta = 10 kg per year per kilogram of plant mass and alpha = 0.10 of plant mass per year, and the assumed production period is 780 days. The linearity is an asserted convenience rather than a derived scaling law, and the paper says so in its own words: it cites two prior studies establishing that ISRU plants follow economies of scale, and then adopts linear scalability anyway in order to preserve the LP formulation (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 31). It repeats the concern when introducing the sensitivity sweep, noting that 10 kg/year/kg may be too optimistic, especially for smaller plants (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 36). No derivation of either constant from plant physics appears anywhere in the paper.

The solver setup is reported as MATLAB 8.3 (R2014a) with CPLEX 12.6 on an Intel Core i7-2640M at 2.80 GHz, taking about 12 s of preprocessing and 1.2 s of optimization per run (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 32).

### Key findings

The paper records for later reference that the nuclear thermal reference case is about 400 t, or 32 percent, lighter in TLMLEO than the chemical and aerocapture reference case.

The baseline problem then relaxes those constraints, and its constraint set is what the headline number depends on (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 32):

- Chemical and nuclear thermal propulsion may be combined, with nuclear thermal never permitted for descent or ascent.
- Aerocapture may be used for cargo flights, but crewed vehicles must perform propulsive orbit insertion at Mars arrival.
- Lunar ISRU at the lunar south pole and Mars ISRU at Gale Crater may produce oxygen from regolith or water from water ice, both at the assumed 10 kg/year/kg with spares at 0.10 of plant mass per year.
- Sabatier, solid oxide electrolysis, water electrolysis, and methane pyrolysis are available as optional self-loops.
- ISRU at Phobos and Deimos is excluded from the baseline as controversial.
- The mission is a single round trip, with a 780-day ISRU production period and a 540-day Mars surface stay.

Under that constraint set the optimized TLMLEO is 271.8 t, which the paper reports as 68.0 percent below the DRA 5.0 NTR reference and 78.3 percent below the DRA 5.0 chemical and aerocapture reference. The ISRU detail behind it (Table 4) is 60,415 kg of plant at the lunar south pole producing 1,291,056 kg of water over 780 days and requiring 12,911 kg of spares, against 2,360 kg of plant at Gale Crater producing 50,428 kg of water and requiring 504 kg of spares. Node traffic totals (Table 5) are dominated by the lunar surface, with outflow of 1376.3 t and inflow of 246.6 t at the lunar south pole, 998.1 t each way at low lunar orbit, and 878.4 t out and 878.5 t in at Earth-moon Lagrange point 2; the paper notes that the 595.1 t inflow at low Earth orbit contains the 271.8 t of TLMLEO within it, and that the traffic at Lagrange point 2, being the last stop before trans-Mars injection, makes it a natural depot site.

Three qualitative results accompany the baseline. All transfers use LOX/LH2 and nuclear thermal propulsion is not selected at all, which the paper attributes to water-ice ISRU producing about eight times more oxygen than hydrogen by mass, so that a hydrogen-only nuclear thermal stage strands the oxygen while a 5.88 mixture ratio consumes both in proportion (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 34). Lunar regolith and the Mars atmosphere go unused, with both ISRU sites drawing on water ice instead. Mars ascent runs entirely on Mars-produced oxygen and hydrogen, where DRA 5.0 had used Mars oxygen with methane brought from Earth.

The arc-level flow breakdown is printed only inside Fig. 8 (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 33) and is invisible to text extraction; it is recovered here because it carries the propellant-origin result at the level the rest of the paper only asserts. Sixteen arcs are in use and all sixteen are chemical.

- Propellant origin by arc: only the launch arc from Kennedy Space Center to low Earth orbit runs on Earth LOX with Earth LH2, and only the Mars ascent arc from Gale Crater to low Mars orbit runs on Mars LOX with Mars LH2. The arc from low Earth orbit to geostationary transfer orbit runs on lunar LOX with Earth LH2. The remaining thirteen arcs run on lunar LOX with lunar LH2.
- Total flow per arc ranges from 14.7 t on the entry arc to the splashdown point to 900.7 t on the lunar ascent arc from the lunar south pole to low lunar orbit, with 537.4 t from low lunar orbit to Lagrange point 2 and 341.1 t from geostationary transfer orbit to Lagrange point 2. The launch arc carries 271.8 t, which is the TLMLEO.
- Largest single commodity movements: 646.5 t of oxygen and 74.6 t of hydrogen lifted off the lunar surface, 463.5 t of oxygen and 43.4 t of hydrogen from low lunar orbit to Lagrange point 2, and 218.2 t of oxygen delivered from Lagrange point 2 down to low Earth orbit.
- Infrastructure routing: the 62.8 t of ISRU plant and 13.4 t of spares that leave Earth split into 60.4 t and 12.9 t routed to the lunar surface against 2.4 t and 0.5 t routed to Mars. The 51.7 t habitat is traced on five arcs from launch to the Mars surface.
- Aeroshell masses on individual arcs reach 133.6 t from geostationary transfer orbit to Lagrange point 2 and 122.0 t from low Earth orbit to geostationary transfer orbit.
- The rows for LOX/LCH4 inert mass, nuclear thermal inert mass, and LCH4 tankage are empty across all sixteen arcs, which is the arc-level confirmation that neither methane nor nuclear thermal propulsion is selected.

Four propulsion variants were solved against the baseline (Table 7), each reported as TLMLEO with the deployed plant masses at the two ISRU sites.

- Prohibiting LOX/LH2 raises TLMLEO by 56.5 percent to 425.5 t and collapses the lunar plant from 60,415 kg to 4,458 kg while raising the Mars plant to 3,754 kg.
- Prohibiting aerocapture for orbital transfer raises TLMLEO by 24.0 percent to 337.0 t and scales the Mars plant up by more than a factor of five, to 12,060 kg.
- A lightweight aeroshell at a mass fraction of 0.15 instead of 0.37, which is an input change rather than a result, lowers TLMLEO by 23.7 percent to 207.5 t and also scales the Mars plant up, to 11,719 kg, which the paper attributes to more plant being deliverable to Mars per unit of braked mass.
- Allowing the trans-Mars and trans-Earth injection stages to be reused rather than jettisoned lowers TLMLEO by only 5.2 percent to 257.7 t, on a lunar plant grown to 75,401 kg. The paper attaches the condition that this is measured over a single round trip and would be expected to matter more across recurring missions.

Four ISRU availability variants were solved (Table 8).

- Adding Phobos and Deimos lowers TLMLEO by 18.5 percent to 221.6 t and redistributes plant mass across all four sites at 25,676 kg, 7,047 kg, 8,994 kg, and 9,363 kg. The paper notes that some Deimos-produced resource is delivered back to geostationary transfer orbit and low Earth orbit, because in delta-V terms low Earth orbit is closer to Deimos than to the lunar surface.
- Removing lunar ISRU and keeping only Gale Crater raises TLMLEO by 49.0 percent to 404.9 t on a 14,012 kg Mars plant, which the paper offers as the measure of lunar ISRU's contribution to the baseline.
- Removing water ice so that only oxygen may be produced raises TLMLEO by 53.2 percent to 416.3 t for the two-site case and by 31.2 percent to 356.7 t for the four-site case. In both, nuclear thermal propulsion returns to partial use.
- In the four-site case without water ice the lunar plant drops to zero, which the paper reads as lunar oxygen being dominated by Phobos and Deimos oxygen at equal assumed productivity.

The productivity sensitivity is the paper's most-cited result and it is a sweep over one assumed input, beta, from 0 to 12 kg/year/kg with all else held at baseline (Fig. 11). Below 0.5 neither site is used and TLMLEO is flat at roughly 533 t on the figure's axis. Mars ISRU enters at 0.6 and lunar ISRU enters at 1.9; between 0.6 and 1.8 only Mars ISRU is used. The figure annotates its x-axis at 0.5, 1.8, and 3.5, which are the last rates at which each behavior still holds, while the running text and conclusions state the thresholds as 0.6 and 1.9, which are the first rates at which the behavior changes; the abstract's phrasing that lunar resources "become attractive at productivity levels above 1.8 kg/year/kg" is the same fact stated in the figure's convention. Above the lunar threshold, TLMLEO decreases monotonically with productivity while the optimized lunar plant mass moves up and down in a sawtooth whose overall trend is upward, reaching roughly 90,000 kg near 11.5 on the figure's right axis. The paper attributes the discontinuities to changes in network topology rather than to plant sizing, and gives a worked instance: at 3.5 the crew vehicle performs trans-Mars injection at geostationary transfer orbit using nuclear thermal propulsion, and at 3.6 and above it performs that burn at Lagrange point 2 using LOX/LH2 with lunar resources, after which lunar ISRU is strongly favored over Mars ISRU. Within each continuous segment, such as 8.6 to 10.6, higher productivity downsizes the plant while the transportation strategy is unchanged. The paper states that the reason nothing is produced below the thresholds is that a plant at 0.5 or below cannot produce even its own mass over the 780-day production period.

The stated conclusions are four.

- LOX/LH2 is much more compatible with ISRU water production than nuclear thermal propulsion.
- The use of aerocapture contributes significantly to reducing TLMLEO, which the paper says encourages development of lightweight aeroshell and thermal protection systems.
- Among the ISRU options examined, lunar water ice is the most valuable resource for Mars missions.
- The productivity thresholds at which ISRU benefit exceeds cost in launch mass are 1.9 kg/year/kg for lunar ISRU and 0.6 kg/year/kg for Mars ISRU.

The conclusions attach their own caveat that these results can change greatly with the parameters, assumptions, and choice of figure of merit, and that the sensitivity of the results to the assumptions may outweigh the impact of introducing ISRU at all.

### Limitations

The paper states most of its own limitations, and they are structural rather than incidental.

The objective function is single and is not a cost. TLMLEO is described as useful for estimating launch cost but as not representing design, development, test, and evaluation costs of components such as ISRU systems and in-space depots; the paper further notes that the increased number of rendezvous and refueling events is likely to contribute adversely to safety and reliability, and that the added complexity and risk of the logistics network are not accounted for. It states that the resulting strategy must therefore be evaluated from multiple perspectives or reformulated as a multi-objective problem (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 30), and the conclusions repeat that reliance on TLMLEO as an exclusive figure of merit may yield misleading conclusions (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 37).

The analysis is static, so there is no ordering of events. All flows occur simultaneously, with the consequence that the propellant used to deliver an ISRU plant to a surface comes from that same plant, which can only exist after the plant has been deployed and operated. The authors address this by interpretation rather than by reformulation, stating that TLMLEO should be read as the total launch mass for the nth mission of a campaign of recurring human Mars missions rather than the first, and that the comparison with DRA 5.0 remains valid because DRA 5.0's TLMLEO does not differ between the first and the nth mission (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 33). The 271.8 t therefore does not describe a first mission.

The model is deterministic and linear. All transports are assumed to occur with certainty and all demands are purely deterministic, with no representation of node or arc failure; the paper names stochastic uncertainty on arcs and loops as future work. Commodities that come in discrete sizes in reality are treated as continuously and linearly scalable throughout. The ISRU scaling assumption is the specific case that matters most to the headline result: the paper cites prior work establishing economies of scale for ISRU plants and then assumes linear scalability anyway to keep the linear programming formulation, and it separately warns that the baseline 10 kg/year/kg may be too optimistic for smaller plants. The threshold results of 1.9 and 0.6 are outputs of a model that carries that assumed scaling law.

Scope exclusions are stated. Solar electric propulsion is excluded because a low-thrust trajectory would require a different definition of arc parameters, despite being acknowledged as attractive for cargo. Only a single round-trip mission is modeled, not a campaign. Mars transfers are fixed at a 180-day fast trajectory although the paper notes the delta-V and time of flight are time-variant. Only resource production is modeled, not surface manufacturing of spares or infrastructure. Raw material is assumed unlimited at ISRU nodes. ISRU on Phobos and Deimos is described as controversial and its microgravity feasibility as a possible challenge, and the cases that include it proceed by assuming both resource availability and feasibility. The paper also notes that parameters it did not sweep, naming inert mass fraction, tank mass fraction, and environmental control and life support closure level, can have significant impact and can change the resulting strategy qualitatively (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 34).

Two limitations belong to this summary rather than to the source. First, four of the eleven figures carry content that no text extraction reaches. Figs. 6 and 8, the delta-V and time-of-flight matrices and the arc-by-arc commodity flow breakdown, are vector figures whose numerals do not appear in the text layer at all, confirmed by a normalized search that found zero instances of values such as 9.50 and 900.7 in extracted text after positive controls fired; they were read from page rasters rendered at 200 dpi. Figs. 2 and 11, the node legend and the productivity sweep, are embedded raster images and were read at their native resolutions of 1667 by 1167 and 1675 by 908. Any earlier text-only treatment of this source would have omitted all four silently. Second, one arithmetic reading is worth recording without adjudicating it: the paper's statement that a plant at 0.5 kg/year/kg or below cannot produce even its own mass holds when the 780-day production period is taken as the two years the paper rounds it to, and the same period taken as 2.14 years would yield about 1.07 times plant mass.

### Topic mapping

Sections I and II state the problem, the two historical logistics paradigms, the cislunar propellant infrastructure concept, and the review of prior space-logistics modeling frameworks against which the method is positioned (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, pp. 25-27).

Section III is the method proper: the minimum-cost flow baseline, the generalized and multicommodity extensions, the GMCNF objective function and its four constraint families, the roles of the flow equilibrium, flow transformation, and flow concurrency matrices, and the graph loop and multigraph devices that carry resource processing and transport-option selection (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, pp. 27-29).

Section IV.A and IV.B instantiate the case study: the 16-node and 598-arc network, the 20-commodity vector, the choice and construction of the TLMLEO objective, the demand definition that encodes a round trip, and the worked A, B, and C matrices for ISRU production, propulsive burn, and aeroshell concurrency. Table 1 lists the commodities, Table 2 lists every assumed parameter, and Fig. 6 carries the delta-V and time-of-flight matrices (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, pp. 29-31).

Section IV.C and IV.D carry the validation against DRA 5.0 in Table 3 and the baseline solution, with ISRU plant sizes and production in Table 4, node traffic in Table 5, an interpreted mission sequence in Table 6, a possible transportation scenario in Fig. 9, and the arc-by-arc commodity flows and propellant origins in Fig. 8 (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, pp. 31-34).

Section IV.E and IV.F carry the eight variant cases, four on propulsion and aeroshell assumptions in Table 7 and four on ISRU site availability and water-ice availability in Table 8, with the resulting network topologies in Fig. 10 (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, pp. 34-36).

Section IV.G carries the ISRU productivity sweep and the threshold values, plotted in Fig. 11 against both TLMLEO and deployed plant mass (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 36).

Section V states the conclusions, the caveat on parameter sensitivity, and the future work on risk, nonlinearity, and time evolution of network topology, including the intended role as a front end to SpaceNet (Ishimatsu et al., 2016, p. 37).
