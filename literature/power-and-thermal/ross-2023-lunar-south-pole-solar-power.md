## Abstract

Assessment of the maximum time-averaged solar power obtainable from the restricted high-illumination regions (HIRs) near the lunar South Pole, as a function of solar-panel or tower height and of the illumination fraction an application demands. Using average illumination maps for heights from 2 m to 20 m (near-term) and up to 2 km (long-term), the study finds that overshadowing of illuminated ground by sunward panels sharply limits the extractable power below what the illuminated area alone would suggest. For near-term vertical panels up to 20 m, the upper limit is about 55 to 63 MW at greater than 70 percent illumination, falling to about 6 MW at greater than 90 percent illumination, enough to power thermal mining of roughly 1600 to 2450 tonnes of water per year. For 2 km towers, up to about 22,000 MW at greater than 70 percent and about 15,500 MW at greater than 90 percent could be realizable.

---

### Background and objective

- The lunar South Pole is a focus for bases and for water mining from nearby PSRs (which stay below 110 K), with as much as about a billion tonnes of water suggested to be present. A key constraint on mining is available power, and only solar and nuclear fission are currently feasible options.
- Solar power near the pole is available nearly continuously only from vertically deployed panels in quite restricted HIRs close to the PSRs. Choosing between solar and nuclear hinges partly on how much solar power is actually obtainable; if solar is inadequate even in a best case, fission becomes the only option.
- The objective is a preliminary upper-bound estimate of the total solar power available from HIRs versus deployment height and required illumination fraction. The authors frame it as a maximum-power estimate, not an engineering design of towers or panels.

### Methods and scope

- Average illumination maps are computed for a range of heights above local topography: 2 m to 20 m for near-term deployment, and up to 2 km for long-term consideration.
- Total available power is evaluated as a function of time of lunar day and of the illumination percentage an application demands (for example greater than 70 percent versus greater than 90 percent).
- A central effect modeled is overshadowing: solar panels placed in sunward locations at a given time of day cast shadows on otherwise highly illuminated ground, so the aggregate extractable power is much smaller than the raw illuminated area implies.

### Key findings

- Near-term vertical panels (up to 20 m): the upper limit to time-averaged power is about 55 to 63 MW at greater than 70 percent illumination, reducing to about 6 MW at greater than 90 percent illumination.
- That near-term power is sufficient to meet thermal mining requirements for water production at a rate of about 1600 to 2450 tonnes per year.
- Long-term towers (up to 2 km): a maximum time-averaged power of order 22,000 MW at greater than 70 percent illumination could be realizable, and about 15,500 MW at greater than 90 percent illumination.
- The variation in generated power with lunar time of day ranges from a factor of about 1.1 to about 3.
- The stronger the illumination fraction demanded, the sharply smaller the available power, because the near-continuously-lit ground is very restricted and overshadowing compounds the loss.
- The authors conclude that sufficient solar power could be available for currently anticipated base or mining needs, but that securing it may require careful governance agreements over the scarce high-illumination sites.

### Limitations

- The study is explicitly preliminary and computes a maximum potential power, not a demonstrated or engineered supply; it does not address tower infrastructure or solar-panel technology.
- The 2 km tower scenario is a long-term, hypothetical case; the near-term result is bounded at 20 m panel height.
- Power estimates depend on the demanded illumination percentage and on the illumination maps and overshadowing model, and the scarce HIRs raise governance and allocation questions the paper flags but does not resolve.

### Topic mapping

- The introduction states the South Pole water-mining motivation (about a billion tonnes in PSRs), the solar-versus-fission framing, and the mining power estimates it draws on (Sowers and Dreyer: 1600 tonnes/year water needs 2 to 2.5 MW electrical plus 0.5 MW thermal; Kornuta: 2450 tonnes/year needs 0.8 MW thermal plus 2 MW electrical, totaling 2.8 MW, at 4 wt% ice worst case). The methods develop the height-dependent illumination maps and the overshadowing treatment. The results present the near-term (about 55 to 63 MW, down to about 6 MW at greater than 90 percent) and long-term (about 22,000 MW, about 15,500 MW at greater than 90 percent) power limits and the time-of-day variation factor of 1.1 to about 3, closing with the governance implication.
