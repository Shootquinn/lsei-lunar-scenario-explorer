## Abstract

Describes the conceptual and detail mechanical design of the Government Reference Design (GRD) demonstrator for NASA's Vertical Solar Array Technology (VSAT) program, a mast-elevated deployable and retractable solar array intended for the lunar south pole. Covers two design iterations of a three-axis gimbal, a telescoping wire-rope-driven mast, folding array housings with mock array panels, a deployable-leg base, and the demonstrator's control system, together with the incremental assembly and operational test sequence planned at the time of writing. The article reports no mass figures, no power-production analysis, and no test results beyond a gimbal functional check. Quantitative content is confined to geometry, requirement values, and drivetrain gear ratios.

---

### Background and objective

The VSAT program exists to elevate photovoltaic arrays above the terrain shadowing produced by boulders, hills, mountains, and crater walls at the lunar southern polar region, so that surface assets can be powered and recharged near-continuously (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1). The program awarded initial development contracts to three industry awardees to mature their own concepts to at least TRL-6, and Langley Research Center designed and built the GRD demonstrator in parallel with those contracts (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1). The GRD serves two stated purposes: to surface and mitigate mechanical deployment and stowing problems, and to inform reviewers ahead of the program's upcoming awardee down-selection (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1). It is therefore a government yardstick built alongside the competition, not a flight article and not one of the competing designs.

The originating concept came from VSAT program advisor Martin Mikulas, who proposed a deployable array system on a deployable mast under the earlier name Retractable Surface Array (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1). Two parameters defined that concept: a minimum power capability of 10 kW, and a minimum elevation of 10 m from the lunar surface to the bottom of the arrays. Those two parameters produced an estimated array size of 6 m by 6 m, split into two arrays flanking a central mast (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1). The same lineage runs through Pappa et al. 2021, which the present paper lists as its Reference 2 and which the reader should treat as the analytical companion to this mechanical design paper.

### Methods and scope

The paper proceeds as a design narrative through two iterations rather than as an analysis. Sections walk through the concept and requirements, a first flight-like preliminary design iteration covering the gimbal, mast, array and array housing, and base, then a second iteration that repackages the arrays and revises the mast and array drivetrains, and finally the control system and the planned test sequence. No structural, thermal, dynamic, or photovoltaic analysis is presented, and no analysis appendices exist. This is the principal structural difference from the sibling Pappa et al. 2021 source, which carries six analysis appendices and a mass and load summary.

Two scope decisions constrain everything the paper reports. First, the GRD was intended to be flight-like and to carry all the features needed to proceed to TRL-6 alongside the awardees, but cost constraints drove the use of commercial off-the-shelf items wherever possible (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 3). Second, and more consequentially for any quantitative use of this source, the team decided to focus on the deployment and stowage of the arrays and to not pursue actual solar panels or the surface interaction components (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 3). The arrays in the demonstrator are mock arrays. No photovoltaic cells, no power conditioning, no energy storage, and no surface interface hardware were designed or built.

Those decisions produced a truncated requirement set for the demonstrator. The lunar mission requirements listed on p. 3 are: 36 m2 of solar array area; 10 m from the lunar surface to the bottom of the arrays; the ability to be off-loaded from the landing vehicle; accommodation of a local slope of no more than 15 degrees at initial placement before deployment; autonomous mast verticality; autonomous deployment and retraction of the mast and arrays; transportability for relocation to other sites; and survivability during the lunar night. The truncated GRD requirements on the same page keep the 36 m2 area as mock array area, keep the 15 degree slope, keep autonomous plumbness and autonomous deployment and retraction, and replace the 10 m elevation with the minimal clearance the test cell ceiling permits.

### Key findings

Power figures in this source are design goals, not analyzed or measured outputs, and they carry no stated conditions. The 10 kW value appears three times: as a minimum power capability parameter of the Mikulas concept (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1), as a callout labeling the deployed array in Figure 1 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 1), and on p. 3 where the array sizes are said to have been determined by "the goal of reaching 10 kilowatts of power available to end users." That last phrasing is the only statement in the paper about where in the power chain the 10 kW is measured, and it points at the user end rather than the array terminals, but the paper does not define the boundary, does not identify what lies between the array and the end user, and does not quantify any loss. No statement is made anywhere about beginning-of-life versus end-of-life output, solar incidence angle, illumination fraction, array pointing or tracking performance, operating temperature, cell type, cell efficiency, or degradation. The paper contains no watts-per-square-metre figure and does not compute one.

The only pair of numbers from which an areal power density could be formed is the 10 kW goal and the 36 m2 array area requirement, both stated on pp. 1 and 3. The paper does not perform that division and attaches no conditions to either term, so the quotient would inherit the same unstated boundary as the 10 kW itself.

The paper reports no mass figures of any kind. There is no total system mass, no subsystem mass, no component mass, no areal mass density, no specific mass, and no specific power. This absence was verified against the full text of both the on-file version and the published proceedings version, and against every figure callout in both, including the exploded assembly views and the drivetrain views where component labels appear. Because no mass value exists, no inclusion boundary can be attached to one, and no watts-per-kilogram figure is derivable from this source at the array quantum, the system quantum, or any quantum in between.

Mass appears in the paper only as a qualitative design driver, in three places. The mast extrusions were to receive lightening features and wall thinning to minimise mass (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 6). The sheet metal array housings received lightening cutouts to reduce mass, with Mylar film covering the cutouts to keep regolith off the panels during landing and transport (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 7). The mock array panel material was changed from thin aluminium to corrugated plastic to reduce mass, with flexible tape replacing piano hinges and bent aluminium stiffeners retained only on the first and last panels of each stack (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 14). None of these carries a before or after number. Weight is also used functionally rather than numerically: the housing closing panels add weight to the array stack to assist deployment in lunar gravity (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 8), gravity rather than a driving mechanism is available to retract the mast (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 6), and the array panels descend under their own weight as the ropes pay out (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 8).

Stowed packaging is the one area where the paper produces original quantitative results, and they appear only as callouts on Figure 11 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 10), captioned VSAT Iterations, Stowed Dimensions. The first iteration, carrying two 3 m wide array packages on a six-segment mast, stows at 4.36 m tall by 1.90 m wide. The second iteration, carrying four 1.5 m wide array packages on a twelve-segment mast, stows at 2.90 m tall by 1.73 m wide, and a fourth view of that second iteration enclosed in a transparent rectangular envelope is labelled 5.72 m3. The figure prints only height and width for each iteration, so the envelope depth is not given and the 5.72 m3 value cannot be reconciled against the printed dimensions without it. No volume is printed for the first iteration. The second-iteration views in the figure show the stowed leg structure inside the envelope, but the text does not state what the 5.72 m3 includes and never mentions the number at all. The paper does not compute a volumetric packaging efficiency in kW per m3, and it makes no comparison to the 20 kW/m3 target that its own Reference 2 uses.

Geometry driving the stowed envelope is stated in the text. An array net width of 6 m implies two 3 m wide arrays whose housings must be slightly larger than 3 m, and those housing sizes set the retracted mast height (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 4). Mast segments were consequently limited to roughly 3 m, and a net deployed height of 16 m, composed of 6 m of array height above 10 m of surface clearance, required six segments (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 6). The second iteration halved the array width to four 1.5 m arrays, which reduced the stowed housing height and in turn required the mast to go from six segments to twelve (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 10). The added housings increased stowed width, but the net width stayed inside the base footprint, which the authors report as the better package volumetrically (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 10).

Array panel geometry is given as 3 m wide by 0.75 m high, with 8 panels per package, arranged in a folding pattern with links at top and bottom attaching to the housing and to the housing closing panel (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 7).

The mast design borrows from terrestrial telescoping light-tower masts rather than from spacecraft booms, because it must resist buckling under gravity and must retract, which few spaceborne booms do (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 6). A winch on the lowest segment pulls the second segment up, and each subsequent segment is attached to the previous one by wire rope over a pulley at the segment top, bootstrapping all segments simultaneously (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 6). The mast drive is a worm and worm gear driven by a gearmotor turning a wire rope drum (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 6). Bespoke nesting box-section aluminium extrusions were modelled first, but proved excessively expensive for a concept demonstrator, so the design switched to formed sheet metal components riveted into segments, with sealed bearings at the segment corners, eight bearings per frame, and corner doublers reinforcing the thin walls (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 11). Wire ropes, drums, pulleys, and terminations follow MIL-DTL-83420P, which sets safety factors, minimum radii, and breaking strengths; helical drum grooves and single-wrap drum sizing removed the need for an active rope-guiding fairlead (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 11).

The three-axis gimbal exists to make the mast plumb on terrain sloping up to 15 degrees, and its design prioritised control authority and stability over compactness given the demonstrator's scale (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 4). X and Y motion come from commercial ball-screw linear actuators with worm drives and recirculating ball nuts driven by DC brushed motors, selected as larger versions of self-braking actuators originally sized for a conceptual lunar crane (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 4). Z rotation uses a central high-strength steel axle, two tapered roller bearings in a spool-shaped hub, and upper and lower plates carrying the mast, driven by a right-angle worm drive with a commercial gearmotor whose helical pinion turns a ring gear (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 4). A slip ring below the rotator, with concentric copper traces on the rotor meeting spring-loaded pins on the stator, permits continuous rotation and replaces the alternative of a service loop or an omega-shaped flat cable that would force the mast to rotate through plus and minus 180 degrees (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 4). Figures 3 and 4 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 5) also label an azimuth angle encoder and an IMU mount and housing.

The second-iteration array housing drivetrain resolves a problem the four-package layout created. The inboard housings could still be deployed by a single rope routed through the mast core, but the outboard housings needed a separate method, so a common drivetrain was built with one drum for the inboard housing and two for the outboard (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 13). A right-angle worm drive powered by a commercial gearmotor carries the outboard drums directly and drives the inboard housing through a 5:1 planetary gear set; that reduction combined with the two drum diameters yields the required 2:1 folding ratio, because the inboard housing must fold up 90 degrees while the outboard housing folds down 180 degrees relative to it (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 13). Two kickers were added to the outboard housing to prevent a zero-angle rope crossing at the inboard-to-outboard hinge line, with kicker heights set so that rope tension stays within the rope's working load (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 13). Figure 15 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 14) labels the array housing drive as a 24 VDC gearmotor with a 30:1 worm and wormgear. Figure 17 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 15) labels the array panel drive as an 80:1 right-angle drive with a commercial 130:1 24 VDC gearmotor and an adjustable clutch.

Array panel deployment moved to an individual driveline per package in the second iteration, using two nylon strap reels on a common driveshaft turned by a right-angle gearbox and gearmotor, with an adjustable clutch between drive and driveshaft to prevent stalling on retraction and adjustable anchors on the bottom housing panels for strap length (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 14). A behaviour observed against the CAD expectation is recorded: the panels do not unfold simultaneously, but stay folded near the bottom of the stack while the uppermost panels unfold first, with the last panels reaching position only once the ropes are fully paid out; the ropes are woven so the panels cannot fold the wrong way during retraction (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 8).

The base was designed but not built. It consists of a triangular platform with three leg mounting points and the gimbal at the centre, the legs retracting upward to stow parallel to the mast, driven in common by a single motor turning a triangular pivot with connecting rods to each leg (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 9). Launch constraint hardware comprises a central ball-ended pin captured by an over-centre hook latch plus three cup-cones, an interface the authors note could be reused on a transport vehicle for relocation (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 9). Project management decided not to build a demonstrator of the base and leg design, so the second iteration dropped it and redirected effort to the mast and array packages (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 10).

The control system uses a SpeedGoat target machine as the main controller, converting user and sensor input into analogue control signals that a motor driver turns into 24 volt PWM signals (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 16). Gimbal control reads pitch and roll from an IMU into a PID loop; mast, housing, and panel deployment stops are determined from potentiometers and limit switches placed through the structure; and the operator works through a GUI on a host machine linked to the SpeedGoat over Ethernet, with manual per-motor control available (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 16).

Design maturity at the time of writing is stated plainly in the Conclusion (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 17) and should be read as the paper's own account of what has and has not been demonstrated. Demonstrated: the GRD is undergoing assembly in a test cell in building B1293B at Langley, all major components are complete or near complete with revisions continuing as assembly proceeds, control system wiring is partially complete, gimbal testing has been conducted successfully using a dummy mast with deadweight at the top to simulate the stowed-condition inertial properties, and the completed mast has been installed. In progress: the off-loader system is being installed. Planned but not yet performed: IMU zeroing for plumbness, gimbal operation with the mast stowed, mast deployment and retraction, inboard array housing installation and deployment with the mast stowed, mast deployment with inboard arrays stowed, inboard array and inboard array panel deployment and stowing, outboard array installation and deployment with the mast stowed, mast deployment together with array housing deployment and stowing, and outboard array panel deployment. The only completed functional test reported anywhere in the paper is therefore the gimbal check against a dummy mast.

The demonstrator is a one-gravity, ambient, indoor mechanism testbed. The paper reports no thermal-vacuum testing, no regolith or dust testing, no reduced-gravity offload, no lunar-night survival testing, no launch load testing, and no photovoltaic performance testing, and it does not claim any.

- Any PMAD mass, topology, voltage, or efficiency. The only electrical hardware described is the demonstrator's own motor and control wiring, its slip ring, and a flat ribbon cable self-retracting electrical reel labelled on Figure 12 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 11).

- Any structural or thermal analysis, load, stress, deflection, buckling margin, or temperature.
- Any beginning-of-life or end-of-life power distinction, illumination fraction, incidence angle, tracking accuracy, or degradation rate.

### Limitations

The paper is a design description of an in-progress ground demonstrator, written before the demonstrator had been assembled or functionally tested beyond its gimbal. Its own account of maturity is explicit on this point (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 17).

The demonstrator is not representative of flight hardware in construction or materials. Cost constraints drove commercial off-the-shelf selection wherever possible (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 3), bespoke aluminium extrusions were abandoned for riveted sheet metal on cost grounds (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 11), the mock array panels are corrugated plastic with tape hinges (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 14), and the mechanisms use steel axles, tapered roller bearings, ball-screw actuators, and DC brushed gearmotors chosen for a terrestrial testbed.

The built article differs from the designs the paper describes. The mast built for the GRD has seven segments rather than the twelve of the second-iteration design, shortened by test chamber constraints, and Figure 12 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 11) labels it as approximately 10 m deployed against the 16 m of the design (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 11). The base and leg system was designed but never built (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 10).

The stowed dimensions and the 5.72 m3 envelope exist only as figure callouts on Figure 11 (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 10). They are not repeated, defined, or discussed in the text, their inclusion boundary is not stated, and the envelope's third dimension is not printed.

Lunar night survivability appears in the requirement list (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 3) but no survival strategy, thermal design, heater provision, or battery is described anywhere in the paper.

Relocation and transportability appear in the requirement list (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 3) but the only hardware described for them is the lifting point on the array support assembly (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 7) and the reuse of the launch constraint cup-cones and ball-end pin on a transport vehicle (Belbin et al., 2024, p. 9). No transport vehicle, mass, or operation is defined.

The two published versions of this paper differ in pagination and, from Figure 13 onward, in figure numbering. A locator taken from one version will be wrong against the other.
