## Abstract

Presents a conceptual design for a 10 kW deployable, retractable, free-standing solar array for lunar South Pole surface power, called the Relocatable Solar Array (RSA). The concept is a pair of solar cell blankets hanging freely from a horizontal cross arm on a vertical telescoping mast, set on a deployable tripod base. Lunar gravity itself is used to deploy and hold the blankets extended, and the vacuum, low-gravity environment removes any deployed-vibration-frequency requirement, permitting very slender, low-mass structural members. The design was developed in part as a NASA reference concept against which other proposed lunar arrays can be compared. The paper reports preliminary sizing, structural and thermal analysis, and a deployment sequence, targeting a volumetric packaging efficiency of 20 kW/m3.

---

### Background and objective

The array is intended for the lunar South Pole, where the Sun stays within a few degrees of the horizon (p. 1, citing Reference 1), requiring vertical, free-standing arrays deployed in 1/6 g. Major requirements stated in the Introduction: the array must be capable of retraction and relocation to an alternate site, must keep the solar cell blanket elevated 10 m above the surface to avoid terrain-shadowing, must rotate to track the Sun over the 708-hour lunar day, and must be mechanically as simple as possible given the retraction requirement (p. 1). The paper's stated main objective is to show that a plausible 10 kW array design exists at a 20 kW/m3 volumetric packaging efficiency, and to provide dimensional and force requirements for future hardware (joints, latches, actuators, slender structural members) (p. 2). The design parameters are described as "intentionally optimistic" to establish a lower limit on achievable packaging volume and array mass; no requirement is imposed that the array be fully operational on Earth without gravity offloading (p. 2). The work was funded by the Vertical Solar Array Technology (VSAT) project under NASA's Game Changing Development Program in the Space Technology Mission Directorate (p. 6). The initial RSA concept is credited to co-author Martin Mikulas (p. 6).

### Methods and scope

The paper performs preliminary sizing and closed-form structural and thermal analysis rather than hardware testing. Section II sets out design parameters and assumptions (Table 1); Section III describes a six-step deployment/retraction sequence (Figures 2 and 3); Section IV gives a preliminary design overview including a mass and load summary (Figure 4) and stowed-package dimensions (Figure 5); Section V covers the tripod deployment mechanism concept (nested two-tube legs with linear actuators); Section VI describes the packaged configuration; Section VII discusses volumetric design margins. Six appendices carry the supporting analysis: Appendix A (mast bending/buckling under gravity load), Appendix B (thermal distortion of the mast from solar heating), Appendix C (gravitational loads in the tripod legs), Appendix D (tripod sizing against a tipping requirement), Appendix E (options for a shorter stowed package length), and Appendix F (a Z-folded wiring harness concept for the power extension cord). The array is analyzed as an idealized constant-diameter cantilever tube; the authors state that detailed joint, latch, and actuator design, along with detailed packaging/mass/complexity trade-offs, are beyond the paper's scope.

### Key findings

Design targets and derived array properties (Table 1, printed p. 13): volumetric packaging efficiency PPV = 20 kW/m3 for the complete array including the integrated tripod; solar cell blanket power per unit area Pi = 300 W/m2; blanket thickness t_b = 1.3 mm (0.05 in); resultant blanket volume fraction (PPV times t_b divided by Pi) = 0.087, or 8.7 percent; blanket areal mass density = 1 kg/m2; resultant blanket-only specific power = Pi divided by areal density = 300 W/kg. This 20 kW/m3 target is compared against a 60 kW/m3 goal used in an earlier reference (Reference 2), with the lower figure here attributed to three factors: inclusion of a deployable tripod stand, slightly thicker blanket panels to account for Z-fold stiffening, and the support mast occupying a larger volume fraction at this smaller array's scale (p. 3). The complete array must stow within 0.5 m3 (p. 3).

Mass and load breakdown (Figure 4, printed p. 16; also stated in text on p. 4 and in Appendix D on p. 10-11): total array mass approximately 120 kg. Upper mass (blankets plus cross-arm/deployment hardware) approximately 50 kg, composed of 36 kg for the two solar cell blankets totaling 36 m2 (18 kg per 3 m by 6 m blanket) plus a 14 kg allowance for the upper and lower cross-arm support members and winch-and-cable blanket deployment system; this 50 kg produces an 80 N (18 lb) downward tip load on the mast in 1/6 g. Mast mass approximately 36 kg. Tripod mass approximately 34 kg, producing 66 N (15 lb) load at each of the three tripod leg tips. Approximate center of gravity of the assembled array is 9.5 m above the base (p. 10), noted as inexact because the tripod mass location is only approximate. At the array's rated 10 kW output, the roughly 120 kg total mass corresponds to about 83 W/kg for the complete system, distinct from the 300 W/kg blanket-only specific power in Table 1; the source presents these as separate figures for the blanket alone versus the assembled array (Table 1 and Figure 4, printed pp. 13 and 16).

Stowed package (Figure 5, printed p. 16; Appendix E, p. 11): the packaged array envelope is 0.3 m by 0.4 m by 4 m, fitting inside a 5 m diameter launch shroud and occupying less than 1 percent of the shroud's volume, so that numerous arrays could share a single launch. The 4 m stowed length is set by the 3 m long blanket canisters plus the 4 m tripod legs. Shortening the tripod legs by telescoping could cut stowed length to 3 m (a 25 percent reduction) at the cost of added mechanical complexity; alternatively, adding a second fold to each blanket (a four-blanket variant, Figure E1) could shrink the canister to 1.5 m but would require twice as many telescoping mast segments, a larger mast diameter, and more complex tripod deployment to still reach the needed 6 m footprint.

Structural member sizing and slenderness (pp. 4-5): cross-arm support members are 2.5 cm (1 in) thick, giving a slenderness ratio of approximately 120; the mast diameter is 0.15 m (6 in) over a 16 m height, giving a slenderness ratio of approximately 107; tripod legs are 4 m long by 3 cm (1.2 in) diameter, giving an aspect ratio of 130. These ratios are stated as at least twice those normally accepted in terrestrial civil engineering practice, justified by the low lunar gravitational loading; the authors note testing will be required to confirm adequacy.

Mast structural analysis (Appendix A, pp. 7-8): mast modeled as a composite tube, elastic modulus 130 GPa (19 msi), average wall thickness 2 mm (0.08 in), density 1600 kg/m3, minimum diameter set at 0.15 m (6 in) by telescoping/actuator packaging needs (a 0.1 m/4 in mast would otherwise suffice structurally). Euler buckling load for the 16 m mast under axial compression: 3564 N, against an applied compressive tip load of 80 N. At an assumed 15 degree mast tilt, the lateral load component is 20.7 N, producing a root bending stress of 1284 psi (about 9 MPa), stated as well below an assumed composite allowable exceeding 50,000 psi (about 345 MPa), and a lateral tip deflection of 7.6 cm (3 in), about half the mast diameter. The bare mast tube mass alone is calculated at 24.9 kg.

Thermal analysis of the mast (Appendix B, pp. 8-10; Table B1 and B2, Figure B1 and B2, printed pp. 19-20): assumed graphite-epoxy composite properties (Table B1) are emissivity 0.84, absorptivity 0.92, density 1576 kg/m3, specific heat 1044 J/kg-K, conductivity 1.73 W/m-K, with solar constant taken as 1367 W/m2. The circumferential temperature gradient across the mast diameter is stated at approximately 210 C (p. 2) and, for low-conductivity material, can exceed 220 K (400 F) (p. 9-10). Table B2 gives hot-side/cold-side steady-state temperatures by mast diameter: 0.20 m gives 430.8 K (315.8 F) sun-facing versus 207.2 K (-86.7 F) shaded; 0.15 m gives 425.9 K (306.9 F) versus 212.1 K (-77.9 F); 0.10 m gives 413.8 K (285.2 F) versus 224.2 K (-56.1 F). A target coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of approximately plus/minus 0.18 x 10^-6/C (0.1 x 10^-6/F) is assumed achievable for the mast composite; Reference 3 is cited as stating that a CTE below plus/minus 0.09 x 10^-6/C (0.05 x 10^-6/F) is not practical for laminate designs. At this CTE goal, the resulting thermal tip deflection for a 0.15 m diameter mast is approximately 2.5 cm. Figure B2 plots tip deflection (0 to 0.20 m) against CTE (0 to 1.0 x 10^-6 m/m-C) for three mast diameters: the 0.1 m line is steepest, reaching 0.20 m deflection near CTE 0.7 x 10^-6; the 0.15 m line reaches about 0.16 m deflection at the plotted M55J/934 lamina CTE of about 0.9 x 10^-6; the 0.2 m line reaches about 0.105 m at that same CTE and about 0.12 m at CTE 1.0 x 10^-6. The figure marks a "CTE goal" reference near 0.17 x 10^-6 alongside the M55J/934 lamina value near 0.9 x 10^-6.

Tripod leg loads (Appendix C, p. 10; Figure C2, printed p. 21): each leg tip carries a gravity-induced load of 66 N (15 lb). For a 3 cm (1.2 in) diameter, 4 m long leg of 240 GPa (35 Msi) composite, the Euler buckling load exceeds 4400 N (1000 lb). The parametric loads in Figure C2, plotted against actuator attachment position a (0 to 1.0 m) for 3 m and 4 m leg lengths in both compression ("Column") and tension ("Actuator or cable") configurations, remain below 900 N (200 lb) across the geometry range; at the design point a = 0.5 m the plotted loads are approximately 570 N (130 lb) for the upper curve pair and approximately 420 N (95 lb) for the lower pair, both well under the approximately 3600 N (870 lb) reference buckling capacity noted in the figure's inset.

Tipping stability and tripod sizing (Appendix D, p. 10-11): with total array mass 120 kg and CG approximately 9.5 m above the base, and a design requirement that the array not tip on a 15 degree slope, the metastable tipping geometry requires tripod legs to extend at least approximately 3 m from the center; the design uses tripod legs of almost 4 m to give a 6 m diameter footprint. The distance from the tripod center to the tipping point is noted to vary by as much as a factor of two depending on the tripod's azimuth orientation relative to the local slope, which the authors suggest should inform placement procedures. Alternatives mentioned but not developed further: adding base mass (e.g., batteries) to lower the CG, or anchoring the base to the terrain.

Deployment sequence (Section III, Figures 2 and 3, printed pp. 14-15): six steps: (1) the packaged RSA is unloaded from the lander, assumed by crane, and transported to the operational site by rover; (2) tripod legs deploy via linear actuators, tolerating up to a 15 degree terrain slope and correcting for regolith settling; (3) the mast telescopes to its vertical position; (4) the stowed array canisters rotate to horizontal via cable or gear-driven actuator; (5) the Z-folded blankets deploy under lunar gravity via extendable cords, requiring no blanket-stiffening springs as would be needed in zero-g; (6) the array reaches the fully deployed configuration, with panels intentionally left slightly under-extended to aid correct retraction, or alternatively using hinge-line torsional springs to enforce folding.

Wiring harness concept (Appendix F, p. 12; Figures F1-F4, printed pp. 24-26): elevating the 10 kW array 10 m above the surface requires an approximately 8 m power extension harness from the blanket down to the surface. The concept follows the Z-folded flat harness design used on the ISS solar array (Reference 6, Figures F1/F2). The harness is sized to stow within a U-shaped lower cross-arm channel 5 cm (2 in) deep by 30 cm (12 in) wide, assumed 15 cm (6 in) wide and under 1 mm (0.04 in) thick, with 0.5 m (20 in) Z-folds requiring 10 stowed layers and a packaged thickness of about 1 cm (0.4 in), fitting without a separate volume penalty. After subtracting the roughly 2 m tripod base height and 3 m stowed mast/canister length, about 5 m (200 in) of harness remains to deploy, consistent with Figure F4's dimension stack of 6 m (blankets) plus 5 m (harness) plus 3 m plus 2 m totaling the 16 m deployed height. Unheated harness temperature in shade is noted as potentially as low as 40 K (-380 F). The rotary-joint power transfer (slip rings versus periodic 180 degree rewinding) is noted as unaddressed beyond the red rotary joint shown in the figures.

Design conclusions (Section VIII, p. 5-6): three novel features are credited with achieving the mass and volume targets: freely hanging, unstiffened blankets simplifying structure and mechanisms; structural members sized for the 1/6 g environment, yielding slenderness ratios over twice terrestrial civil-engineering norms; and a tripod base of three deployable truss legs with nested linear actuators for compact packaging. The analysis is reported to show large margins against gravity-induced stress and buckling, with gravity- and thermal-induced mast tip deflections each a fraction of the mast diameter.

Volumetric margin discussion (Section VII, p. 6): as configured, little margin remains within the 20 kW/m3 goal for launch support hardware, deployment/latching mechanisms, power harnesses, and other unanticipated needs. Two paths to recover margin are identified: reducing cross-arm thickness or mast diameter, or using an external robotic tool to sequentially extend mast segments, eliminating a dedicated internal deployer and potentially reducing both mast diameter and mass.

### Limitations

The paper is explicitly a preliminary conceptual design study, not a built or tested hardware program; detailed design, development, and verification of the deployment mechanisms are stated as required before the array could meet all operating requirements, including launch loads and thermal extremes (p. 6). Design parameters are described as "intentionally optimistic" to explore a lower bound on mass and volume rather than a flight-qualified baseline (p. 2). Detailed design of the tripod deployment actuators (e.g., screw jack versus winch-driven cable) and the telescoping mast actuation mechanism are stated as beyond the paper's scope (p. 3-4). Tension-stiffening cables, which could improve mass and packaging metrics, were not considered in this study (p. 2). The CG location used in the tipping analysis is approximate because the tripod mass distribution is only assumed (p. 10). Detailed trade-offs among packaging volume, mass, and deployment complexity for the reduced-length package variant (Appendix E) are stated as beyond the current study's scope (p. 11). The choice between slip rings and periodic array rewinding for the rotary power joint is not addressed (p. 12). No requirement is imposed that the array be fully operational under Earth gravity without weight offloading, which the authors note would otherwise impose a large mass and volume penalty (p. 2).
