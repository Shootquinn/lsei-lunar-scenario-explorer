## Abstract

Parameter study of direct-current electrical power transmission lines for a lunar surface base, developed from a literature survey and lunar power-system requirements overview conducted at Auburn University's Space Power Institute. Models line radius, total line mass, conductor material temperature, conductor resistance, specific mass (mass per unit length), and percent power loss, each as a function of operating voltage, across an iterative electrical-thermal calculation. The study varies transmission line application (power and length), geometry (two-wire parallel or coaxial), deployment location (above or below the lunar surface), conductor material (aluminum or copper), operating voltage (1 to 10 kV), efficiency (chiefly 95%, with some cases at 99% and 97%), and burial depth (3, 25, or 50 cm), across 28 defined cases. Power conversion equipment is identified as necessary to the overall system but its mass and volume effects are explicitly excluded from this report. An AC transmission companion study is stated as underway at the time of writing.

### Background and objective

The report's own abstract (Report Documentation Page, p. 188 of the PDF) frames it as one deliverable from a broader Auburn University grant studying electrical and thermal energy management for lunar facilities. The stated work sequence was: June to November 1989, literature survey and assessment of lunar power needs and system requirements; November 1989 to April 1990, development of DC transmission line models; March to May 1990, development of AC transmission line models. This document is the DC model-development and parameter-study deliverable, bound separately from the literature survey, the lunar power-system requirements overview, and the AC model, "because of the large amount of information in the model development and application to a wide parameter space" (Report Documentation Page).

The document's table of contents (p. v of the PDF, printed p. v) sets out nine sections: Introduction; The Lunar Environment (vacuum, radiation, micrometeorites, thermal, gravity, soil); Power Transmission on the Lunar Surface; DC Transmission Overview; Factors Affecting Transmission Line Design (material selection, conductor material, dielectric material, primary transmission line parameters, power conversion, deployment geometry); Analysis Techniques (four line-configuration derivations); Parameter Studies; Summary and Conclusions; and Recommendations and Further Work. Sections I, II (prose), III, IV, and VI were not read for this summary (see Limitations); their scope is inferred from the table of contents, the list of figures and tables, and the parameter-study introduction's cross-references into them.

The parameter study's introductory text (p. 41 of the printed body) states that the five candidate transmission lines came from an "initial lunar base design discussed in section III," identified as: 100 kW over 200 m, 100 kW over 5 km, 120 kW over 5 km, 200 kW over 1 km, and 800 kW over 1 km. The report notes the 100 kW/5 km and 120 kW/5 km lines "are very similar and give almost identical results," reducing the effective study to four classes: a relatively low power, short line (100 kW, 200 m); a low power, long line (100 kW, 5 km); a medium power, medium length line (200 kW, 1 km); and a high power, medium length line (800 kW, 1 km).

### Methods and scope

For a fixed power level and efficiency, the model sets voltage, derives the required current, derives the conductor resistance needed to hold the specified power loss, and derives the conductor radius that gives that resistance. A thermal model (Section VI, not read in detail for this summary) then predicts heat flow and material temperature from the line geometry and resistive heating; since conductivity depends on temperature, the resistance calculation is redone and the process iterated until temperature and resistance converge (p. 43 of the printed body). Results are reported as five plots against voltage for each case (six for two-geometry or two-material comparison cases): line radius, total line mass, line temperature, conductor resistance, specific mass, and, for several comparison cases, percent power loss. Voltage is swept from 1 to 10 kV in 1 kV increments, giving ten piecewise-connected data points per curve; the report notes that "occasionally, when a parameter is changing rapidly the curve is not very smooth" (p. 43).

Two line geometries are modeled: a two-wire parallel line, insulated by vacuum (or by the lunar soil itself when buried), and a coaxial line, insulated by a solid dielectric (TFE polytetrafluoroethylene in all coaxial cases). For the two-wire line, radius, temperature, and resistance are computed per wire, while total mass and specific mass cover both wires; for the coaxial line, resistance is computed for one conductor while radius, temperature, total mass, and specific mass cover both conductors plus the dielectric, with the inner conductor radius set equal to that of one wire of an equivalent two-wire line under the same conditions (p. 43). Two deployment locations were modeled for each geometry: above the lunar surface, analogous to a terrestrial overhead line, and below the surface, analogous to a buried terrestrial line. Two conductor materials, aluminum and copper, are compared in a subset of cases. Most cases use daytime lunar surface conditions as the more thermally stressful case; a subset uses nighttime conditions for comparison. Most cases fix the transmission line efficiency at 95% (5% power loss); a subset varies efficiency across 1%, 3%, and 5% power loss (99%, 97%, and 95% efficient). Most below-ground cases use a 25 cm burial depth; a subset varies depth across 3, 25, and 50 cm.

The following material constants, stated as fixed inputs to every case, are given on p. 43 of the printed body: initial (295 K) resistivity of aluminum, 2.83 x 10^-8 ohm-m; initial resistivity of copper, 1.77 x 10^-8 ohm-m; density of aluminum, 2700 kg/m^3; density of copper, 8920 kg/m^3; density of TFE, 2175 kg/m^3; view factor for the above-ground two-wire line to space, 0.89; conductor emissivity, 0.9; daytime lunar surface temperature, 380 K; nighttime lunar surface temperature, 100 K; soil temperature below about 30 cm, 250 K; thermal conductivity of lunar soil, 0.008 W/m-K; thermal conductivity of aluminum, 237 W/m-K; thermal conductivity of copper, 401 W/m-K; thermal conductivity of TFE, 0.251 W/m-K; and dielectric breakdown strength of TFE, 430 V/mil.

Table 10 ("Parameters for Case Studies," p. 42 of the printed body) is a matrix defining all 28 cases against these parameters, coded by fixed value (single condition) versus swept value (family of curves on one plot), together with which of six plot types (r vs V, M vs V, T vs V, R vs V, specific mass vs V, %PL vs V) each case includes. Each case's individual plot page also states its own transmission line parameters and a schematic of the line cross-section (wire pair above ground, wire pair buried, coaxial above ground, or coaxial buried, with burial depth labeled where applicable), plus a short numbered list of the report author's own comments on that plot.

Two patterns recur across these readings. First, aluminum's specific mass is consistently lower than copper's at every voltage in every case checked, by roughly a factor of two, despite aluminum's higher resistivity, because aluminum's lower density (2700 versus 8920 kg/m^3) dominates the mass calculation (Case 1 comments, p. 44-48; Summary and Conclusions, "Conductor Material," p. 166). Second, the coaxial geometry's specific mass does not keep decreasing with voltage the way the two-wire geometry's does. Both geometries fall together at low voltage, but the coaxial curve flattens and, in the below-ground cases, turns upward again at high voltage (Cases 5, 9, 10, 22, 28), because the solid dielectric's mass, which scales with voltage through the breakdown-strength-driven wall thickness, comes to dominate over the conductor mass as the conductor shrinks. The report states this directly: "there is an optimum voltage at which the radius and mass are minimized" for coaxial lines, and higher voltages provide "lower volume, lower mass, and better line flexibility except for solid dielectric insulated lines in which the dielectric mass and volume may dominate over the conductor mass and volume at high voltages" (Summary and Conclusions, "Voltage Level," p. 166).

Burial depth, checked directly in Cases 27 and 28, has little effect on specific mass for either geometry across the tested 3 to 50 cm range; the three depth curves in each case nearly overlay each other. The Case 27 comment states the small remaining difference is attributable to conductor material's effect on conductivity rather than to depth itself. The Case 28 comment states the same for the coaxial case: "the small variation of temperatures of the coaxial lines at the three depths will not be enough to significantly affect the radius and mass of the line design."

Power loss level (1%, 3%, 5%), checked directly in Case 22, has more effect on specific mass at low voltage than at high voltage: at 1 kV the 1%-loss (99% efficient) line is roughly 60% heavier than the 5%-loss line, but by 10 kV the three loss levels converge to within about 20% of each other. The Case 22 comment attributes this to high-power lines gaining mass efficiency chiefly by lowering operating temperature rather than by changing conductor size, so tightening the loss target matters less once the line is already large.

**Total line mass** follows the same voltage dependence as specific mass, scaled by line length; for Case 1 (100 kW, 5 km, two-wire, above ground), total mass falls from approximately 20,000 kg (aluminum) or 43,000 kg (copper) at 1 kV to approximately 180 kg (aluminum) or 380 kg (copper) at 10 kV (Case 1.B, p. 45), consistent with the specific-mass figures above multiplied by the 5 km line length. The report cautions that "total mass plots have limited usefulness, except for observing a general magnitude of the total masses of lines considered for actual applications," since the specific parameter combinations chosen for the study are the initial lunar base's candidate lines rather than a general design tool (Summary and Conclusions, "Miscellaneous," p. 168).

**Temperature and the thermal problem.** For Case 1, conductor temperature rises from approximately 329 K to approximately 349 K (aluminum) or 354 K (copper) as voltage increases from 1 to 10 kV, because the fixed 5% power loss requires the same absolute heat dissipation while the radiating surface area shrinks as the conductor radius decreases with rising voltage (Case 1.C comments, p. 46). Because lunar soil conducts heat poorly, 0.008 W/m-K by the report's own constant, burial changes the thermal picture rather than simply improving it. The Summary and Conclusions state plainly that "it is impossible to dissipate more than a few W/m of power for conductors beneath the soil," so "high power or short lines must be better than 95% efficient to keep the power loss per unit length low" if buried ("Efficiency," p. 167), and that "a power loss in the 10s of W/m results in such a high material temperature that more conductor material is needed to compensate for the decreased value of conductivity," recommending power loss be kept "to less than 10 to 15 W/m" ("Temperature," p. 167). The location-specific conclusions state that for low power loss per unit length (around a few W/m or less), the soil "can actually cool the lines enough so that they operate at a lower temperature than they would above ground," and below-ground siting then gives slightly lower mass than above-ground siting for the same reason. But "lines with high power loss per unit length (greater than 3 or so W/m) must be operated above ground" ("Location," p. 168). Coaxial lines are stated to be "more difficult to cool underground than two-wire lines" ("Temperature," p. 167; "Conductor Geometry," p. 168), consistent with the plateau-then-rise behavior seen directly in the below-ground coaxial specific-mass curves (Cases 5, 10, 22, 28) above. At night, conductor resistance can drop by as much as a factor of one-half, and low-power-loss above-ground lines can reach temperatures low enough to risk damaging the conductor or dielectric material, potentially requiring artificial warming through load control (p. 167-168).

**Insulation comparison.** The report states that vacuum insulation, used for the two-wire lines, "provides mass and size reductions over solid dielectric insulation," and that this "becomes very significant at high voltages where the dielectric mass can dominate over the conductor mass" (p. 168), matching the coaxial-versus-two-wire divergence documented above in Cases 9 and 10. It also notes that "if the lunar soil has a high enough resistivity then vacuum insulation might work even for bare cables buried in or laying on top of the soil," and that radiation damage has little effect on vacuum insulation but may degrade solid dielectric insulation over time (p. 168).

**Power conversion.** Table 8 (p. 21) lists NASA-source-recommended power conversion techniques for a lunar DC distribution system: for DC/AC, pulse-width-modulated MOS-controlled thyristor resonant inverters; for DC/DC, resonant-mode bridge converters (series, parallel, or series-parallel, with the series-parallel type preferred for low-voltage, high-current outputs); for AC/DC, either conventional link converters, switched-mode rectifiers, and controlled rectifiers, or a newer class of AC/DC converters, of which the "type 2" design is stated as best suited to high power ratings. The report states explicitly that "the effects of the power conversion methods on mass, etc., are not included in this current report" (p. 20), so the specific-mass figures above cover conductor and insulator mass only, not converter or transformer mass.

**Other general design guidance from the Summary and Conclusions:** copper lines are thinner and more flexible and require less transport volume than aluminum, but aluminum requires less transport mass because of its lower density (p. 166). Higher voltage generally reduces mass and volume and improves flexibility, but raises resistance and thus operating temperature for a fixed efficiency (p. 166). Conductor radii below a few millimeters may be mechanically too fragile to handle (p. 166, 168). At 1% power loss (99% efficient), line temperature becomes "a very weak function of voltage" (p. 167).

Numeric values quoted above from log-scale plots are visual readings, not source-tabulated values; the source itself states each curve is ten piecewise-connected points at 1 kV increments from 1 to 10 kV, with no underlying data table printed. Readings should be treated as approximate to the resolution of a 140 dpi page render.

The report's own stated limitations (Summary and Conclusions, p. 166): it does not promote DC transmission over other approaches, but studies the interaction of DC line design parameters with the lunar environment; it does not account for the characteristics or requirements of the electrical loads or sources the lines would connect; most lines in the study were fixed at 5% power loss regardless of actual power transmitted or line length, which the report itself later states was "not the optimum approach for some of the studies"; and the five candidate lines were chosen for a specific initial lunar base design rather than as a general parametric range. Power conversion equipment mass and volume are explicitly excluded from all figures in this report.

This is Part I of a planned multi-part study. The Recommendations section (p. 169) states that "the study of ac transmission on the lunar surface is currently underway" at the time of writing, covering inductance, capacitance, skin effect, EMI, dielectric constant, and frequency (60 Hz and 20 kHz options) effects not applicable to DC. This project's reference set holds Part I (this document) only; no AC companion volume is available, and the recommendations text is silent on that companion volume's actual publication status, funding continuation, or NTRS identifier beyond stating it was underway as of this report's period of performance (June 1989 to April 1990, per the Report Documentation Page).
