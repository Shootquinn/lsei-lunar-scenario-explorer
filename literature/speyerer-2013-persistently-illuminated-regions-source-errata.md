## Abstract

Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) Wide Angle Camera (WAC) images, acquired roughly every 2 hours over both poles for a full lunar year (15 February 2010 to 5 February 2011), and Narrow Angle Camera (NAC) images at 0.5-2.0 m ground sampling distance, are used to build ratio-based and time-weighted illumination maps, illumination profiles, and high-resolution mosaics of the lunar polar regions. The dataset validates prior digital-elevation-model (DEM) based illumination simulations and identifies localized sites illuminated for nearly 94% of the year with longest eclipse periods as short as 43 hours, along with small illuminated peaks (tens of meters across) not resolved by earlier, coarser studies (p. 122).

---

### Background and objective

The Moon's 1.5-degree spin-axis tilt relative to the ecliptic normal (versus Earth's 23.3 +/- 1.3 degrees) leaves many polar crater interiors and depressions in permanent shadow while adjacent crater rims and massifs remain sunlit for extended portions of the year, producing a benign thermal environment (220 K +/- 10 K) suited to long-duration missions and sustainable outposts (p. 122).

Prior image-based and model-based studies are reviewed: Shoemaker et al. (1994) used Clementine UVVIS images (250-500 m ground sampling distance, 71-day mission) to estimate at least 30,000 km2 of persistent south-polar shadow; Nozette et al. (1996) composited more than 50 UVVIS images per pole into first-order illumination maps; Bussey et al. (1999, 2005) further quantified illumination from the same Clementine dataset; Margot et al. (1999), Garrick-Bethell et al. (2005), and Bryant (2009) derived illumination from Earth-based radar interferometric DEMs; Noda et al. (2008) and Bussey et al. (2010) used the Kaguya Laser Altimeter (LALT, 474 m/pixel); Mazarico et al. (2010) used the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA, 240 m/pixel) (p. 122-123).

As of 15 September 2012, LROC had acquired 24,270 WAC and 31,673 NAC images within 2 degrees of the poles, compared with 59 south-pole and 53 north-pole Clementine images at roughly 10-hour spacing. LRO's 50 km mapping orbit provides polar imaging every 1.9 hours (Robinson et al., 2010; Tooley et al., 2010). The stated objective is to use this higher temporal- and spatial-resolution direct-imaging dataset to characterize actual surface lighting conditions, validate previously published numerical illumination models, and identify small persistently illuminated regions relevant to future human and robotic mission planning (p. 123).

### Methods and scope

**Broad-scale multi-temporal mapping (WAC).** Over one lunar year, the WAC acquired 3,548 north-pole and 3,807 south-pole observations at a mean spacing of about 2.1-2.4 hours (Table 1, p. 123). Gaps arose from spacecraft anomalies and from other instrument operations, notably the Mini-RF radar campaigns, which reduced WAC frequency to alternating orbits (3.8 h) from June to August 2010 and added a second north-pole gap in November 2010. Observations with near-identical lighting geometry from other mission periods (234 north, 229 south) were substituted to fill gaps, reducing the longest coverage gap to 5 orbits (9.41 h) at the north pole and 4 orbits (7.53 h) at the south pole, versus 69-70 orbits (about 130 h) without fill (Table 1, p. 123-124).

Each WAC image was radiometrically calibrated, map-projected to 100 m/pixel polar stereographic using an improved WAC distortion model, a 30 m LOLA DEM, and refined spacecraft ephemeris (accurate to within one 100 m pixel), then converted to a binary illuminated/shadowed image via a filtering pipeline: a 5x5 high-pass filter to enhance contrast boundaries, a threshold to produce a binary high-contrast image, a 5x5 low-pass filter and second threshold to delimit smooth illuminated terrain while preventing growth beyond the true terminator, and a conservative global threshold on the original image to catch smooth illuminated surfaces; the two binary results were merged into a final illumination frame (p. 124-126). Analysis was limited to within 1 degree of the poles, where multi-temporal coverage exceeds 90% because of LRO orbit-inclination drift and altitude variation (about 6.5 km standard deviation); illumination maps and time-weighted maps (Figs. 5B, 7B) extend to 88 degrees latitude at lower temporal frequency for context (p. 126, 127).

Two map types were produced at 100 m/pixel over the full lunar year: a ratio-based map (fraction of imaging instances a pixel was illuminated, no interpolation) and a time-weighted map (linearly interpolates out-of-field-of-view pixels between bracketing observations to reduce bias from non-uniform temporal sampling, weighting sparsely-sampled periods such as the Mini-RF campaign more heavily) (p. 126). Illumination profiles (subsolar longitude versus subsolar latitude, colored by illuminated/shadowed/out-of-field state) were also produced for high-priority targets to allow prediction of illumination state at arbitrary subsolar geometries (p. 126-127).

**Meter-scale mapping (NAC).** Around each pole's summer solstice, when shadows are minimized, hundreds of NAC images were combined into 2 m/pixel mosaics of 85.5-90 degrees N/S (most cross-track-summed by a factor of 2, halving cross-track resolution for improved signal-to-noise). Long-exposure (24.2 ms) NAC observations were used to detect single-reflection indirect illumination reaching some permanently shadowed crater floors, though doubly-shadowed regions (no direct or indirect illumination) remain undetected (p. 128-129).

**Validation.** A 240 m/pixel LOLA lighting simulation (Mazarico et al., 2010) was compared against WAC and NAC images acquired at the same time (18 March 2011, 15:59 UTC) near 89.972 S, 344.5 E; the LOLA simulation reproduced the major Shackleton-rim terminator but missed some local depressions and small craters resolved by WAC at approximately 100 m scale (p. 129-130).

**Regional (m x n) illumination analysis.** Because fine spatial resolution can either reveal small illuminated peaks missed by coarser DEM-based models, or lower single-pixel illumination estimates as illumination "marches around" a small peak at high resolution, both effects were examined by testing illumination of m x n pixel groups (region sizes 0.01, 0.25, 1.00, 2.25, and 4.00 km2, i.e., squares from 0.1 x 0.1 km to 2.0 x 2.0 km), with a region scored illuminated if any pixel within it was illuminated, sized to reflect plausible surface mobility (p. 126, 130-131). This regional method was applied to 44 north-pole points (Table 3) and 51 south-pole points (Table 4), each reporting ratio-based illumination percentage, time-weighted illumination percentage, and longest continuous period in shadow (hours) at all five region sizes (p. 131-133).

### Key findings

- Temporal-fill observations reduced the longest coverage gap from 69 orbits (129.95 h) to 5 orbits (9.41 h) at the north pole and from 70 orbits (131.83 h) to 4 orbits (7.53 h) at the south pole (Table 1, p. 123).
- At the finest scale examined (single 100 m WAC pixel, 0.01 km2), the highest illumination values found were 63.3% ratio-based / 63.9% time-weighted at the north pole (89.370 N, 127.6 E, Whipple crater rim) and 71.6% ratio-based / 71.7% time-weighted at the south pole (89.740 S, 201.2 E, Shackleton crater rim) (Table 2, p. 128; p. 126-127).
- These single-pixel LROC WAC (100 m/pixel) maxima are lower than prior coarser-resolution studies at the same 1-degree-of-pole search: Noda et al. (2008, 474 m/pixel) reported 87% (north) and 84% (south); Bussey et al. (2010, 474 m/pixel) reported 82% (south); Mazarico et al. (2010, 240 m/pixel) reported 84.56% (north) and 89.01% (south) (Table 2, p. 128). The paper attributes the lower single-pixel values at finer resolution to illumination "marching around" small peaks pixel-by-pixel, a competing effect against the higher-resolution dataset's ability to detect small illuminated peaks that coarser models miss.
- No location was found to be permanently (100%) illuminated over the year examined, consistent with prior full-year studies (Noda et al., 2008; Bussey et al., 2010; Mazarico et al., 2010) (p. 135).
- Regional (m x n) illumination rises steeply with region size while longest shadow duration falls; for example, north-pole point N13 (89.382 N, 127.6 E) rises from 35.1% ratio-based illumination at 0.01 km2 to 86.0% at 4.00 km2, and point N02 (89.816 N, 117.3 E) falls from a longest shadow of 4,065 h at 0.01 km2 to 142 h at 4.00 km2 (Table 3, p. 131-132).
- Three stations along the Shackleton crater rim, south pole: Station 1 (89.685 S, 196.7 E), Station 2 (89.740 S, 201.2 E), and Station 3 (89.808 S, 205.9 E), corresponding to Table 4 rows S28, S30, and S33. Stations 1 and 2 (1.8 km apart) were collectively illuminated 86.9% of the year with a maximum joint shadow of 63 h; Stations 2 and 3 (2.1 km apart) were illuminated 85.5% of the year with a maximum joint shadow of 60 h. Combined, at least one of the three stations was illuminated 92.1% of the year (93.7% is also cited in the Conclusions) with the longest period all three were simultaneously in shadow lasting only 43 h; continuous illumination reached 4,960 h (over 58% of the lunar year). Adding about 100 m of local mobility at each station raised the combined illuminated fraction to 93.8% (p. 133-134, 135).
- A separate south-polar pair along the 205 E meridian, 89.418 S/221.3 E and 89.500 S/222.1 E (2.5 km apart), were illuminated 63.3% and 45.6% of the year individually; combined they reached 91.8% illumination with a longest joint shadow of 107 h, rising to 94.2% illumination and 78 h longest shadow with about 100 m of added mobility at each site, with a longest uninterrupted joint illumination span of 5,592 h (66% of the lunar year) (p. 134).
- At the north pole, three sites on the rim of Peary crater were examined at 4.00 km2 (2.0 x 2.0 km) region size: a Whipple-crater-rim region (89.366 N, 128.0 E) illuminated 85.9% of the year with longest shadow 207 h; a region halfway between Whipple and Hinshelwood craters (89.816 N, 117.3 E) illuminated 80.7% of the year with longest shadow 142 h; and two 0.01 km2 regions near Hinshelwood crater, 1.5 km apart (89.636 N, 331.0 E and 89.660 N, 324.1 E), collectively illuminated 80.1% of the year with longest joint shadow 284 h. No 0.01 km2 north-pole region was found with fewer than 298 h of persistent shadow, a less benign thermal profile than the south-pole sites (p. 134-135).
- Shackleton crater's shadowed interior area was estimated at 235 km2 from WAC images, consistent with the 233 km2 found by LOLA-based simulation (Mazarico et al., 2010) (p. 130-131).
- Meter-scale NAC images identified small illuminated peaks (about 0.01 km2) in an area a 474 m/pixel Kaguya DEM (Bussey et al., 2010) had modeled as eclipsed for 276 h; the NAC observations reduced the modeled longest eclipse at that point ("Point B") to fewer than 180 h. Conversely, NAC imaging also revealed small craters in shadow at times a coarser model showed as illuminated (p. 129-130).
- Long-exposure NAC mosaics of Shoemaker crater's permanently shadowed floor showed most of the floor receiving diffuse, single-reflection indirect illumination from sunlit terrain near the rim, though some areas remain doubly shadowed (p. 128-129).
- Following LRO's transition to a frozen orbit in December 2011, coverage and resolution trade-offs shift: north-pole imaging broadens with lower resolution (NAC 2 m, WAC 300 m), while south-pole imaging gains higher resolution with smaller footprints (NAC 0.6 m, WAC 60 m) (p. 135).

Table 1 (temporal coverage, p. 123) and Table 2 (cross-study comparison of highly illuminated points, p. 128) are reproduced above. Table 3 (p. 131-132) tabulates all 44 north-pole points and Table 4 (p. 132-133) all 51 south-pole points, each giving latitude, longitude, WAC observation count, longest temporal coverage gap, and the ratio-based percentage, time-weighted percentage, and longest shadow duration (hours) at all five region sizes; these are the load-bearing per-site data underlying the Key findings above and are not reproduced in full here. Figure 7 (p. 128) presents both quantities as polar color maps: a linear 0-300 hour scale for longest temporal gap/eclipse duration, and a 0 to greater than 70% grayscale for time-weighted illumination.

### Limitations

- The 100 m WAC pixel scale and 1-degree-of-pole analysis extent mean sub-pixel illuminated features and areas beyond 1 degree (though mapped to 88 degrees at reduced temporal frequency) are outside the primary quantitative analysis (p. 126).
- Longest-shadow-duration values are reported as unknown ("-") for several point/region-size combinations where temporal coverage gaps occurred while the region was transitioning between shadowed and illuminated states; this affects multiple rows in both Table 3 and Table 4 (p. 131-133).
- NAC-based detection of indirect (single-reflection) illumination in permanently shadowed craters depends on exposure time and the amount of secondary illumination available, and does not resolve doubly-shadowed terrain (p. 128-129).
- Illumination simulations built from lower-resolution DEMs (Kaguya LALT 474 m/pixel, LOLA 240 m/pixel) are described as sensitive to input DEM quality at the extreme incidence angles found near the poles, and early radar-derived DEMs additionally had incomplete far-side topographic coverage (p. 130).
- The study period is one lunar year (15 February 2010 to 5 February 2011); the authors state that no location on the Moon is permanently illuminated once lunar eclipses (during which the entire polar region goes dark) are accounted for (p. 135).

Two items are reported here neutrally as printed in the source, without correction. Table 4's first two data rows are both labeled "S01" in the source (distinct points at 89.883 S/33.5 E and 89.918 S/45.0 E); the ID sequence then continues from S03, skipping "S02," so the table still contains 51 distinct south-pole rows. Figure 14's caption reads "LROC WAC illumination map of the north polar region," but the map depicted is centered on Shackleton crater at 89.5 S and is explicitly linked to Table 4 (the south-pole table); the "north" in the caption appears to be a source labeling error.

### Topic mapping

Sections 3 and 4 (Broad-scale multi-temporal mapping; Meter-scale mapping and analysis) and Tables 1-2 address the imaging methodology, temporal coverage, and cross-study comparison of illumination percentages. Section 5 (Discussion) and Tables 3-4, together with Figures 13-15, address the site-level regional illumination analysis, including the named Shackleton-rim stations and Peary-crater-rim sites, with per-site ratio-based percentage, time-weighted percentage, and longest-shadow-duration values across five region sizes. Section 6 (Conclusions) addresses the overall finding that no location is permanently illuminated but that closely spaced site groups can collectively provide near-continuous solar access with short maximum eclipse durations.
