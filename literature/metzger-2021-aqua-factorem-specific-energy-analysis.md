## Abstract

Proposes an ice-mining architecture ("Aqua Factorem") that treats lunar ice as a fine granular mineral phase already broken apart by micrometeoroid gardening, and extracts it by particle-separation methods (pneumatic, magnetic, electrostatic, with vibrational separation as a backup to electrostatic) rather than by thermal vaporization. Reports a benchtop, low-fidelity prototype tested with lunar soil simulants (LMS-1, LHS-1) and a plastic ice-surrogate for magnetic and pneumatic-scale processes, plus a separate demonstration of tribocharging with actual cryogenically ground water ice. Builds a bottom-up mass and power roll-up for a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) surface segment (less than 2,500 kg, an estimated 50 kW) sized to produce enough propellant to boost one 2,500 kg geosynchronous satellite from GTO to GEO every four months, and compares this MVP, by mass, power, and cost, against a full-scale thermal-extraction ice-mining architecture published by Kornuta et al. (2018, 2019). States a 98.3% reduction in surface-segment power relative to that baseline architecture, and a separate, earlier-stated 97.5% (and potentially greater than 99%) reduction relative to thermal extraction in a back-of-envelope illustration. States TRL context throughout: pneumatic conveyance is characterized as already TRL 5/6 on NASA/Honeybee Robotics heritage; the ice-specific beneficiation sequence as a whole is treated as a low-TRL concept whose next step, after this Phase I, is laboratory, vacuum-chamber, and analog-field development toward TRL 6.

### Background and objective

The report frames its motivating problem as the energy cost of driving off water vapor from lunar regolith by heating: most of the input energy heats the non-ice lithic fraction rather than the ice, and experiments and modeling cited by the authors show that vapor driven off by heat migrates away from, not toward, the heat source, so much of it re-freezes elsewhere in the soil instead of being captured (p. 1). The authors state this creates two related problems for thermal extraction: a large energy budget, and vapor loss to re-freezing that undermines yield.

The proposed alternative treats lunar ice as physically equivalent to any other lunar mineral rather than as a substance requiring phase change to recover. The report's central physical argument, developed across pp. 1-9, is that billions of years of micrometeoroid bombardment has already broken essentially all crystalline solids in the upper regolith, including ice deposited in permanently shadowed regions (PSRs), into fine grains mixed with lithic fragments, citing modeling by Hurley et al. (2012) and Near-Infrared reflectance evidence for ice grain sizes on the order of 8 to 70 micrometers (citing LCROSS and Moon Mineralogy Mapper data). If ice exists as discrete fine grains rather than as solid lenses or ice-cemented soil, it can in principle be separated from lithic grains by grain-sorting methods without ever vaporizing it, avoiding both the thermal energy penalty and the vapor-migration yield loss. The stated design goal is a "Minimum Viable Product" (MVP) architecture: a startup-scale mining operation, small enough for early commercial investment, that can begin producing revenue while smoothly scaling to larger operations as risk is retired (p. 2), rather than an architecture intended from the outset to match full-scale thermal-extraction throughput.

The stated commercial application is boosting geosynchronous communications satellites from GTO to GEO using mined and manufactured lunar propellant, delivered by a reusable Lander/Tug pair, as an alternative to the six-to-twelve-month self-boost using onboard electric thrusters (p. 43-44).

### Methods and scope

The report combines four kinds of evidence: physical/geological argument for the state of lunar ice, analytical and spreadsheet modeling of particle size distributions and separation-stage mass balances, benchtop laboratory experiments using simulants, and a systems-level mass/power/cost roll-up used to compare the proposed architecture against a published baseline.

Simulants used were Exolith Lab's Lunar Mare Simulant (LMS-1) and Lunar Highlands Simulant (LHS-1), chosen to represent the two dominant PSR mineralogies (mafic and feldspathic), with published density and magnetic susceptibility for their major mineral components given in Table 1 (p. 19). Ice was represented in most tests by ground plastic sandblasting media, chosen for its similarity to ice in density and (low) magnetism, to avoid the practical difficulty of keeping real ice frozen through repeated benchtop handling; the report states electrostatic properties of plastic were not validated as ice-representative, so a separate set of tests (Section 2.9.1) used actual water ice, ground with an ice grinder and then further ground by hand in a mortar and pestle inside a liquid nitrogen bath, mixed with liquid-nitrogen-chilled simulant.

Because plastic-in-simulant concentration could not be measured directly (immersion density testing is confounded by plastic's tendency to float, and microscope-based grain counting was judged out of scope for Phase I), the authors instead calibrated a bulk-density-to-concentration correlation, filling a container to a defined maximum-density packing and relating measured bulk density to known plastic fraction (Figure 11, p. 20-21), with linear fits reported as C(LMS-1) = (115.05 +/- 8.11) - (62.763 +/- 4.96) x rho(LMS-1), and C(LHS-1) = (152.41 +/- 7.15) - (88.762 +/- 4.44) x rho(LHS-1), where C is plastic concentration and rho is measured bulk density.

Magnetic separation was tested on a benchtop drum apparatus (permanent magnets on a rotating roller, Figure 15-16) processing LMS-1 and LHS-1 with 5 wt% plastic added, in one-pass and two-pass configurations, at a measured throughput of 11 kg/hr. Magnetic susceptibility of the simulants and their magnetic/non-magnetic isolates was independently measured on a Faraday scale referenced against aqueous and crystalline nickel chloride (Table 2, p. 22). Electrostatic separation was tested on a purpose-built apparatus (a 20,000 VDC charged plate, a grounded plate, and a vibrating-motor feed chute, Figure 18) first with plastic-in-simulant, then with actual cryogenically prepared icy simulant (Figure 19-20). Vibrational separation was tested by vibrating LMS-1 (with and without a narrow pre-sieved size cut) at angles between level and 15 degrees from horizontal and sieving/weighing the resulting layers (Table 17-18).

Particle-size distribution modeling (Appendix A) fit a soil-mechanics-style cumulative distribution function to Apollo lunar soil data, then derived four candidate ice-grain models ("Ice Model 1" through "Ice Model 4") by adjusting the same functional form's fines and coarse-tail exponents to match, respectively, the lunar soil model itself, the roughly 8 micrometer LCROSS-derived mean grain size, the roughly 70 micrometer M3-derived mean grain size, or a combination (Table 3/A.1, p. 24 and p. 4 of Appendix A). All four models were carried through the de-dusting, magnetic, and pneumatic-sorting mass-balance calculations in parallel to bound the effect of this uncertainty.

The architecture-level comparison (Section 3) builds a bottom-up mass roll-up (Tables 21-22) and a bottom-up power roll-up (Appendix Table B.2) for an MVP surface segment sized against a stated requirement (Table B.1): enough propellant to boost one 2,500 kg satellite from GTO to GEO every four months, assuming 50% mining/hauling duty cycle and 5% ice yield from regolith. This MVP architecture, and a second, larger-throughput variant (Surface Segment mass 8,700-8,770 kg, supporting 12 missions per year), are compared against the ice-mining architecture published by Kornuta et al. (2018, "Commercial Lunar Propellant Architecture," and 2019 companion paper), used throughout as the report's baseline comparator. A separate transportation/astrodynamics model (Sections 3.2.2-3.2.7), built with NASA GMAT trajectory simulation, mass-estimating relationships for vehicle subsystems, and a Rocket Propulsion Analysis specific-impulse model, evaluates variants of the Lander/Tug concept of operations (orbital-tug cryo-transfer, ion propulsion return, aerobraking, zero-boil-off, stoichiometric propellant mixture, split lift) against a baseline GTO-GEO case requiring 9,300 kg propellant.

### Key findings

**Physical state of the ice.** The report argues, without direct ground truth, that PSR ice is most likely present as fine, loose or weakly cemented grains rather than as solid lenses or thick ice coatings bonding soil grains together, citing four lines of evidence (pp. 5-8). First, a geothermal-gradient argument: in a PSR the near-surface geothermal gradient dominates at millimeter depth (versus 30-80 cm where a solar-driven thermal wave operates outside PSRs), so vapor entering a PSR freezes at the coldest, uppermost surface with no physical mechanism to migrate it deeper, meaning subsequent gardening mixes pure ice fines with pure lithic fines rather than creating an ice-soil composite. Second, a micrometeoroid-impact energy calculation: comparing the shock energy per grain-to-grain contact (as a function of distance from an impact site) against experimentally measured forces needed to slide lunar-simulant grain contacts (citing Cole and Peters, 2008), the authors conclude impacts are a net comminuting (fragmenting), not net bonding, process, consistent with the general absence of significant cementation in returned Apollo samples. Third, a phase-physics argument: the flash-evaporation rate of liquid water in vacuum (about 2.7 g/s even under an assumed 100 mbar water-vapor atmosphere at 19 K) is fast enough that a 100 micrometer liquid droplet would vaporize in about 0.2 picoseconds, ruling out a "water agglutinate" mechanism analogous to the glass-splash agglutinates formed by molten lithic material. Fourth, a worst-case fallback: even if some ice is weakly bonded to lithic grains, light grinding (already included in the architecture) is expected to liberate ice fragments along grain boundaries at far lower energy cost than vaporization, with an energetics calculation showing a 1 micrometer ice particle broken off a lithic grain would be heated only from 100 K to 103.4 K, a stated 30x margin below the roughly 200 K threshold at which 1 micrometer ice grains sublime within about 12 hours. The report classifies possible ice/lithic grain relationships (Figure 4, credited to Doug Rickman, NASA/MSFC, p. 8) into six morphologies: Continuous and "Raisins in Pudding" (judged likely not excavatable), Bridging and "Nodules, Fracture Fills, and Lenses" (excavatability dependent on cementation degree), and Non-Bridging and Non-Filling (excavatable). The report notes prior field tests (Mantovani et al., 2014; Metzger et al., NASA/TM-2011-216302) found the Filling and "Raisins" cases as hard as granite at cryogenic temperature, unsuitable for an excavation-based approach, while the Bridging case should be workable at lower water concentrations if the ice "neck" bonding two grains is weak.

**Beneficiation architecture.** The proposed process (Figure 5, p. 13) runs excavated icy regolith through rock rejection (a "grizzly," with autonomous rock-unjamming logic), gravel separation (a mechanical wiper/auger with jam-prevention gearing and a release-hatch backup, innovated by an Embry-Riddle team at the 2016 NASA Robotic Mining Competition), and light grinding, then into a Beneficiation System combining pneumatic separation, magnetic separation, and electrostatic (or, alternately, vibrational) separation applied twice, once to a de-dusted fine stream and once to the coarse granular stream. Pneumatic separation exploits particles' ballistic coefficient (density times diameter); because rock density is roughly three times ice density, pneumatic separation alone places lithic particles of diameter d in the same size bin as ice particles of diameter 3d (nine times the inertia), so it achieves only partial separation and is described as a sorting pre-stage rather than a standalone method. Magnetic separation exploits the higher paramagnetic susceptibility of lithic/glass material (dominated by nanophase iron, npFe, in space-weathered glass patina) relative to ice, and is stated to discard up to 80% of lithic material to slag ahead of the electrostatic stage; the report notes the Curie-Weiss law predicts roughly 6x stronger magnetic susceptibility for olivine at PSR temperature (40 K, using an 11 K Curie constant) versus room temperature (293 K), making magnetic separation more effective in the cryogenic PSR environment than at Earth ambient. Electrostatic (tribocharging) separation exploits published charging-power-law coefficients showing ice accelerates in an applied electric field 100 to 10,000 times more than silicate grains of the same diameter, built on prior NASA electrostatic beneficiation of ilmenite from silicates (50-60% concentration in one pass, 90% in two passes, per Trigwell et al., 2012). Vibrational separation, tested as a lower-risk alternative to electrostatic for the final stage, exploits the Brazil Nut Effect: coarser particles migrate downward under vibration while finer particles rise, and because pneumatic size-sorting can be tuned so ice grains in a given stream are systematically larger than co-occurring lithic grains (Finding 6, p. 40), the finer/coarser split correlates with an ice/lithic split without needing charge-based separation.

**Experimental results.** Benchtop magnetic beneficiation of LMS-1 (mare simulant) discarded a mean 63.41 +/- 0.25 wt% of material as non-magnetic tailings after one pass and 78.97 +/- 0.09 wt% after two passes (Table 9), corresponding to a modeled ice concentration increase from 5 wt% to 12.5 wt% (2.5x) after one pass and to 20 wt% (4x) after two passes, with a third pass producing no further benefit. LHS-1 (highlands simulant) showed a smaller effect, given its lower-susceptibility mineralogy: one-pass tailings of 44.95 +/- 0.94 wt% and two-pass tailings of 48.49 +/- 0.55 wt%, corresponding to a modeled ice concentration increase from 5 wt% to 8.7 wt% (one pass) and 9.3 wt% (two passes). Using the calibrated bulk-density correlation, measured plastic (ice-surrogate) concentration after magnetic beneficiation was 15.57 +/- 0.04 wt% for LMS-1 (a 3.1x increase from an as-mixed 4.36 +/- 1.09 wt%) and 12.57 +/- 1.96 wt% for LHS-1 (a 2.51x increase from an as-mixed 4.62 +/- 1.32 wt%) (Table 10). The electrostatic separator apparatus was validated with plastic-in-simulant (observable increase in spray angle with the field energized) and then, in Section 2.9.1, with actual cryogenically ground water ice mixed into chilled simulant: the same field-on/field-off spray-angle change was observed, which the report interprets as confirming that ice tribocharges alongside lithic particles in a mixture, but the report explicitly states that demonstrating and quantifying enhanced separation efficiency for real ice "is left to future work" -- no separation-efficiency number is reported for the real-ice test. Vibrational separation of LMS-1 produced a "visibly finer" isolate that was 97 wt% below 250 micrometers and a "visibly coarser" isolate that was 35 wt% above 425 micrometers (Table 17); a follow-up test on a narrow pre-sieved size cut found top and bottom fractions had similar bulk density (1.58 vs. 1.55 g/cm3) and specific gravity (2.61 vs. 2.77 g/cm3) (Table 18), leading the authors to conclude the vibrational effect is driven primarily by particle size rather than density.

**Scaling the magnetic stage to full Kornuta et al. throughput.** The benchtop magnetic drum's measured 11 kg/hr rate is stated to reach the MVP's 88 kg/hr benchmark by widening the drum fourfold (to 1 m) and running two units, or one unit if the beneficiation stage is decoupled from the mining cycle by surge capacity. Scaling the same hardware concept to match the full Kornuta et al. mine's throughput (3,880 kg/hr, at daylight-only operation and 5% yield) is estimated to require 88 such 1-m drums, or, if future magnetic-field and geometry improvements provide a further 10x speed increase, as few as nine units (p. 33).

**Modeled separation-stage results.** For a repeated (10-split) in-line magnetic diversion of the fine dust stream, the modeled non-magnetic contamination remaining with the magnetic isolate is (0.5)^10 = 0.1%. Under an assumed "mature mare soil" magnetic-susceptibility scenario, the model predicts the non-magnetic dust stream would already be about 78 wt% pure ice, potentially requiring no further processing (Finding 3, p. 27); under an "immature highlands soil" scenario, the model predicts the same stream's non-magnetic anorthosite content at 99.6% purity, framed as a potential aluminum-extraction byproduct (Finding 4, p. 27). De-dusting mass-balance modeling (Table 4) found the fraction of total ice ending up in the fine dust stream ranges from 9.4 to 64.5 wt% depending on which of the four ice particle-size models is used, and the ice fraction within that dust stream ranges from 1.7 to 10.4 wt%; the authors note this is high enough that a simplified "pneumatics-only" version processing only the dust stream, with granulars treated as tailings, would be appropriate for an early Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) demonstration mission (Finding 1), while noting roughly 70% of the resource remains in the granular stream so a full-scale system needs granular processing as well (Finding 2). Granular magnetic separation modeling found the ice concentration increase from magnetic beneficiation of the coarse stream ranges, across the four ice-grain models, from +192% to +270% for LMS-1 and +89% to +149% for LHS-1 (Table 12), with the downstream mass flow rate into subsequent stages reduced 45-67% (Finding 5, p. 39). Pneumatic size-sorting modeling split the granular stream into eight channels by particle size (Table 14) and found the computed mass flow rate in each channel small enough to be easily scaled (Finding 8, p. 42); if the ice particle-size distribution is in fact coarser than the lithic distribution (as in Ice Models 3 and 4), the largest-size channels become strongly ice-dominated, which the authors suggest could support a simplified excavation-plus-pneumatic-only approach for that fraction of the resource (Finding 7, p. 42).

**Site assumptions and excavation rate.** The architecture targets what the report calls "Type 2" PSR locations (citing Austin et al., 2019, and an Elliott et al., 2019, poster), sites within a few kilometers of sunlight on gentle slopes, with an expected shallow dry overburden of about 30-50 cm to be removed before reaching ice-bearing regolith (p. 9). Excavation is modeled on the NASA RASSOR (Regolith Advanced Surface Systems Operations Robot) bucket-drum concept: a 25 kg robot hauling 20 kg per load at 20 cm/s, drawing 120 W while driving, 240 W while positioning, and 48 W while excavating loose material, requiring an 8-hour recharge after 16 hours of operation (p. 59). At a modeled 100 m round-trip distance between dig site and the Aqua Factorem plant inside the PSR (8-minute one-way transit, 4 minutes to excavate 20 kg), each excavator completes 3 round trips per hour, moving 60 kg/hr, for 960 kg/day at 16 hr/day operation; the MVP architecture specifies two such excavators. A separate Transporter rover of the same mass and power class hauls the beneficiated (post-Aqua-Factorem) output roughly 4 km from the PSR plant to the sunlit water-processing equipment, also completing 3 round trips per hour (60 kg/hr, up to 960 kg/day at 16 hr/day). Two further RASSOR-equivalent robots handle site preparation (grading and berming a 100 m2 landing pad, treated with an Earth-supplied polymer binding agent) and subsequently haul finished water from the cleanup plant to the Lander/Tug.

**Mass and power estimates (Section 3.7, Appendix B).** The report's roll-up gives a total Surface Segment mass of 2,382.50 kg (about 2.4 t) for the MVP architecture: 225.00 kg of elements inside the PSR (two 25 kg excavators, a 100 kg beneficiation system, a 50 kg crusher, a 25 kg transporter; Table 21) and 2,157.50 kg of elements outside the PSR (Table 22), of which the largest single item is a 1,500.00 kg power/thermal/control system, followed by 550.00 kg of storage vessels; water-cleanup subsystem items (melter, degasification stages, reverse osmosis, ion exchange resin, catalytic carbon filter, electrolysis, liquefaction) are each in the 3.5-9.0 kg range. Table B.2's power roll-up totals 429,677.55 kWh of annual energy, splitting into 253,653.75 kWh/yr for water processing (melting ice, 33,544.28 kWh/yr; raising water 0-100 C, 41,418.14 kWh/yr; reverse-osmosis/ion-bed pumping, 43,200.00 kWh/yr; electrolysis at a stated 39 kWh/kg H2 (4.33 kWh/kg H2O), 120,807.00 kWh/yr; LH2 liquefaction at a stated theoretical minimum of 3.3 kWh/kg, 10,219.77 kWh/yr; LO2 liquefaction at a stated theoretical minimum of 0.18 kWh/kg, 2,787.21 kWh/yr for the primary stream plus 1,677.35 kWh/yr for excess LO2 production) and 176,023.80 kWh/yr for "other equipment" (four excavators and one transporter at 644.76 kWh/yr each where active, a crusher estimated as equivalent to one water pump at 43,200.00 kWh/yr, and the Aqua Factorem beneficiator itself estimated as equivalent to three water pumps at 129,600.00 kWh/yr). Dividing the resulting daily total (1,193.55 kWh) by 24 hours gives 49.7 kW, which the report rounds to and reports as a 50 kW total Surface Segment power requirement (p. 60).

Table B.1 gives the annual MVP throughput underlying these figures: 27,900.00 kg of water, 3,096.90 kg of liquid hydrogen, 15,484.50 kg of liquid oxygen (plus 9,318.60 kg of excess liquid oxygen from the assumed 5.6:1 oxidizer-to-fuel ratio), 558,000.00 kg of regolith excavated in the PSR, and 160,425.00 kg of concentrated regolith remaining after Aqua Factorem beneficiation removes the stated 0.75 waste-material fraction. Dividing the report's own annual totals gives a specific energy, not stated by the source in these units but directly computable from Tables B.1 and B.2, of about 15.4 kWh per kg of water (15,400 kWh per tonne) for the full chain from excavation through liquefaction, and about 6.31 kWh per kg of water (6,310 kWh per tonne) for the "other equipment" category alone (excavation, crushing, and Aqua Factorem beneficiation, excluding water cleanup, electrolysis, and liquefaction).

**The 98.3% power-reduction claim.** The report states in two places (Section 3.7, p. 60, and Section 7.0 Conclusion, p. 63) that the estimated 50 kW MVP Surface Segment power requirement represents "a 98.3% power reduction from the baseline mining system." The baseline is identified earlier in Section 3.1 (p. 46) as the architecture published by Kornuta et al. (2018, 2019), stated there to require a 2.8 MW power system, $4B initial investment, 30,000 kg of surface hardware mass, and annual production of 2,450,000 kg of propellant, versus the MVP's stated $213M initial investment (5.3% of Kornuta et al.), under 2,500 kg mass for the surface segment alone (8.3% of Kornuta et al.), 3,822 kg total surface-plus-space-segment mass, and 27,900 kg of annual propellant production (1% of Kornuta et al.). The same passage separately states the 50 kW figure is "1.7% of Kornuta et al." (consistent with 50 kW against a 2.8 MW baseline). The report does not state the 98.3% figure as a specific-energy (energy per unit water or propellant produced) comparison; by the report's own throughput figures, the MVP architecture whose power draw is being compared produces about 1% of the baseline architecture's annual propellant output. A separate, earlier illustration in Section 1.1 (p. 9) compares Aqua Factorem to thermal extraction directly: citing the Kornuta et al. (ULA) study's stated 800 kW thermal-energy requirement to extract 2,450 t of water per year at 5% ice concentration (49,000 t of regolith per year, or 1.55 kg/s), the report estimates that under a 70% solar duty cycle and a cruder 2% yield assumption, matching that output would require processing at 5.55 kg/s, which, assuming a larger-scale beneficiator processing 3 kg/s (ten times the MVP pilot-plant target) at 50% mining/hauling duty cycle would need four such units at 5 kW each (20 kW total), yielding "a 97.5% power reduction versus thermal extraction," with a further, qualitatively stated ">99% reduction of total energy and infrastructure in the PSR" once subsequent water-processing steps are also relocated to sunlight. The report states reducing power need is "the primary objective of our project" (p. 60).

**Transportation architecture.** A Lander/Tug concept of operations is modeled with a baseline GTO-GEO boost requiring 9,300 kg of propellant. Individual enhancements are quantified relative to this baseline: aerobraking alone produces a 3% net propellant loss for GTO-GEO (the mass penalty of thermal protection outweighing the saving) but a 39% reduction for LEO-GEO; an orbital tug (cryo-transfer in low lunar orbit rather than landing) gives a 15% reduction; ion propulsion on the GEO-to-LLO return leg gives an 11% reduction; zero-boil-off slow aerobraking gives a 15% reduction for GTO-GEO and 40% for LEO-GEO; switching to a stoichiometric oxidizer-to-fuel ratio (rather than an oxygen-excess ratio) gives a 29% reduction and is described as the single largest individual improvement, adopted as the new baseline; combining the orbital tug with stoichiometric propellant gives a 39% reduction; adding aerobraking to that combination gives a 46% reduction for GTO-GEO; and a split lift with aerobraking gives a 60% reduction for the larger LEO-GEO delta-v case. The report separately notes these transportation and mining-architecture results support other stated business opportunities, including sale of excess oxygen (about 3 t every four months), transportation services to NASA's Gateway, and orbital-debris disposal demonstrations, without quantitative modeling of the latter.

**Water treatment.** Table 19 (p. 55-56) lists potential vapor-mobilized and solar-wind-implanted volatile species that could be present in lunar ice (nitrogen, carbon monoxide, argon, methane, krypton, an argon clathrate, xenon, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, ammonia, pentane, hydrogen cyanide, toluene, ammonium hydrosulfide, sulfur, copper, zinc, arsenic, selenium, silver, bromine, iodine, plus solar-wind hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, helium, neon, argon, krypton, and xenon), each mapped to a candidate treatment method (degasification, reverse osmosis, ion exchange, or activated catalytic carbon filtration). Table 20 evaluates eight generic water-treatment technologies (micro/ultra-filtration, carbon filters, greensand filters, reverse osmosis, fractional distillation/sublimation, ion exchange resins, electrodeionization, and differential permeation) against these contaminants, and Table 21 lays out a notional 11-step water cleanup process combining sieving, crushing, two degasification stages, reverse osmosis, ion exchange, catalytic carbon filtration, electrolysis, liquefaction, and storage.

### Limitations

The report identifies several items explicitly as out of scope for Phase I or as needing further work. Mineralogical analysis of experimentally beneficiated samples was stated as out of scope; the composition breakdown in Table 11 was instead back-solved algebraically (two unknowns, X and Y, representing the fraction of ilmenite/basalt found insufficiently magnetic and the fraction of anorthosite found sufficiently magnetic) rather than measured directly. The report flags, as a risk identified during the research itself, that if PSR ice develops an npFe-bearing glassy patina the way lithic dust does, ice in the fine dust stream could itself become magnetic, which would undermine the assumption that magnetic separation cleanly isolates non-magnetic ice from that stream; the report calls this "only a modest issue" but states it "highlights the need for ground truth of the ice" and defers resolution to Phase II or another effort. Electrostatic separation was validated with real ice only qualitatively (a tribocharging signature was observed) and the report states explicitly that demonstrating and quantifying actual separation efficiency for real ice is left to future work. Vibrational separation was tested only with the plastic surrogate and plain LMS-1, not with real ice. Development work is stated as needed for a jam-proof gravel separator with a release-hatch backup, and for comparing grinding, rolling, and impact-hammering methods to maximize ice liberation without excessive heating. The physical-state-of-ice argument (four lines of evidence, above) is explicitly qualified: "we need ground-truth to prove the physical state of the ice." Magnetic susceptibility values used in the separation models are stated as "typical" literature values rather than measurements specific to PSR-conditioned regolith, with a full analysis called out of scope for Phase I. The basalt entry in the triboelectric series (Table 8) was estimated by analogy and interpolation rather than measured, with the report stating "experimental work that is beyond the scope of Phase 1 would be required to quantify this." The LEO-GEO transportation variants were modeled at lower fidelity than the GTO-GEO baseline, using "by hand" iteration rather than a converged computer optimization, with the report stating a more robust program is needed. On technology readiness, the report characterizes pneumatic conveyance and size-sorting as already TRL 5/6 based on prior NASA and Honeybee Robotics work, but frames electrostatic separation of ice specifically as "a low-TRL concept," and states in Section 5.0 that the path forward after this Phase I effort is to develop the beneficiating technology to TRL-6 through laboratory experiments, vacuum-chamber tests, and/or analog field demonstrations, followed by a Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) flight demonstration before full-scale implementation.

### Topic mapping

Section 2 (pp. 15-43) covers simulant selection and characterization (Tables 1-2), ice and soil particle-size models (Table 3), and stage-by-stage experimental and modeled beneficiation results (Sections 2.3-2.10, Tables 4-18, Findings 1-8), including the actual-ice electrostatic tribocharging test (Section 2.9.1). Section 3 (pp. 43-64) covers the architecture-level analysis: transportation and propellant-boost concept of operations and variant results (Section 3.2, Figures 22-29), surface power system (Section 3.3), water cleanup and electrolysis (Section 3.4, Tables 19-21), mining and site preparation (Sections 3.5-3.6), and the mass/power/cost roll-up and the 98.3% power-reduction claim against the Kornuta et al. baseline (Section 3.7, Tables 21-22).
