## Abstract

A policy white paper examining whether modern super heavy lift (SHL) launch vehicles, defined
by NASA as those that can lift more than 50,000 kg (50 metric tons) to low Earth orbit (LEO),
can be a sustainable and profitable commercial business rather than an engineering marvel that
fails on economics. The paper contrasts the two leading commercial SHL competitors, SpaceX
(Starship, fully reusable) and Blue Origin (New Glenn, partially reusable), against the
government SHL vehicles (NASA SLS, China Long March 9 and 10), reviews the interdependent
technical and economic factors that govern SHL cost per kilogram (economies and diseconomies
of scale, payload and fairing utilization, expendability versus reusability versus
refuelability), and then presents three hypothetical Starship cost scenarios. Those scenarios
model a fully reusable Starship falling from a full-cost first-use figure of 733 to 900 dollars
per kg down to a matured 67 to 233 dollars per kg after nine to ten reuse cycles, on an initial
rocket manufacturing cost basis of 50 to 100 million dollars. Table 1 gives the fully reusable
Starship headline range as 67 to 900 dollars per kg to LEO. The paper surveys the potential
demand sinks that could justify SHL (large broadband constellations, orbital data centers,
new commercial space stations, sustained lunar presence, space-based solar power, space
tourism, Mars colonization, and point-to-point transport), and concludes that the near-term
path to profitability most likely runs through deploying and replenishing large communication
and broadband megaconstellations at a competitive price per kilogram, with vertical
integration between an SHL rocket (supply) and a large constellation (demand) as the critical
factor. The A380 jumbo jet is used as the cautionary analogy: greater size does not
automatically yield greater margins.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper frames SHL as a renewed expansion in payload mass and volume to orbit, distinctive
today because two commercial competitors, SpaceX and Blue Origin, are each building SHL rockets
(Starship and New Glenn) alongside the traditional government-funded vehicles (NASA SLS, China
Long March 9 and 10). SHL rockets have existed since 1967, when NASA's Saturn V set the record
for the largest payload capacity, stated as 140,000 kg to LEO, and flew 13 orbital launches
between 1967 and 1973 before retiring, after which there were no SHL launches for 50 years. The
modern SHL renaissance began in 2018 with the inaugural flight of SpaceX's Falcon Heavy.

The central question is explicitly economic and is posed through a transport-industry analogy:
will SHL be an economic success like the maritime sector's ultra-large container vessels
(which carry more than 20,000 standard containers and around which shipping has optimized), or
an economic failure like the Airbus A380 jumbo jet, whose massive size introduced higher costs
and operational constraints and lost out to smaller, more fuel-efficient, point-to-point
aircraft. The paper opens with the epigraph "Loved by travelers but shunned by accountants,
the A380 remains aviation's grandest reminder that innovation alone can't defy economics" and
returns to this warning repeatedly: higher cargo volume does not automatically equate to higher
margins. The stated objective is to survey SHL programs past and present, focus on the two
leading commercial competitors and the differences in their engineering and commercialization
strategies, examine the interdependent technical and economic factors likely to determine
market success, and finally survey the potential future markets that could supply SHL demand.

### Methods and scope

This is a qualitative policy-and-strategy analysis, not an econometric study or a bottom-up
engineering cost model. Its quantitative core is a comparison table of SHL vehicles (Table 1),
a strategy comparison of the two commercial competitors (Table 2), several conceptual economics
diagrams (Figure 2 on diseconomies of scale, Figure 3 on smaller-versus-larger launch vehicle
tradeoffs), and a set of three illustrative Starship cost scenarios visualized as a bar chart
(Figure 4). The cost scenarios are described by the authors as hypothetical and illustrative,
built on stated assumptions rather than observed SpaceX accounting.

Table 1 ("Launch Vehicles Carrying 50,000 kg or More to LEO," printed page 3) compiles, for
each SHL vehicle, its reusability class (Expendable E, Partially Reusable PR, or Fully Reusable
FR), an approximate dollar cost per launch in 2025 dollars, a dollar per kilogram to LEO, a
payload in metric tons, and a fairing diameter and volume. A footnote (a) states that the cost
per kg is calculated for a fully loaded fairing and "implies that this is the lowest cost point
to be achieved," so the table's per kilogram figures are best-case, fully-utilized numbers, not
typical realized prices.

The three Starship cost scenarios (printed pages 11 to 12) share a common method, stated
verbatim in the setup text. Launch cost is defined as "the depreciated cost of launch vehicle
based upon usage (e.g., number of times launched), which declines with each use, plus the
fixed marginal cost at cadence," and is distinguished from price (the fee charged to a
customer, which may include profit margin). All three scenarios assume a fully loaded fairing
of 150,000 kg where both the super heavy lift booster and the second-stage Starship are
returned to the launch site and reused ten times; they apply a constant marginal cost per use
(expressed as a percentage of initial launch vehicle value) covering maintenance, propellant,
refurbishment, logistics, ground support, and payload integration; and they use a "unit of
production" depreciation method that spreads the vehicle's cost across its number of launches
rather than its years in service. The authors flag this depreciation choice as nonstandard,
noting it raises the cost-per-kg estimate and incorporates a self-insurance mechanism to cover
potential loss of the launch vehicle. Scenario 1 assumes 35 percent marginal costs; Scenarios 2
and 3 assume a decline to 20 percent marginal costs from streamlined operations. The scope is
limited to Starship for these worked scenarios; no equivalent scenario set is built for New
Glenn or any other vehicle.

### Key findings

**Table 1 vehicle comparison (printed page 3), verified by vision.** The Starship row is
tagged FR (Fully Reusable). Its approximate cost per launch is listed as 10 to 100 million
dollars, its dollar per kilogram to LEO as 67 to 900 dollars (with footnote (h): "Starship
costs per kg varies widely depending upon first use, marginal cost"), its payload as 150 metric
tons (LEO FR) and 250 metric tons (LEO PR), and its fairing as 9 meters diameter and 1,000
cubic meters volume. Footnote (e) states the Starship cost figures are derived from Elon Musk
statements in 2022, with the dollar per kg calculated from that information. Comparators in the
same table, for context: Falcon Heavy (PR) at 90 million dollars per launch (PR config) or 150
million dollars (E config), giving 1,400 dollars per kg (PR) or 2,350 dollars per kg (E), 64
metric tons to LEO; NASA SLS Block 1 at about 2 billion dollars per launch, 28,500 dollars per
kg, 70 metric tons; SLS Block 1b at 19,000 dollars per kg, 105 metric tons; SLS Block 2 at
15,400 dollars per kg, 130 metric tons; Saturn V at about 1.5 billion dollars per launch,
10,714 dollars per kg, 140 metric tons; Blue Origin New Glenn 9x4 (PR, first stage only), 70
metric tons; China Long March 9 (plans for PR), 150 metric tons, and Long March 10 (exploring
PR), 70 metric tons. Starship's 67 to 900 dollars per kg is by a wide margin the lowest per
kilogram figure in the table.

**The Starship 67 to 900 dollars per kg range is a fully reusable range, not an expendable
one.** This is the single most important labeling point for the review. The Starship row is
tagged FR, and per footnote (h) the range spans first use (the 900 dollar high end) down to
matured reuse (the 67 dollar low end). The report gives no expendable Starship dollar per kg
anywhere; the only expendable per kilogram figures in Table 1 are for other vehicles (for
example Falcon Heavy at 2,350 dollars per kg expendable, SLS variants, Saturn V). The 67 to 900
figure must therefore never be labeled expendable.

**Scenario 1, least optimistic (printed page 11), verified by vision.** Scenario 1 applies 100
million dollars for the initial rocket and 35 percent marginal costs. A fully loaded Starship
at full cost would yield 900 dollars per kg and would be reduced to 233 dollars per kg after ten
reuse cycles. The report notes this is lower than Falcon 9's price per kg on a maximum 17.5-ton
capacity basis of about 4,000 dollars per kg.

**Scenario 2, middle (printed page 12), verified by vision.** Scenario 2 applies a 100 million
dollars initial rocket cost and a lower 20 percent marginal cost from improved efficiencies
through repeatable relaunch. A fully loaded Starship at full cost would yield 800 dollars per kg,
reduced to 133 dollars per kg after ten reuse cycles.

**Scenario 3, most optimistic (printed page 12), verified by vision.** Scenario 3 assumes a
lower rocket manufacturing cost of 50 million dollars, from a steep manufacturing learning
curve, and a low marginal cost reduced to 20 percent. A fully loaded Starship at full cost
would yield 733 dollars per kg, reduced to 67 dollars per kg after nine use cycles. The report
states that under this scenario, 67 dollars per kg is the same as Musk's estimate in 2022.

**Figure 4 chart (printed page 12, "Reusable Launches 1 to 10"), verified by vision.** The
y-axis is "Cost per Kg" gridlined from 0 to 1,000 dollars in 100-dollar increments; the x-axis
is the use number, 0 (full-cost first use) through 10. The three legend series are: #1, 100
million dollars initial rocket cost and 35 percent marginal cost (blue); #2, 100 million
dollars initial rocket cost and 20 percent marginal cost (orange); and #3, 50 million dollars
initial rocket cost and 20 percent marginal cost (green). Reading the bar heights: at use 0 the
three series sit at about 900, 800, and 733 dollars per kg, matching the three stated full-cost
figures; at use 10 they sit at about 233, 133, and 67 dollars per kg, matching the three stated
matured endpoints. The chart and the body text are internally consistent.

**Basis statement for the matured 67 to 233 dollars per kg spread (the rung this source
anchors).** Read together, the three matured scenario endpoints are 233 dollars per kg
(Scenario 1: 100 million dollars initial rocket cost, 35 percent marginal, ten reuse cycles),
133 dollars per kg (Scenario 2: 100 million dollars initial rocket cost, 20 percent marginal,
ten reuse cycles), and 67 dollars per kg (Scenario 3: 50 million dollars initial rocket cost, 20
percent marginal, nine use cycles). So 67 to 233 dollars per kg brackets the full spread of
matured fully-reusable endpoints, from least optimistic (233) to most optimistic (67). The
basis that must be stated explicitly: this is a MATURED FULLY-REUSABLE range on an initial
ROCKET (manufacturing) cost basis of 50 million to 100 million dollars, not a launch-price
basis, and NOT expendable. The 50 million dollar figure applies only to the 67 dollar endpoint
(Scenario 3); the 100 million dollar figure applies to the 233 and 133 dollar endpoints. The
report's per-launch figure (Table 1's 10 to 100 million dollars) is a different quantity from
the per-rocket manufacturing cost used in the scenarios and should not be conflated with it. A
footnote adds context on the marginal-cost assumption: at the Mars Society's 2020 convention
Musk stated a goal of reducing Starship's marginal launch cost to as low as 2 million dollars,
and if the eventual Starship cost were 10 million dollars (as indicated by Payload Research in
2024), that implies a 20 percent marginal-cost metric.

**Two competing commercial strategies (Table 2, printed page 4).** SpaceX Starship is
characterized by rapid, iterative, high-risk innovation, funding primarily from venture and
private equity capital, an estimated 150 to 200 metric tons to LEO, stainless steel structure,
full reusability (booster plus upper stage) with return-to-launch-tower "chopstick" catch,
methalox propellant on both stages, and very high vertical integration (backward to parts and
components, forward to Starlink). Blue Origin New Glenn 9x4 is characterized by careful
"measure twice, cut once" reliability, funding primarily from founder Jeff Bezos, an estimated
70 metric tons to LEO, lighter aluminum and carbon-fiber composite materials, partial
first-stage-only reusability with downrange sea landing, methalox first stage and hydrolox
second stage, and high (but indirect, via Amazon Leo) vertical integration. The New Glenn 9x4
uses nine BE-4 engines on the first stage and four BE-3U engines on the second.

**Economics of scale and payload utilization (printed pages 8 to 10).** The paper argues SHL
economics hinge on utilization, not size alone. Figure 2 shows a U-shaped average-cost curve
against launch vehicle size, with marginal costs falling then rising past an optimal design
point; some space economists are cited as postulating that Falcon 9 is near that optimal design
point. Figure 3 presents a conceptual green/yellow/red-zone framework showing that a larger,
less economical vehicle only wins when its extra lift and fairing volume are actually utilized;
a partially utilized large rocket can be more expensive per kilogram than a fully filled
smaller one. The key mechanism is that cost per launch is fixed for a given flight, so cost per
kg falls only by maximizing mass (and volume) delivered. The paper quotes the James Webb Space
Telescope's then-chief engineer describing launch limits as "the tyranny of the fairing," and
notes SHL's wider fairing (Starship at 9 meters, 1,000 cubic meters) relaxes that constraint.
It distinguishes expendability, reusability, and refuelability as separate levers, notes Falcon
9 booster B1067 returned from space for the thirty-fifth time on June 8, 2026 as evidence of
demonstrated reuse durability, and observes that in-space refueling of upper stages remains
unproven but is central to the Artemis Human Landing System architecture.

**Vertical integration and the Starlink-Starship link (printed pages 12 to 13).** The paper
identifies Starlink as Starship's anchor demand. Starlink's continued expansion depends on
deploying heftier V.3 satellites weighing up to 2,000 kg (versus current V2 Mini models at
about 575 kg each), with close to 60-meter wingspans folding to about 7 to 8 meters, offering
about 1,000 Gbps (a tenfold improvement over the V2 Mini's 96 Gbps). Starlink's roughly 10
million broadband subscribers provide recurring revenue that helps fund Starship development.
In 2024 SpaceX introduced a "Pez dispenser" deployment system to pack and dispense flatter
Starlink satellites from Starship, which the paper likens to the standardization power of the
intermodal shipping container. The paper flags that this same high vertical integration and
first-mover advantage could raise competition and regulatory concerns.

**Potential commercial markets for SHL (printed pages 13 to 17).** The paper catalogs demand
sinks that lower launch cost could unlock, cautioning that many rely on a circular dependency
(the markets are not practicable without SHL, but SHL may not be sustainable without the
markets). Context figures given: the number of satellites on orbit grew roughly tenfold from
about 1,400 in 2016 to more than 14,000 today, while the annual global launch rate rose only
about threefold (the gap reflecting more satellites per launch and falling cost per kg); the
US space sector grew 6.3 percent in current-dollar terms in 2023 (real growth only 0.6 percent)
with a 10-year average of 2.4 percent, and some forecasts see the space sector reaching 1
trillion dollars or more within ten years. The market segments surveyed are: (1) large
constellations (broadband), named as the near-term "killer application," with emerging
competitors Amazon Leo (formerly Project Kuiper), Blue Origin TeraWave, Eutelsat OneWeb,
Telesat Lightspeed, China's Qianfan and Guowang, and with Amazon Leo already contracting New
Glenn for as many as 27 launches; (2) orbital data centers (ODCs), backed by Nvidia's
Starcloud, Google and Planet's Project Suncatcher, and SpaceX's xAI acquisition proposing an
ODC constellation of up to one million LEO satellites, where a single Starship offers a
pressurized habitable volume of more than 600 cubic meters (about two-thirds of the ISS), but
whose viability against rapidly improving terrestrial data centers (5 to 40 percent energy
savings per IEA) and whose radiation, in-space assembly, and thermal-management challenges are
unresolved; (3) new commercial space stations, potentially deployable far faster than the ISS
(built over 42 assembly flights), though demand is called "highly speculative without continued
government interest"; (4) sustained lunar presence and resource extraction, requiring regular
reliable SHL access; (5) space tourism; (6) space-based solar power, with China intending to
use Long March 9 for a 1-km solar array beamed from GEO, and a 2025 Caltech technoeconomic
analysis finding Starship could deliver 559 solar modules in only 11 launches versus 280 Falcon
9 launches, yielding a much lower levelized cost of energy (Overview Energy separately announced
up to 1 gigawatt for Meta data centers as soon as 2030); (7) Mars colonization; and (8)
point-to-point terrestrial space transport (Air Force Research Laboratory "Rocket Cargo").

**Author's overall verdict (Conclusion, printed page 17).** The paper concludes it is too
early to tell whether SHL will produce a boom or a bust. The most likely near-term maturation
path is serving broadband megaconstellations at a regular launch cadence, with vertical
integration between an SHL rocket (supply) and a large constellation (demand) as the critical
factor to get over the commercialization hump. Beyond that, an evolutionary path could see SHL
enable large space-infrastructure projects (ODCs, space stations, lunar operations, space-based
solar power) that become future demand sources. The overarching lesson, drawn from mature
aviation and maritime transport, is that bigger is not always better: excessive scale can bring
complexity, reduced agility, and reduced payload utilization, and broader market factors and
externalities determine economic success. The A380 stands as the warning; the ultra-large
container ship as the aspiration.

### Limitations

The three Starship cost scenarios are explicitly hypothetical and illustrative, built on stated
assumptions (a 150,000 kg fully loaded fairing fully utilized, ten reuse cycles, fixed
percentage marginal costs, and a nonstandard unit-of-production depreciation method that the
authors themselves note raises the cost estimate), not on observed SpaceX cost accounting. The
underlying cost figures trace to Elon Musk statements from 2022 (Table 1 footnote (e)), so they
are company-sourced claims, not independently audited numbers; the report elsewhere treats the
lowest figures as aspirational. All Table 1 per kilogram figures are calculated for a fully
loaded fairing and are described as the lowest cost point to be achieved, so they are best-case,
fully-utilized numbers rather than typical realized prices, and the report's own economics-of-
scale argument (Figures 2 and 3) stresses that real utilization is frequently below full. The
cost scenarios are worked only for Starship; no equivalent scenario set is built for New Glenn
or government vehicles, and several New Glenn and Chinese vehicle cells in Table 1 are blank.
The demand-side survey is qualitative and forward-looking: the paper repeatedly labels the
future markets speculative, unproven, or reliant on a circular supply-demand dependency, and
does not estimate the dollar size or capture share of any of them. It offers no expendable
Starship dollar per kg at all, and no cislunar or lunar-surface delivered cost (no gear ratio
or trans-lunar-injection surcharge is computed here; that anchor comes from the separate Harry
W. Jones "Take or Make" primary). Being a policy white paper, it carries no formal statistical
methodology, confidence intervals, or peer review, and its central Starship figures should be
read as scenario illustrations of a plausible cost-descent curve rather than as sourced prices.
