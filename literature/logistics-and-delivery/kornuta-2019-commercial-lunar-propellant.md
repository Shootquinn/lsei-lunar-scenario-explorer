## Abstract

This is the full collaborative study report (not the condensed journal article) documenting a
multi-organization, multi-author examination of the technical and economic feasibility of a
robotic, commercial venture that mines water ice from lunar polar permanently shadowed regions
(PSRs) and processes it into liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen (LOX and LH2) propellant for
cislunar and Earth-orbit markets. The study's headline result is that a near-term annual demand
of 450 metric tons (MT) of lunar-derived propellant, requiring processing of 2,450 MT of lunar
water, could generate about 2.4 billion dollars of annual revenue, achievable with about 2.8
megawatts (MW) of total power (2 MW electrical, 0.8 MW thermal) and an estimated 4 billion
dollar initial investment, using a sublimation-based thermal mining extraction method rather
than traditional excavation.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The report opens by framing water in space as scarce and valuable relative to Earth, citing
direct mission evidence (LCROSS, Lunar Prospector, LRO) that permanently shadowed polar regions
hold substantial water ice concentrations. It frames water-derived hydrogen and oxygen as dual
use: life support (drinking water, radiation shielding, breathing oxygen) and rocket propellant
via electrolysis. The stated purpose is to examine, from an industry, government, and academic
expert perspective, the approach, challenges, and payoffs of a private business that harvests
and processes lunar ice as the foundation of a lunar, cislunar, and Earth-orbiting economy. A
stated key assumption throughout is that all construction, operation, transport, maintenance,
and repair is performed by robotic systems, with no human presence required. The report states
its aim is to demonstrate both technical and economic feasibility of a commercial lunar
propellant production capability and to provide a path to implementation for government and
private organizations.

### Methods and scope

The document is a collaborative, multi-author study organized by section, with named section
authors credited throughout (for example, the Executive Summary is attributed to Gordon
Roesler and David Kornuta; the Power Requirements section to Kornuta and Dallas Bienhoff; the
Economic Analysis section to Erica Otto and Kornuta; the Thermal Mining section to Angel
Abbud-Madrid and Chris Dreyer of Colorado School of Mines). Contributing organizations include
United Launch Alliance, Colorado School of Mines, Honeybee Robotics, Paragon Space Development
Corporation and its partner Giner Inc., Lunar Outpost, Cislunar Space Development Company,
Atomos Nuclear and Space, OffWorld Inc., Altius Space Machines, and several universities and
NASA-affiliated experts. The study proceeds by section through prospecting and resource
characterization, extraction technology (drilling and augering versus a thermal-mining capture
tent sublimation approach developed at Colorado School of Mines), water and propellant
processing hardware (the IHOP purification/electrolysis subsystem under NASA NextSTEP-2
development by Paragon and Giner), power generation and delivery options (solar photovoltaic,
heliostat, wired, and beamed transmission; nuclear fission), in-space transportation and storage,
communications, customers and demand, propellant pricing by cislunar location, mine sizing, and
a discounted cash flow economic model built in Excel to estimate Net Present Value (NPV) across
seven demand scenarios. Figures and tables throughout size specific subsystems (for example,
Table 1's thermal-mining design point and Table 14's seven-scenario NPV comparison). The study
does not report new hardware testing; it synthesizes prior mission data, contributor expertise,
and existing subsystem development programs into an integrated architecture and cost model. A
separate part of the study situates this architecture against prior ISRU economic literature,
citing Blair (2002), a 2004 STARLITE study (Colorado School of Mines, Florida Institute of
Technology, NASA Johnson and Kennedy Space Centers, and the Northern Centre for Advanced
Technology), NASA's 2005 Concept Evaluation and Refinement program, and a 2007 Spaceworks
economic analysis, describing methods ranging from simple cost-estimating relationships to
bottoms-up parametric models; Tables 16 through 20, which the text says compare production
levels, unit costs, and breakeven prices across these prior studies, did not extract numeric
data from the source PDF.

### Key findings

**Near-term demand, water throughput, and revenue.** The Executive Summary states the study has
identified a near-term annual demand of 450 metric tons (MT) of lunar-derived propellant,
equating to 2,450 MT of processed lunar water, generating 2.4 billion dollars of revenue
annually.

**Electrolysis specific energy (headline parameter for FA5).** In the Power Requirements
section, the report states that using electrolysis to produce LOX and LH2 from water requires
approximately 4.41 kWh per kg of water (citing reference 50). Processing the study's 2,450 MT of
water per year is stated as equivalent to 280 kg per hour. At 4.41 kWh/kg, this is stated to
require 1,235 kW of electrical power for electrolysis alone. This 4.41 kWh/kg figure is the
document's only directly stated specific-energy value for water-to-propellant conversion.

**Total power split (2.8 MW).** Beyond the 1,235 kW electrolysis load, contributing experts
estimated an additional 765 kW of electrical demand for robotic systems, pumps, cryocoolers,
heaters, and communications, producing a stated need for 2 MW of electrical power. Separately,
for the worst-case design point of 4 weight-percent (wt%) water concentration, sublimation to
yield 2,450 MT of water is stated to require 580 kW of thermal power; this was conservatively
rounded up to 800 kW thermal to cover unforeseen efficiency losses or locally lower ice
concentration. The total stated architecture power need is therefore 2.8 MW: 2 MW electrical
plus 0.8 MW thermal.

**Water weight-percent assumptions.** The report states the best estimate of lunar PSR water ice
content comes from the LCROSS mission at 5.5 wt%, while other estimates put ice content at 10
wt% and the most pessimistic estimates at 1 wt%. This is stated to suggest the thermal mining
process should be situated at a location with more than 4 wt% ice, which the report adopts as
its worst-case design point. The report separately notes that recent reanalysis of mission data
found areas with more than 30 wt% ice, and that at 30 wt%, the power needed to sublimate icy
regolith is 33% less than at 4 wt% and only 10% greater than the power needed to sublimate pure
ice. Figure 17 plots power to sublimate 2,450 MT of water per year at 70% solar availability as a
function of water weight percent, showing power is highly sensitive to concentration below about
2 wt% (dominated by the energy cost of heating regolith) and relatively insensitive above about
5 wt%.

**Thermal-mining plant sizing (Table 1, a distinct quantity from near-term demand).** Table 1
sizes a thermal-mining design for water extraction of 2,450 MT per year, stated to produce 1,640
MT of propellant per year, at both a 4 wt% and a 30 wt% design point. For the 4 wt% case: area
mined 100,000 square meters per year, yield per square meter 25 kg, ice sublimated per square
meter 27 kg, dwell time 48 hours, move time 12 hours, 128 tent placements per year, power 580 kW,
tent diameter 32 meters, tent plan area 780 square meters, exit area to cold traps 2 square
meters, leak area 0.2 square meters. For the 30 wt% case, the same production target needs only
370 kW, a 10-meter tent, and 10,000 square meters mined per year. This 2,450 MT water to 1,640
MT propellant plant-sizing figure is a separate quantity from the Executive Summary's 450 MT
near-term propellant demand figure; both use 2,450 MT of water as the input, but the Executive
Summary pairs it with 450 MT of propellant (a ratio of about 5.4 to 1, derived here, not stated
in the text) while Table 1 and later sections pair the same 2,450 MT water figure with 1,640 MT
of propellant (a ratio of about 1.5 to 1, derived here, consistent with the study's stated 8 to 1
water electrolysis mass ratio versus a roughly 5.35 to 1 engine LOX/LH2 mixture ratio). The
report does not reconcile these two propellant totals against each other within the text.

**Initial investment ($4 billion).** The Executive Summary states the initial investment for
this operation has been estimated at 4 billion dollars, described as "about the cost of a luxury
hotel in Las Vegas." The Economic Analysis section grounds this figure in its seven-scenario
model: lunar mining hardware is assumed to have a mass of 30,000 kg, costing 100,000 dollars per
kg to develop, plus 35,000 dollars per kg to launch from Earth to the lunar surface, yielding a
total initial investment of roughly 4 billion dollars (30,000 kg times 135,000 dollars per kg is
about 4.05 billion dollars, derived here). Annual operating costs are estimated at 20 million
dollars plus 800 kg of replacement parts per year (a figure the report states is comparable to
the International Space Station), over a stated 10-year hardware lifespan.

**Customer and demand breakdown.** The Demand section identifies distinct cislunar customer
segments with different propellant prices at the lunar surface, based on a 25% discount lunar
propellant is assumed to command relative to the cost of delivering propellant from Earth to the
same location: a lunar-surface and Near Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO) crewed lander customer
(100 MT per year demand at the lunar surface, priced at 7,500 dollars per kg); an Earth-Moon
Lagrange point 1 (EML1) customer modeled on Lockheed Martin's Mars Base Camp concept for Mars
departures (280 MT per year at the lunar surface, priced at 3,750 dollars per kg, derived from a
stated 140 MT per year demand at EML1 requiring transport from the Moon); and a Low Earth Orbit
(LEO) customer supporting reusable upper-stage refueling for LEO-to-GEO missions, assumed at
three super-heavy flights per year requiring 210 MT delivered to LEO, which is stated to require
1,260 MT of propellant produced at the lunar surface (1,880 MT of water mined) because of
propellant burned in transport, priced at 500 dollars per kg at the lunar surface. Table 11
tabulates propellant cost by location for propellant sourced from Earth versus the Moon: Earth
surface 1 dollar per kg; LEO 4,000 dollars per kg from Earth versus 3,000 dollars per kg from the
Moon; GTO 8,000 versus 1,500; GEO 16,000 versus 1,500; EML1 12,000 versus 1,000; lunar surface
36,000 versus 500 (all dollars per kg).

**Economic model and seven NPV scenarios.** A discounted cash flow model built in Excel
estimates NPV from the mining company's perspective, using a 10% discount rate, across seven
scenarios combining the three customer segments. Table 14 reports demand at the lunar surface,
average propellant price, annual revenue, NPV, and Rate of Return (ROR) for each: Scenario 1
(Moon only), 100 MT/yr, 7,500 dollars/kg, 750 million dollars revenue, negative 234 million
dollars NPV, 9% ROR. Scenario 2 (EML1 only), 280 MT/yr, 3,750 dollars/kg, 1,050 million dollars
revenue, positive 1,609 million dollars NPV, 19% ROR. Scenario 3 (LEO only), 1,260 MT/yr, 500
dollars/kg, 630 million dollars revenue, negative 972 million dollars NPV, 4% ROR. Scenario 4
(LEO plus Moon), 1,360 MT/yr, 1,015 dollars/kg average, 1,380 million dollars revenue, positive
3,639 million dollars NPV, 28% ROR. Scenario 5 (EML1 plus LEO), 1,540 MT/yr, 1,091 dollars/kg
average, 1,680 million dollars revenue, positive 5,481 million dollars NPV, 37% ROR. Scenario 6
(Moon plus EML1), 380 MT/yr, 4,737 dollars/kg average, 1,800 million dollars revenue, positive
6,218 million dollars NPV, 40% ROR. Scenario 7 (all customers), 1,640 MT/yr, 1,482 dollars/kg
average, 2,430 million dollars revenue, positive 10,092 million dollars NPV, 56% ROR. The report
notes the 2,430 million dollar revenue and 1,640 MT/yr demand figures in Scenario 7 numerically
match the Executive Summary's stated 2.4 billion dollar revenue figure and the Table 1
thermal-mining plant output of 1,640 MT propellant per year (both derived observations made
here, not stated as a cross-reference in the text itself), even though the Executive Summary's
headline near-term demand is separately stated as 450 MT of propellant.

**Power infrastructure hardware.** The report states the required 800 kW thermal or 2 MW
electrical power levels exceed the kilowatt-class reactors that have been NASA's recent focus
(such as the Kilopower project). Two private companies, Atomos Nuclear and Space and USNC, are
described as developing space reactors with power levels from 150 to 1,000 kW and a mass around
5,000 kg; at 30% conversion efficiency, such reactors would need to reject 300 to 2,000 kW
thermal, which the report notes could be radiated or redirected to thermal mining. For solar
power, the report assumes three deployable photovoltaic systems, each capable of delivering up
to 1 MW to a crater-floor substation, using 15 individual panels of 900 square meters each (a
total array area of 13,500 square meters, derived here), assuming 29% photovoltaic efficiency
from a Roll Out Solar Array (ROSA)-type deployable and a 54% power-beaming efficiency to support
the 2.8 MW total power requirement. Three photovoltaic technology options are compared: direct
PV at Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 7 to 9 with 34% solar-to-DC conversion efficiency,
concentrator PV at TRL 7 to 9 with up to 65% theoretical conversion efficiency at ten times solar
intensity, and solar dynamic (heat engine) systems at TRL 5 to 7 with 34% conversion efficiency
at ten times solar intensity.

**Prospecting approach.** The Prospecting section reviews orbital and in-situ evidence for polar
volatiles (Clementine bistatic radar, Lunar Prospector neutron data, LCROSS's 5.6% plus or minus
2.9% water-by-mass plume measurement at Cabeus crater, LOLA and LAMP reflectance data, LEND
water-equivalent-hydrogen mapping, and M3 spectral detections showing some ice-bearing pixels at
roughly 30 wt% ice intimately mixed with dry regolith). It defines siting criteria for future
missions using the terms Permanently Shadowed Region (PSR), Permanent Darkness (PD) within a
PSR, and Permanently Lit Area (PLA), recommending that water ice deposits be located within 10
km of a PLA so that power transmission from sunlit terrain is feasible. A strawman prospecting
rover payload is proposed: a drill or mole (the report references Honeybee Robotics' 16 kg
rotary-percussive TRIDENT drill, capable of penetrating to 2 meters at greater than 1 meter per
hour using 100 to 200 watts), sample handler, gas chromatograph/mass spectrometer, an oven to
bake soil and release volatiles, a neutron spectrometer, imaging LIDAR, and a multispectral
imager. The cancelled NASA Resource Prospector (RP) mission concept is cited as the design
reference, with its Neutron Spectrometer Subsystem (NSS) used to locate hydrogen-rich sites and
its Near InfraRed Volatiles Spectrometer Subsystem (NIRVSS) used to characterize sampled
material for water ice and hydrocarbon content.

**Thermal-mining mechanism.** Thermal mining, developed by Colorado School of Mines, applies heat
directly to the surface via concentrated sunlight or heating elements, or subsurface via
conducting rods or borehole heaters, sublimating ice into vapor that is captured by a
dome-shaped tent over the heated surface and vented into external cold traps where it refreezes.
Filled cold traps are removed and replaced, then transported to a central plant for purification
and electrolysis. Surface temperature for sublimation is set at a design point of 220 K, based on
cited findings that sublimation increases rapidly above 200 K and that 100 micrometer ice grains
lose mass rapidly above 170 K. The vapor transport and freezing process is modeled as an
effusion process, with tent pressure equilibrating at 10 to 30 Pa for a 1 square meter cold-trap
entrance area; vapor loss through tent leaks is targeted below 10% of the sublimation rate. The
entire operation is envisioned as tele-operated from Earth.

**Phased development architecture.** A recommended six-phase program is described: Phase 0
(establish business viability: propose demand and pricing, precursor prospecting, space law
development, company formation, financial modeling, secure investment); Phase I (individual
technology demonstrations, including regolith-simulant sublimation and reusable lander
development); Phase II (subscale terrestrial demonstration plant, including vacuum-chamber
integrated system testing); Phase III (subscale lunar production plant supporting robotic
exploration missions, becoming the seed for full-scale production); Phase IV (full-scale
commercial lunar production facility, sustained lunar presence); and Phase V (iterative system
enhancement using lunar-produced propellant to expand the facility itself).

### Limitations

The Executive Summary's near-term demand figure (450 MT of propellant from 2,450 MT of water)
and the body's thermal-mining plant-sizing figure (1,640 MT of propellant from the same 2,450 MT
of water, Table 1) are not reconciled anywhere in the text; the two propellant totals differ by
a factor of roughly 3.6, and the 2.4 billion dollar revenue figure in the Executive Summary
numerically matches the Scenario 7 all-customers case (2,430 million dollars, at 1,640 MT/yr),
not a scenario built around 450 MT of propellant. Readers using this report as a data source
should treat the 450 MT headline figure and the 1,640 MT plant-sizing and revenue-model figure
as two different numbers from two different parts of the same document rather than as
corroborating one another.

Table 13, Comparison of Ice Mining Options, which the text says compares the three CSM
extraction concepts (excavation, drilling, and thermal-mining tent) on mass, power, and cost, is
captioned in the extracted text but its data rows did not extract; no values from that table are
reported here. The same is true of Tables 16 through 20 in the historical ISRU cost-study
comparison discussion; only the surrounding narrative text extracted, not the tabulated
production levels, unit costs, or breakeven prices those tables are described as containing.

The report states multiple times that further precursor prospecting is required to confirm the
distribution, concentration, depth, and geomechanical properties of PSR ice deposits before a
production architecture can be finalized; existing orbital evidence is characterized as
indicative rather than a confirmed resource. Key processing hardware (the IHOP purification and
electrolysis subsystem, its electrolyzer stack) is reported at relatively low Technology
Readiness Levels (TRL 4 for the electrolyzer stack, TRL 4 to 5 for related water-extraction
subsystems), and the report itself frames Phases I through III of its recommended roadmap as
still-needed demonstration work before a full-scale plant could be built. The 70% solar
availability assumption underlying Figure 17's sublimation power curve, and the 25% price
discount assumed for lunar propellant relative to Earth-sourced propellant at each cislunar
location, are stated design assumptions rather than measured or contracted values. The economic
model is explicitly described as an estimate dependent on adjustable inputs (discount rate,
demand, price, investment, lifespan) rather than a finished business case; the authors note that
risk, financing source, timeline, and policy considerations would need further analysis. No
funding source or conflict-of-interest disclosure statement was found in the document.
