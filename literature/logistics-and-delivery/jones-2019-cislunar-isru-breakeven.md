# Cis-lunar ISRU Propellant Cost Breakeven

## Citation

Jones, C. A., Klovstad, J. J., Komar, D. R., & Judd, E. L. (2019). Cost breakeven analysis of cis-lunar ISRU for propellant. *AIAA SciTech 2019 Forum*. https://doi.org/10.2514/6.2019-1372

Publisher URL: https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/10.2514/6.2019-1372

## Abstract

Parametric cost model comparing six architectures for delivering hydrogen and oxygen propellant to a cis-lunar aggregation point, three of which launch the propellant from Earth and three of which produce it on the Moon by in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) and deliver it with a reusable lander. Propellant demand and duration are drawn from the Evolvable Mars Campaign, 59 tonnes per year for 14 years. Cost per kilogram of delivered propellant is computed for each architecture under baseline parameter values, then swept across sensitivity ranges for lander mass fraction, ISRU power plant specific mass, propellant plant specific mass, ISRU specific power, sparing rate, propellant demand, and campaign duration. Reports which architecture is cheapest at baseline, what single-parameter and joint-parameter improvements would be required for a lunar ISRU architecture to reach cost parity with Earth-launched propellant, and the time horizon at which lunar ISRU breaks even under baseline assumptions.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Human Mars missions in the Evolvable Mars Campaign require hundreds of tonnes of propellant in space, delivered across multiple launches because no single vehicle can carry the whole load. The 2018 confirmation of water ice at the lunar poles, combined with the lower launch energy needed to leave the Moon compared with Earth, had suggested that lunar-derived propellant could reduce the mass and cost of supplying that campaign. The paper states its research question directly: independent of any ongoing lunar mission, should lunar-derived propellant be used for human Mars missions, judged on cost rather than mass alone.

The paper positions itself against three prior studies. Ishimatsu et al. modeled lunar-derived propellant against NASA's Design Reference Architecture 5.0 and reported a 68 percent reduction in total launch mass to low Earth orbit once lunar transportation and ISRU infrastructure were deployed and operating, but did not account for the mass needed to deploy that infrastructure in the first place, since the model used lunar-manufactured propellant to build the very system that would produce it. A follow-on study by Ho corrected for that time dependency and found the mass savings fell to 22 percent, with several years of lead time required before the first Mars mission. Neither study addressed cost. Miller et al. proposed an Evolvable Lunar Architecture built on commercial lunar mining and a public-private partnership, which the present paper notes depends on that partnership existing and on significant human lunar infrastructure being deployed first. Kutter proposed a lunar propellant price of $3 million per tonne in low Earth orbit that would make a commercial launch business case work, while also stating in his own words that his analysis made no claim about whether mining and processing lunar propellant for $500,000 per tonne was itself economically feasible. The present paper's stated purpose is to close that gap with a parametric cost, not mass, comparison, using sensitivity analysis to identify which factors most affect the outcome.

### Methods and scope

Six architectures were defined by propellant origin, ISRU buildup strategy, and transport path to a cis-lunar aggregation point:

1. Propellant delivered from Earth by tanker(s) launched on SLS (legend label Earth-SLS).
2. Propellant delivered from Earth by tanker(s) launched on a commercial vehicle (legend label Earth-C).
3. Propellant delivered from the Moon by a single reusable lunar lander flying directly to cis-lunar aggregation, with the ISRU infrastructure deployed all at once, "All-Up" (legend label 1V-All).
4. Propellant delivered from the Moon by a reusable lunar lander to low lunar orbit (LLO), then a reusable in-space stage from LLO to cis-lunar aggregation, All-Up infrastructure deployment (legend label 2V-All).
5. Same transport path as Architecture 3 (lander direct to cis-lunar aggregation), with the ISRU infrastructure built up over time by "Bootstrapping": an initial small system produces enough propellant for the lander to retrieve and deliver additional ISRU plants from the aggregation point (legend label 1V-Boot).
6. Same transport path as Architecture 4 (lander to LLO, in-space stage to aggregation), with Bootstrapped infrastructure buildup (legend label 2V-Boot).

Baseline propellant demand was set at 59 tonnes per year of hydrogen and oxygen for 14 years, a figure the authors attribute to earlier Evolvable Mars Campaign analysis. SLS Block 2 was assumed for all lunar-infrastructure launches and for Architecture 1's propellant tankers, sized to deliver 45 tonnes to the cis-lunar aggregation point; a next-generation commercial heavy-lift vehicle was assumed for Architecture 2, sized to 15 tonnes to the same point. Delta-v was assumed at 640 m/s between the aggregation point and LLO and 1,870 m/s between LLO and the lunar surface. The reusable lunar lander and reusable in-space stage were sized by inert mass fraction, baselined at 0.26 for the lander (from the authors' assessment of the Altair concept) and 0.13 for the stage (from the Delta Cryogenic Second Stage). The surface ISRU system was modeled as a robotic, nuclear-powered, self-deploying and self-maintaining plant with zero boil-off storage and lossless transfer, with nuclear power specific mass baselined at 75 kg/kW from a Mason high-power design. Because the authors state that little data exists for integrated lunar ice mining and processing systems specifically, the excavation, plant, and power sizing parameters were instead taken from a molten regolith electrolysis system (Schreiner): 10 kg of excavator mass per tonne/year of propellant, 109 kg of plant mass per tonne/year, and 48 kW of ISRU power per tonne of ISRU system, with an assumed sparing rate of 10 percent of system mass per year over the 14-year operating life. Table 1 in the source lists all baseline values together with the ranges swept in sensitivity analysis; the full parameter and range list is reproduced in Key findings below.

Cost estimates for propellant tankers, the reusable lunar lander, and the reusable in-space stage were built with two parametric cost tools, the Project Cost Estimating Capability (PCEC) and Parametric Review of Information for Costing and Evaluation - Hardware (PRICE-H), with PCEC applied to the lander and stage and PRICE-H to the Earth-based tankers. Launch costs came from the Launch Services Provider Catalog for the commercial vehicle and a rough order of magnitude estimate for SLS, treated as a marginal cost against an assumed ongoing exploration campaign that covers SLS fixed costs; the authors checked sensitivity to this marginal SLS cost assumption and found it did not change the ordering of architectures. Lunar surface infrastructure costs were built from prior Constellation-program architecture studies. All costs are reported in fiscal year 2018 dollars. The cost model excluded flight and mission operations, technology advancement, spares hardware (addressed separately as a caveat, though the launch cost of spare mass was retained), and any Mars mission systems; reserves were included for all elements except launch vehicles. Learning-curve improvements were assessed separately and found not to change the architecture ordering, so they are excluded from the headline results.

### Key findings

The primary metric is cost per kilogram of propellant delivered to cis-lunar space, computed as total architecture cost divided by total propellant delivered over the 14-year horizon. Under baseline parameter values (Table 2 in the source):

| Architecture | Cost ($/kg) |
|---|---|
| 1 (SLS delivery from Earth) | 46,000 |
| 2 (Commercial delivery from Earth) | 40,000 |
| 3 (Lunar ISRU, All-Up, single lander) | 101,000 |
| 4 (Lunar ISRU, All-Up, lander plus in-space stage) | 80,000 |
| 5 (Lunar ISRU, Bootstrapped, single lander) | 78,000 |
| 6 (Lunar ISRU, Bootstrapped, lander plus in-space stage) | 202,000 |

Architecture 2 was the cheapest overall at $40,000/kg, followed by Architecture 1 at $46,000/kg. Architecture 5 was the least expensive lunar ISRU option at $78,000/kg. The paper states, in the Baseline Results section and again in its Conclusion, that lunar ISRU propellant was found to be 97 percent more expensive than Earth-based propellant, and frames this result explicitly against its own assumptions: "under the assumptions used in this study, which the authors see are favorable towards lunar ISRU, the most cost effective approach to deliver propellant to cis-lunar space is to launch it from Earth."

Architecture 6's high cost is attributed to the number of additional ISRU plants the bootstrapping approach required: 262 additional plants, each producing under 6 tonnes/year, to reach the 594 tonnes/year lunar surface production rate needed to meet 59 tonnes/year of cis-lunar demand. That buildup took 63 years from first ISRU delivery to reach full production and required 31 SLS flights over that time to deliver spares.

Table 3 in the source adds propellant efficiency, the ratio of usable cis-lunar propellant to total propellant produced on the Moon, and total SLS launches over the 14-year horizon, for all six architectures. Propellant efficiency for the four lunar ISRU architectures ranged from 10 percent (Architecture 6) to 19 percent (Architecture 4). SLS launch counts were 28 (Architecture 1), 0 (Architecture 2), 32 (Architecture 3), 22 (Architecture 4), 21 (Architecture 5), and 50 (Architecture 6).

Sensitivity analysis (Table 1's ranges, Figs. 5 through 8 in the source) tested each parameter individually against the cost-per-kilogram metric:
- Reusable lander inert mass fraction: even substantial improvement beyond the baseline 0.26 brought only Architecture 5 to cost parity with Earth launch, and only at an inert mass fraction of 0.21.
- Nuclear power system specific mass: baseline of 75 kg/kW is more efficient than more recent designs exceeding 125 kg/kW cited by the authors; a threefold improvement to 25 kg/kW was required for Architecture 5 to reach parity, and that parity is against the SLS-delivered Earth option only, not against the cheaper commercial-delivery option that the paper's headline comparison otherwise uses. Architecture 5 does not reach parity with the $40,000/kg commercial option anywhere in the plotted 25 to 125 kg/kW range.
- Propellant plant specific mass: baseline of 109 kg per tonne/year (from the molten regolith electrolysis proxy) would need to improve 2 to 3 times, to better than 50 kg per tonne/year, for lunar propellant to become competitive with the commercial Earth option.
- ISRU specific power: baseline of 48 kW per tonne of ISRU system would need to improve 2 to 5 times, to under 20 kW per tonne, for the same result.

Varying the time horizon (Fig. 9) put the baseline breakeven point, where lunar ISRU cost equals Earth-launched cost, at 35 years of the 59 tonne/year annual demand, corresponding to roughly seven Mars missions at the Evolvable Mars Campaign's cadence. That breakeven is a single crossing within the figure: the Architecture 5 trace against the Architecture 2 (commercial-delivery) trace, at about 34 years. The other three lunar ISRU architectures do not reach parity with commercial Earth launch anywhere inside the 40 years that Figure 9 plots. The authors note that including spares and replacement costs for the reusable landers and in-space stages, neither of which was modeled, would push this breakeven point further out. Varying annual propellant demand from 10 to 1,000 tonnes/year is reported to produce no crossover between lunar ISRU and Earth-based cost at any demand level, with the added cost of launching and deploying more ISRU capability said to always exceed the added cost of launching more Earth propellant; this claim is carried by a single sentence of prose in the source, with no accompanying figure or table of the underlying sweep. Varying the in-space stage inert mass fraction from 0.1 to 0.2 is likewise reported, in prose only, to show almost no sensitivity in cost per kilogram.

With sparing removed entirely (0 percent sparing rate, Table 4 in the source), Earth-based propellant was still cheaper than any of the four lunar ISRU architectures: Architecture 1 at 46,000 $/kg, Architecture 2 at 40,000, Architecture 3 at 66,000, Architecture 4 at 53,000, Architecture 5 at 47,000, and Architecture 6 at 72,000. Relative to their Table 2 baselines, this is a reduction of 35 percent for Architecture 3, 34 percent for Architecture 4, 40 percent for Architecture 5, and 64 percent for Architecture 6; the Earth architectures are unaffected because the sparing rate applies to lunar surface infrastructure. The authors state that adding spares hardware costs back in, which were never in the estimate to begin with, would make Earth-based propellant compare even more favorably, and identify including spares costs as a priority for future work.

A joint, equal-percentage sensitivity sweep (Fig. 10) varied four parameters together, reusable lander inert mass fraction, power plant specific mass, propellant plant specific mass, and ISRU specific power, and found that an 11 percent simultaneous improvement in all four, beyond the already favorable baseline values, was required for Architecture 5 to reach cost parity with Earth-supplied propellant.

In its Conclusion, the paper restates that for propellant requirements derived from the Evolvable Mars Campaign, lunar ISRU propellant is 97 percent more expensive than Earth-based propellant, and translates the cheapest Earth option, $40,000/kg commercial delivery, into a required lunar production cost: because cis-lunar propellant efficiency ranged 10 to 19 percent across the four lunar architectures, propellant would need to be produced on the Moon itself at $4,000 to $8,000/kg to trade favorably. This range is a derived requirement, obtained by dividing the commercial parity point by the propellant-efficiency range, and is not a cost estimate the paper makes for lunar surface production.

Figures 1 through 3 are concept-of-operations schematics rather than plots and carry no numeric values. Figure 1 lays out the two Earth-delivery architectures across bands for the lunar surface, LLO, cis-lunar aggregation, and Earth, showing SLS (Architecture 1) and a commercial vehicle (Architecture 2) each launching to the aggregation point. Figures 2 and 3 use the same band layout for the four lunar ISRU architectures: Figure 2 shows the two All-Up architectures (3 and 4), each with a deployment phase that lands the full ISRU system before an operations phase cycles propellant to the aggregation point; Figure 3 shows the two Bootstrapped architectures (5 and 6), which insert a third, buildup phase between an initial seed deployment and steady operations, in which the lander (and, for Architecture 6, the in-space stage) retrieves and lands additional ISRU plants using lunar-produced propellant.

Figures 4 through 10 are the cost plots underlying the results above. Every figure is a raster image with no embedded vector text, so the source text extraction returns nothing from inside any plot; the values reported in this summary from the figures are pixel-measured readings against the plotted gridlines, not printed values, carrying a stated uncertainty of about plus or minus $400/kg in Figure 5 and about plus or minus $700/kg in Figures 6 through 10. A check of Figure 4's six bars against Table 2 found the bars agree with the table to within that uncertainty, with Architecture 6's bar drawn to the plot's $200,000 ceiling because its tabulated $202,000 exceeds the plotted axis maximum, a drawing limit rather than a change in the underlying value.

One internal inconsistency in the source is worth recording for anyone reading this summary rather than the paper itself. The paper's twin statements of 97 percent are reproducible from its own unrounded model output, not from the two headline per-kilogram values it prints in Table 2. Every measured bar in Figure 4 reads 300 to 600 dollars below its corresponding Table 2 value, which means Table 2 itself rounds upward. Recomputing the ratio from Table 2's printed values, 78,000 over 40,000, gives 95 percent; recomputing it from the underlying, unrounded figure readings, about 77,700 over about 39,400, gives 97.2 percent, matching the paper's own figure. The 97 percent should therefore be quoted as the paper states it and not re-derived from Table 2, since deriving it from the table reproduces the 95 percent and would read as an error in the paper when it is in fact an artifact of the table's own rounding.

### Limitations

The authors state directly that the cost model excludes spares hardware costs for the reusable landers, in-space stages, and surface infrastructure, though the cost of launching the spare mass was retained; they call the exclusion of spares hardware a major caveat of the study. Table 4 quantifies the launch half of that exclusion: removing the sparing-driven launch mass entirely (the 0 percent sparing case) drops the four lunar ISRU architectures' costs by 34 to 64 percent relative to their Table 2 baselines, and even at that reduction every lunar architecture remains more expensive than both Earth options. Costs to replace the reusable landers, reusable in-space stages, and surface infrastructure over the campaign were not included at all; the authors state that including them would push the breakeven point further out. Zero boil-off propellant storage and lossless propellant transfer were assumed for both hydrogen and oxygen, in every architecture, on the surface and in space. The surface ISRU system was modeled as deploying, operating, and maintaining itself without physical human intervention, which removes any cost for crewed lunar infrastructure. The baseline nuclear power plant specific mass of 75 kg/kW is drawn from a high-power design the authors describe as more efficient than more recent designs exceeding 125 kg/kW, so the baseline sits at the favorable end of the published range before the sensitivity sweep is even run. Only the marginal cost of each SLS flight was charged, not any share of the fixed SLS program cost, on the assumption of a concurrent ongoing exploration campaign. Flight and mission operations costs and technology advancement costs were excluded from every architecture, and system reliability, including that of a reusable lunar lander over many flights, was not modeled at all; the authors list it among the factors left for future analysis. Because the authors state that little data exists in the literature for integrated, large-scale lunar ice mining and processing systems specifically, the excavation, plant, and power-sizing parameters for the ISRU system were instead drawn from a molten regolith electrolysis system, offered as a starting point and a capability target rather than as a prediction for ice-based ISRU. The paper twice describes its own assumption set in these terms, once in the Baseline Results section and again in its Conclusion, calling it favorable toward lunar ISRU even as the result comes out against it.

Multivariate sensitivity analysis was limited to a single joint sweep of four parameters at equal percentage change; the authors state that in-depth multivariate analysis was not conducted. The claim that breakeven time is insensitive to annual propellant demand, and the near-flat result for in-space stage inert mass fraction, are each supported only by a sentence of prose rather than by a published figure or table. The six architectures examined do not cover the full solution space; the authors note that commercial launch vehicles for lunar infrastructure, or an intermediate strategy between All-Up and Bootstrapped deployment, might improve lunar ISRU's cost performance but were not modeled here. The $4,000 to $8,000 per kilogram lunar-surface figure in the Conclusion is a derived requirement, obtained by dividing the $40,000 per kilogram commercial parity point by the 10 to 19 percent propellant-efficiency range in Table 3, not a cost estimate the paper makes for lunar production.

### Topic mapping

The Introduction and Previous Studies sections state the paper's research question and situate it against Ishimatsu et al., Ho, Miller et al., and Kutter. The Architectures section defines the six architectures and their concepts of operations (Figs. 1 through 3). The Element Sizing and Costing section carries the ground rules and assumptions, Table 1's full parameter and sensitivity-range list, and the PCEC/PRICE-H costing methodology. The Baseline Results section carries Fig. 4, Table 2's cost-per-kilogram results, and Table 3's propellant-efficiency and SLS-launch-count figures, along with the first statement of the 97 percent finding. The Sensitivity Results section carries Figs. 5 through 10, the single-parameter and joint-parameter sensitivity findings, the 35-year breakeven point, the demand-insensitivity claim, and Table 4's zero-sparing cost comparison. The Conclusion and Recommendations section restates the 97 percent finding, the $4,000 to $8,000/kg derived requirement, the 35-year breakeven, and closes with capability gaps the authors identify for future work: reusability, autonomy, resource prospecting, resource acquisition, and propellant production, plus system reliability and cost-estimate uncertainty as further analysis priorities.
