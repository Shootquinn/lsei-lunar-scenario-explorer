## Abstract

A trade-press research analysis published just after Starship's third integrated flight test
(IFT-3), which reached orbital velocity and, in Payload's framing, "largely validated"
Starship's capability as an expendable vehicle while full reusability remained unproven. The
piece tracks the IFT-1 through IFT-3 progression and then estimates Starship's internal cost per
kilogram in an expendable configuration. Its headline number is a **Payload estimate that
Starship V.1, at the SpaceX-stated roughly 200 metric tons (200,000 kilograms) to LEO
expendable, would have an internal cost of about $500 per kilogram to orbit**, derived from a
post-research-and-development full-stack build cost Payload estimates at below $100 million. For
comparison, the piece estimates Falcon 9's internal cost on an expendable basis at likely above
$2,000 per kilogram, and projects that a matured Starship V.3 (roughly 400 metric tons
expendable) could roughly halve the expendable internal cost again, toward about $250 per
kilogram. Every one of these is an internal-cost estimate, not a launch price or list price, and
each is labeled as Payload's own analysis.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The article is written in the immediate aftermath of IFT-3 (the week before the March 20, 2024
byline) and opens by declaring that "we have entered the Starship era." Its purpose is twofold:
to chronicle the fast-iteration flight-test progress of Starship, and to estimate what an
expendable Starship implies for the cost to reach orbit. The framing distinction that runs
through the piece is between Starship as an expendable vehicle (which IFT-3 largely
demonstrated, by reaching orbital velocity and coasting in space) and Starship as a fully
reusable vehicle (which was not yet demonstrated, as both the booster and the second stage were
lost on return during IFT-3). The economic argument is built entirely on the expendable case,
which the authors treat as the already-validated capability, while flagging that full
reusability is SpaceX's ultimate goal and would lower cost further.

### Methods and scope

This is an industry analyst piece, not a formal cost model or peer-reviewed study. Its cost
figures are Payload's own estimates, explicitly labeled "per Payload analysis" or "Payload
estimates," and are internal-cost estimates (what it costs SpaceX to build and fly), not launch
prices charged to customers and not published list prices. The build-cost logic is stated
qualitatively: Starship is built from inexpensive stainless steel rolls rather than carbon or
aluminum, cutting material cost; Raptor engines are produced at about one per day, bringing cost
per engine down to about $1 million (per Payload analysis); and building boosters and ships at
high rate spreads fixed costs. From these, the authors estimate that in the next couple of
years, post-research-and-development, SpaceX will be able to build each full-stack Starship for
below $100 million (against a stated potential Starship research-and-development spend of up to
$10 billion). Performance figures (roughly 200 tons to LEO expendable for V.1, roughly 400 tons
expendable for V.3) are attributed to SpaceX. The per-kilogram cost figures are then obtained by
dividing the estimated build cost by the stated expendable payload; the review notes that the
$500 per kilogram figure is consistent with below-$100-million divided by 200,000 kilograms,
though the article states the $500 figure directly rather than showing that division.

### Key findings

**Flight-test progression (context).** IFT-1 lasted about four minutes before ending in a
fireball; IFT-2 lasted about eight minutes, completed stage separation, and reached space; IFT-3
lasted about 49 minutes, reached orbital velocity, and coasted in space before being lost on
re-entry. The booster descended to about 462 meters above sea level before losing control at
about 1,111 kilometers per hour; the second stage lost roll control during re-entry. SpaceX
targeted IFT-4 in about six weeks (per COO Gwynne Shotwell), aiming to recover both stages that
year. The piece notes that nearly every other rocket is fully expendable, so attempting reuse is
treated as a bonus and the vehicle losses were not regarded by the FAA as significant anomalies.

**Expendable capability.** According to SpaceX, Starship is capable of hauling roughly 200 tons
to LEO in an expendable configuration, which the authors call the most capable rocket ever
developed; it would take nine expendable Falcon 9 flights to deliver the same payload volume as
one expendable Starship flight.

**Headline internal-cost estimate (Starship V.1 expendable).** The central number: **Payload
estimates that at Starship V.1's SpaceX-stated 200,000 kilograms to LEO in an expendable
configuration, Starship's internal cost to send a kilogram of payload to orbit would come in at
around $500.** This is explicitly an internal cost (not a launch price and not a list price) and
explicitly a Payload estimate (not SpaceX-stated). It rests on the estimated post-research-and-
development full-stack build cost of below $100 million; the review's own consistency check
(not stated verbatim in the article) is that below $100 million divided by 200,000 kilograms
equals about $500 per kilogram.

**Falcon 9 comparison (internal, expendable).** The authors state that SpaceX's internal cost to
launch **Falcon 9 on an expendable basis is likely above $2,000 per kilogram** (per Payload
analysis). This is the companion internal-cost anchor and is again an estimate, not a price. It
implies that even the expendable Starship internal cost (about $500 per kilogram) is already a
roughly fourfold improvement over Falcon 9 expendable internal cost, before reusability.

**Matured Starship V.3 projection.** SpaceX anticipates Starship V.3 (with Raptor 3 engines at
350 bar chamber pressure, about 20 percent more thrust) will carry roughly 200 tons fully
reusable and roughly 400 tons expendable. The authors state these "video game-type numbers"
would **cut the internal expendable cost to launch a kilogram to orbit in half again**, that is,
from about $500 per kilogram toward **about $250 per kilogram** on the same internal-cost,
expendable basis. This is a forward projection contingent on V.3 reaching its stated 400-ton
expendable performance.

**Full reusability (qualitative).** The piece notes that stopping at an expendable vehicle could
save SpaceX billions in research-and-development cost, but that full reusability remains the
goal because it would bring cost to orbit down even further. No specific fully-reusable
per-kilogram figure is given in this article; the fully-reusable, matured Starship cost range is
supplied elsewhere in this review by the separate Karen Jones (Aerospace Corporation) report
(about $67 to $233 per kilogram after multiple reuse cycles), which should not be conflated with
this article's expendable figures.

### Limitations

Every cost figure is a Payload analyst estimate, not a SpaceX-published number, not a customer
price, and not an independently audited cost; the article itself labels its proprietary
estimates as such. All the per-kilogram figures are **internal costs on an expendable basis**,
so they are not comparable to list prices or to the reusable-configuration numbers used
elsewhere; carrying the exact basis (internal versus list, expendable versus reusable) is
essential when placing them alongside Harry W. Jones's list-price rates or Karen Jones's matured
reusable rates. The build-cost estimate (below $100 million per full stack) is a forward
projection ("in the next couple of years, post-research-and-development"), not a current
demonstrated build cost, and the $250 per kilogram V.3 figure is a projection layered on top of
an unbuilt vehicle's stated performance. The payload masses (200 tons and 400 tons expendable)
are SpaceX-stated capabilities, and at the time of writing IFT-3 had reached orbital velocity
but had not delivered a payload or achieved recovery, so the expendable capability was
demonstrated in flight profile but not in operational payload delivery. As trade press, the
piece carries no formal uncertainty analysis, sensitivity bands, or methodology appendix.

### Topic mapping

FA7, FA5. Internal-cost rung on the launch-cost ladder. This source supplies the **Starship
expendable internal-cost anchor of about $500 per kilogram** (Payload estimate, internal cost,
Starship V.1 at the SpaceX-stated roughly 200 tons to LEO expendable), the companion **Falcon 9
expendable internal-cost estimate of likely above $2,000 per kilogram**, and a forward
projection that a matured V.3 (roughly 400 tons expendable) could roughly halve the expendable
internal cost again toward about $250 per kilogram. These internal-cost estimates sit between
Harry W. Jones's list-price rates (Falcon 9 about $2,940 per kilogram, Falcon Heavy about $1,520
to $2,350 per kilogram to LEO) and the aspirational Starship marginal-cost floor, illustrating
that the gap between list price and internal cost is the business model, not the physics. They
are the labeled-estimate, internal-basis rungs the review uses to bound the LEO launch-cost
floor from below, while keeping the $100 per kilogram figure flagged as aspirational rather than
sourced. Distinct from the Karen Jones (Aerospace Corporation) matured fully-reusable range
(about $67 to $233 per kilogram); this article addresses the expendable configuration only.
