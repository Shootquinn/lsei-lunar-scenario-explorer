# Diviner Observations of Lunar South Polar Cold Traps

## Abstract

Reports Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) Diviner Lunar Radiometer Experiment bolometric
surface-temperature maps of the lunar south polar region acquired 6 September-3 October 2009,
combined with a topography-resolving thermal model, to characterize the extent and stability of
surface and near-surface cold traps. Finds that cryogenic regions extend beyond the boundaries
of persistent shadow, reports measured temperatures down to 29 K, estimates subsurface
temperature at the LCROSS impact site near 38 K, and derives cold-trap areas of 13,087 km^2
(surface) and 119,507 km^2 (near-surface, to 1 m depth) for water ice in the south polar region.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The Moon's polar regions have long been considered candidate sites for cryogenic trapping of
water ice and other volatile species because permanently shadowed terrain there can remain cold
enough, over geologic time, to prevent sublimation loss. The Diviner Lunar Radiometer Experiment
aboard LRO has mapped lunar infrared emission since July 2009. This paper presents Diviner
south polar surface-temperature maps acquired just before the Lunar Crater Observation and
Sensing Satellite (LCROSS) impact in Cabeus Crater, as the Moon approached southern summer
solstice, and combines them with a thermal model to characterize the thermal environment of the
region. The stated objective is to use these measured and modeled temperatures to place
quantitative constraints on where, and to what depth, water ice and other volatile species can
be thermodynamically stable against sublimation loss in the lunar south polar region, and to
place the LCROSS impact site in that broader thermal context.

### Methods and scope

Diviner records infrared emission in seven spectral channels spanning 7.55 to 400 micrometers at
a spatial resolution of approximately 200 m. The reported south polar maps were obtained during
an LRO monthly mapping cycle, with observations acquired between 6 September and 3 October 2009.
Daytime maps were built from data acquired between 11.4 and 13.6 hours local time; nighttime maps
from data acquired between 21.41 and 1.66 hours local time. The primary measured quantity is
bolometric brightness temperature, the wavelength-integrated radiance summed across all seven
Diviner channels and expressed as the temperature of an equivalent blackbody; for unilluminated
regions this is treated as a reasonably accurate proxy for surface temperature, supported by
agreement among channels 7, 8, and 9 at the LCROSS site.

A thermal model was developed to extend the single-epoch Diviner snapshot in depth and in time.
It uses a roughly 500-m-scale triangular mesh built from south polar topography derived from the
Kaguya LALT laser altimeter, together with a spatially uniform set of thermal and reflectance
parameters generally consistent with prior studies. The model accounts for direct and indirect
(scattered and reradiated) solar and infrared heating from surrounding topography and computes,
among other outputs, annual average near-surface temperatures at 2 cm depth. Deeper temperatures
are extrapolated using a zero- and non-zero-heat-flow treatment, with the non-zero case scaled
using thermal parameters derived from the Apollo 15 and 17 surface heat-flow experiments. A
water-ice stability/loss criterion of 1 kg m^-2 per billion years (equivalent to 1 mm per billion
years of surface recession for a pure ice deposit) was applied to the model output to map the
depth at which ice would be lost to sublimation and diffusive migration through the regolith at
that rate or less. Analogous volatility temperatures (the temperature at which a pure solid
exposed to vacuum sublimates at 1 mm per billion years) were compiled for several other candidate
volatile species. Cold-trap areas were computed from the resulting model map. Results were
additionally compared with independently published Lunar Prospector Neutron Spectrometer (LPNS)
hydrogen-abundance data for the south polar regolith.

### Key findings

- Coldest measured temperatures: mid-day Diviner bolometric brightness temperatures as low as
  29 K were measured in intensely cold, localized regions on the floors of large shadowed impact
  craters, attributed to topographic shielding from indirect infrared heating by sunlit crater
  walls in addition to the absence of direct sunlight.
- LCROSS/Cabeus region: Diviner-measured summer-solstice surface bolometric brightness
  temperatures of 46.7 K (daytime) and 38.7 K (nighttime) were recorded in the region surrounding
  the LCROSS impact site. Model-calculated annual average temperature at that site at 2 cm depth
  is close to 38 K, and this is given as the paper's estimate of subsurface temperature at the
  impact site. Using Apollo 15/17-derived heat-flow parameters, temperatures at 2 m depth at the
  LCROSS site are estimated to be less than 6 K higher than the annual average surface
  temperature.
- Stability thresholds by species: water ice and cold-trapped volatiles are evaluated against a
  sublimation-loss threshold of <1 kg m^-2 per billion years (=1 mm per billion years for a pure
  ice layer). At the coldest south polar temperatures, cold-trapped water molecules are described
  as having "negligible mobility," limiting downward diffusion. Larger areas of the south polar
  region are stated to be capable of cold-trapping water and "less volatile" species (mercury,
  sodium) than are capable of cold-trapping water alone, because these species remain stable to
  sublimation at higher temperatures than water ice, aided by diffusive migration through the
  regolith. Conversely, only the coldest surface and subsurface areas are cold enough to trap
  "super volatile" species with higher volatility than water -- sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide,
  formaldehyde, ammonia, and methanol.
- Areal extent, surface vs. near-surface: the paper distinguishes cold-trap area at the surface
  from cold-trap area at depth, and explicitly states these are not coextensive with permanently
  shadowed terrain -- the mapped cryogenic regions "extend beyond the boundaries of persistent
  shadow." The total area of surface water-ice cold traps in the south polar region (Fig. 1D) is
  given as 13,087 km^2. The total area of near-surface water-ice cold traps to a depth of 1 m is
  given as 119,507 km^2 -- roughly a factor of 9 larger than the surface-only figure. Fig. 1D maps
  three classes of terrain: white regions where water ice can currently be cold-trapped at the
  surface; colored regions marking the upper surface of a modeled lunar ice "permafrost" boundary
  (subsurface, within the 1 m depth considered); and gray regions where subsurface temperatures
  are too warm to permit cold-trapping of water ice within 1 m of the surface. No separate,
  independently derived total permanently-shadowed-region area figure is given in the text for
  direct comparison; the paper's quantitative area figures are the modeled thermal cold-trap
  areas described above.
- Fraction of polar cap: surface and near-surface water-ice cold traps together are stated to
  comprise more than 66% of the surface area poleward of 85 degrees south latitude.
  Cryogenic (cold) regions in general are expected to extend to depths of "at least tens of
  meters" below the surface, but the paper states that estimating the volumetric extent of these
  cryogenic regions purely from surface-temperature observations is "highly uncertain."
- Instrument/method: Diviner, 7 infrared channels, 7.55-400 micrometer range, ~200 m spatial
  resolution, observations 6 September-3 October 2009 (near southern summer solstice); thermal
  model on a ~500-m triangular mesh using Kaguya LALT topography, with spatially uniform thermal
  and reflectance parameters, validated by histogram comparison against measured daytime and
  nighttime bolometric temperature distributions.
- Hydrogen/water-equivalent context: LPNS results give an average hydrogen abundance in the
  near-surface south polar regolith of ~70 parts per million (ppm) by weight, translating to a
  water-equivalent abundance of ~600 ppm by weight averaged over the broader region. If that
  hydrogen were instead assumed uniformly distributed only within the modeled cold traps, the
  implied water-equivalent abundance would be ~1000 ppm by weight -- substantially less than the
  1 to 10% water content independently inferred at the LCROSS impact site itself, which the
  authors interpret as evidence the LCROSS site is locally enriched relative to the average south
  polar near-surface cold trap.
- Obliquity history: the Moon's spin-pole is in a tidally damped Cassini State 2 configuration
  with present-day maximum spin-axis/ecliptic-normal angle (theta_max) of 1.54 degrees, having
  decreased over time as the Earth-Moon distance increased. Model annual average near-surface
  temperatures were also computed for theta_max = 4, 8, 12, and 16 degrees. The absolute timescale
  for this tidal evolution is stated as "highly uncertain," but the transition is considered
  likely to have occurred over a period of more than 1 billion years. When theta_max exceeded
  ~10 degrees, the floors of large impact craters were not the coldest terrain on the Moon (their
  relatively shallow walls did not shield floors from direct sunlight); the earliest surviving
  near-surface cold traps are inferred to lie on the floors of intermediate-sized, rather than
  large, craters.

### Limitations

The authors note that bolometric brightness temperature cannot be interpreted as a unique surface
temperature wherever a Diviner footprint contains sub-footprint-scale slopes, shadows, or rocks,
because individual channel brightness temperatures can then vary with wavelength; this is treated
as a lesser concern within cold, unilluminated regions based on multi-channel agreement. The
thermophysical properties of the Moon's cryogenic regolith are described as "not currently well
constrained" and could differ substantially from those of warmer regions, particularly at depth.
Estimating the volumetric extent of cryogenic regions from surface-temperature observations alone
is called "highly uncertain." The thermal model shows a notable discrepancy for daytime
temperatures in shadowed portions of craters (measured range 60-120 K), where the model
underestimates temperature by roughly 15 K, attributed to directionally anisotropic infrared
emission from rough sunlit crater walls that the model does not account for; this discrepancy
propagates to an estimated uncertainty of less than 7 K in annual-average 2-cm-depth temperatures
in the warmest craters (closer to zero in the coldest craters). The model itself uses spatially
uniform thermal and reflectance parameters rather than locally calibrated values. The absolute
timescale for lunar obliquity (Cassini State) evolution is explicitly flagged as highly
uncertain. The paper poses, but does not resolve from these data, the question of whether the
cold traps contain abundant deposits of nearly pure water ice comparable to those inferred from
radar observations of Mercury's polar craters.
