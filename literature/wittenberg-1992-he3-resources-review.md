## Abstract

Review of helium-3 (3He) resources and the means to acquire them for use as fuel in deuterium-helium-3 (D-3He) fusion reactors. The review argues that Earth-based 3He (natural gas, tritium decay) is sufficient for fusion research and development but not for a power industry, while the lunar regolith holds a solar-wind-deposited 3He inventory large enough to supply terrestrial fusion power for centuries. It covers the cosmological synthesis of 3He, terrestrial man-made and natural resources, lunar and planetary resources, and the mining, gas-purification, and isotopic-separation techniques proposed to recover lunar 3He.

---

### Background and objective

- The paper motivates fusion against projected 21st-century electricity demand: US electrical sales in 1990 were 2706 billion kWh (about 11 MWh per person per year for about 250 million people), and a projected 2050 world demand of about 5 MWh per person per year across 10 to 12 billion people gives roughly 6 TW(electric)-yr/yr.
- D-3He fusion is presented as having no radioactive fuel or primary fusion products (some neutrons and tritium arise only from side reactions), making it attractive on safety and environmental grounds.
- The stated objective: show that Earth-based 3He suffices for experimental reactor research and development, and that near-term extraterrestrial 3He resources can contribute to Earth's generating capacity for several centuries.

### Methods and scope

- The review is organized into four categories: cosmological synthesis of 3He and its isotopic ratio; Earth's terrestrial resources (man-made and natural); lunar and other planetary resources; and recovery techniques evaluated on economic, environmental, and legal criteria.
- 3He origin is traced to primordial nucleosynthesis, stellar nucleosynthesis, and cosmic-ray bombardment. The solar wind (principally protium, about 4 percent helium, velocity about 450 km/s, flux about 6 x 10^10 ion/m2-s, with a solar-wind 3He/4He ratio near 480 appm) is the mechanism that implants 3He into airless-body regolith.

### Key findings

- Earth resources are limited: helium from natural gas wells would supply less than 5 kg/yr of 3He at then-current usage rates; radioactive decay of tritium produced in fission reactors could yield about 110 kg of 3He by the year 2000 if retained; a large mantle inventory exists but is inaccessible. These are cast as sufficient for fusion research and development, not for a power industry.
- Lunar resources are large: the solar wind has deposited more than 1 million tonnes of 3He in the fine lunar regolith, confirmed by analyses of returned Apollo samples.
- A strong correlation exists between retained helium and the TiO2 content of the regolith, so high-titanium mare soils are the preferred extraction targets. Remote-sensing of high-titanium maria is used to locate rich sites.
- Surface photographs of Mare Tranquillitatis suggest nearly 50 percent of that mare may be minable and capable of supplying about 7100 tonnes of 3He.
- A mobile mining vehicle is proposed to excavate soil and release solar-wind gases; the evolved gases (H2, helium, CO2, H2O, N2) would be purified by permeators and cryogenic techniques, followed by helium isotopic separation. The review states the energy and financial payback of these operations is substantial when the fuel is used in a D-3He reactor on Earth, with minimal lunar environmental impact and within existing legal regimes.

### Limitations

- The review is a resource assessment and concept study from 1992; the mining vehicle, gas-recovery, and isotopic-separation systems are proposed rather than demonstrated at scale.
- D-3He fusion power itself is prospective; the paper acknowledges a sustained, reliable fusion reactor must still be developed and demonstrated.
- Lunar 3He inventory and minability figures rest on Apollo-sample helium-versus-TiO2 correlations extrapolated across the maria, and on surface photograph interpretation for Mare Tranquillitatis, rather than on in-situ mining data.

### Topic mapping

- The introduction sets the electricity-demand and D-3He safety rationale and states the four-part scope. The cosmological-synthesis section covers 3He origins and the solar-wind delivery mechanism and isotopic ratios. The terrestrial-resources section gives the natural-gas and tritium-decay figures. The lunar and planetary resources section gives the greater-than-1-million-tonne regolith inventory, the helium-TiO2 correlation, and the Mare Tranquillitatis 7100-tonne estimate. The acquisition section describes the mining vehicle, gas purification, isotopic separation, and the payback, environmental, and legal considerations.
