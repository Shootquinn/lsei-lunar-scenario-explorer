## Abstract

NASA's Moon to Mars Architecture Definition Document (ADD), Revision C, is the agency's annually-updated systems-engineering reference for its crewed Moon and Mars exploration campaign. It defines: four campaign segments (Human Lunar Return, Foundational Exploration, Sustained Lunar Evolution, Humans to Mars); 12 sub-architectures (including In-Situ Resource Utilization, Power Systems, Autonomous Systems and Robotics, and Communications and Positioning/Navigation/Timing); 15 architecture elements (spacecraft, landers, rovers, Gateway station, communications networks, the newly-added Lunar Nuclear Fission System); a full objective-to-use-case-to-function decomposition for both lunar and Mars operations; a prioritized catalog of 57 architecture-driven technology gaps; and an initial catalog of 25 architecture-driven data gaps (new in this revision). Rev C's headline additions are the lunar utility rover and lunar nuclear fission system elements and the data-gaps catalog. The document is solution-agnostic throughout: it states capability needs and performance targets, not prescribed technologies, and explicitly excludes the Human Lunar Return segment from its technology-gap catalog on the grounds that HLR capability is already funded through current programs. This summary's load-bearing content, assembled for a downstream technology-gap-to-capability-area traceability task, is the full 57-gap catalog: each gap's title, priority rank (1-57) and bin (1-6), native sub-architecture tag(s), segment(s), current state-of-the-art statement, and performance target.

### Background and objective

The Moon to Mars Architecture Definition Document (ADD) is NASA's annually-revised technical reference for its human deep-space exploration campaign, produced by the Exploration Systems Development Mission Directorate's Strategy and Architecture Office and reviewed each year at an Architecture Concept Review (ACR). The document states its own purpose and limits explicitly and repeatedly: it is a systems-engineering communication tool for commercial industry, international partners, other U.S. government organizations, and NASA's own implementing programs and projects, not a manifest, not a budget request, not procurement direction, and not a policy document. It "reflects the approved baseline for programs of record, contracts, partnerships, and funding" only after those are formally adopted; it does not propose or presuppose them (p.9-13).

The document is built on NASA's separately-published Moon to Mars Objectives (2022): 63 top-level exploration goals grouped into four categories (science, infrastructure, transportation and habitation, operations), further organized into ten goal-code groups used as ID prefixes throughout the decomposition tables: LPS (Lunar and Planetary Science), HS (Heliophysics Science), HBS (Human and Biological Science), PPS (Physics and Physical Science), SE (Science-Enabling), AS (Applied Science), LI (Lunar Infrastructure), MI (Mars Infrastructure), TH (Transportation and Habitation), OP (Operations); plus nine cross-cutting Recurring Tenets (RT-1 through RT-9). NASA's stated methodology is to "architect from the right" (start from long-term objectives, work backward to needed capabilities) and "execute from the left" (build via standard systems engineering, moving toward maturity) (p.13-19).

Revision C (dated per the revision table as both 2025-12-11 and 12/12/2025) is the fifth iteration since the ADD's initial 2023 release. Its own stated headline changes are: reorganized and simplified content; updated objective decomposition; added Mars use cases and functions for four science-objective categories; **added the lunar utility rover and lunar nuclear fission system elements**; added element-definition and pre-formulation methodology content; updated recurring-tenet assessments; renamed "architecture decisions" to "architecture definition"; refined architecture-driven technology gaps; and **added architecture-driven data gaps, a wholly new category in this revision** (p.3-4, p.9).

### Key findings

#### A. Document structure and revision history

The ADD has been revised five times since its initial 04/18/2023 release: Rev A (01/22/2024) added the ISRU, Robotics, Data Systems and Management, and Infrastructure sub-architectures; Rev A.1 (03/27/2024) made minor errata corrections; Rev B (12/13/2024) added Mars-specific objectives and elements and introduced the first architecture-driven technology gaps; Rev B.1 (04/04/2025) corrected unallocated-function listings; **Rev C** (this document) added the lunar utility rover and lunar nuclear fission system elements and the new architecture-driven data-gaps category.

#### B. The three pillars and the campaign rationale

NASA frames the value of human exploration around three pillars: Science, National Posture, and Inspiration (Appendix A.1), balanced rather than ranked. Science emphasizes solar-system-formation and volatile-distribution questions uniquely addressable via lunar and Martian fieldwork. National Posture emphasizes technology-spinoff precedent (the term "software engineering" originated in Apollo guidance-and-navigation work; food-safety standards and telemedicine trace to NASA long-duration-flight research) and a cited **$15 billion in private space-startup investment in a single year**, attributed substantially to NASA's commercial partnerships. Inspiration invokes the Apollo "Moonshot" metaphor as a template for tackling other hard national challenges.

#### C. The four campaign segments

1. **Human Lunar Return (HLR).** The Artemis I/II/III era: re-establishing human presence on and around the Moon after 50+ years, using existing and near-term elements (Orion, SLS, HLS, Gateway Crew-Capable Configuration, CLPS, xEVA, SCaN). Segment key science: South Pole chronology and composition (LPS-1/2), volatile and PSR characterization (LPS-3), solar-history-in-regolith and space-weather (HS-2), partial-gravity biology (HBS-1). Areas of future work flagged by the document itself: increasing utilization down-mass capability; accommodating extravehicular utilization payloads.
2. **Foundational Exploration (FE).** Expanded, longer-duration, more mobile missions; adds Human-Class Delivery Lander, Initial Surface Habitat, Lunar Nuclear Fission System, Lunar Surface Cargo Lander, Lunar Terrain Vehicle, Lunar Utility Rover, Pressurized Rover. Seven illustrative, non-binding reference missions are described: unpressurized mobility, pressurized mobility, robotic uncrewed operations, extended surface habitation, extended cislunar Gateway operations, cislunar-orbit-only missions, and non-polar lunar sorties. The document's own "Areas of Future Work" list for this segment explicitly names: establishing an external power augmentation strategy; **assessing the role of ISRU in lunar exploration**; incorporating waste-management and recycling approaches; and formulating a lunar orbital observation strategy (p.32); that is, ISRU and power augmentation are self-identified as open, unresolved questions for this very segment.
3. **Sustained Lunar Evolution (SLE).** Explicitly an "open canvas," with insufficient definition yet to allocate formal functions; narrative-only discussion of notional growth paths for science capability, economic opportunity (small-scale ISRU propellant production scaling to industrial scale; aggregate power scaling from kilowatts to megawatts), and population and duration (requiring power augmentation, e.g. nuclear fission, to handle lunar-year sunlight variation).
4. **Humans to Mars (HTM).** Initial human Mars presence; Mars-unique science (origins-of-life search, LPS-4); Mars infrastructure objectives explicitly include ISRU demonstration (MI-4); reference missions and element mappings are stated as forward work in this revision.

#### E. The fifteen architecture elements

Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS): 15 task orders, 7 providers, 60+ payloads to date. Exploration EVA System (xEVA): next-generation spacesuit, portable life support. Exploration Ground Systems: Kennedy Space Center launch and recovery infrastructure. **Gateway**: NRHO outpost with two configurations, Crew-Capable (Power and Propulsion Element, Habitation and Logistics Outpost/HALO, Lunar I-Hab, Gateway Logistics Element) and Expanded Capability (adds **Gateway External Robotic System / Canadarm3, provided by the Canadian Space Agency**; Lunar View, formerly ESPRIT; and Crew and Science Airlock, provided by the UAE's Mohammed Bin Rashid Space Centre). Human Landing System (HLS): commercial SpaceX Starship and Blue Origin Blue Moon variants. Human-Class Delivery Lander (HDL): large uncrewed cargo delivery. Initial Surface Habitat: 2-crew, 7-33-day capability. **Lunar Nuclear Fission System**: added in Rev C, external power augmentation explicitly linked in its own description to enabling "technology demonstrations with greater power needs (e.g., for in-situ resource utilization)" and to demonstrating the Mars-forward fission technology NASA has separately selected as the baseline Mars surface power source (implemented via NASA Glenn Research Center's Fission Surface Power program). Lunar Surface Cargo Lander: smaller-capacity delivery. Lunar Terrain Vehicle (LTV): unpressurized 2-crew rover, three competing commercial concepts shown (Astrolab FLEX, Intuitive Machines Moon RACER, Lunar Outpost Eagle). **Lunar Utility Rover**: added in Rev C, uncrewed, thousands-of-kg cargo capacity, multiple autonomy modes, described capability includes maintenance, repair, and servicing of other assets for a "continuous presence" role. Orion Spacecraft: primary Artemis crew vehicle. Pressurized Rover: **provided by Japan (JAXA and the Japanese automotive industry)**. Space Communications and Navigation Networks (SCaN): Near Space Network plus Deep Space Network, including the LCRNS commercial lunar relay project and the internationally-coordinated LunaNet framework. Space Launch System (SLS): super-heavy-lift launch vehicle, Block 1/1B/2 variants.

#### F. Technology-gap methodology (Section 3.2, Appendix D.1-D.2)

A technology gap is narrowly defined: "areas where technology development is required to close the gap between the current state of the art and the Moon to Mars Architecture's anticipated performance or capability targets" (p.77). An area needing further engineering work using **existing** technology is explicitly **not** a technology gap. Each gap entry (one templated page per gap, Appendix D.3, p.204-260) captures: title; four-digit ID (first two digits denote the primary sub-architecture); overall prioritization rating (rank, 1-57); priority bin (a coarser six-tier grouping); description; child gaps (an asterisk denotes a child gap shared across multiple parent gaps); architecture impacts and benefits; an architecture-traceability matrix linking to Key Definition Tasks (with a checkmark marking which are already completed) and to specific use-case/function IDs (rendered as a two-color grid: orange cells for Use Case IDs, green cells for Function IDs); current-state-of-the-art metrics and performance-target metrics, each keyed to a Moon or Mars icon distinguishing which destination the figure applies to; the segment(s) needing the capability; and the relevant sub-architecture(s). **The Human Lunar Return segment is categorically excluded from this catalog**: HLR technology needs are considered already addressed by current funded programs; all 57 gaps trace to Foundational Exploration, Sustained Lunar Evolution, and/or Humans to Mars.

Gaps are prioritized by four weighted metrics, combined and normalized via the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP), with weights in descending order of importance: **Criticality** (Wc, the degree to which closing the gap enables or enhances the architecture, from trade studies and the use-case/function decomposition) > **Urgency** (Wu, how soon investment is needed, capturing long-lead items) > **Breadth** (Wb, prevalence across sub-architectures; gaps mapped to more sub-architectures score higher, so cross-cutting gaps outrank single-application ones) > **Depth** (Wd, dependency on future architecture-definition-task outcomes). Cost and other implementation-specific metrics are explicitly excluded from the formula. The resulting ranks (1-57) group into six priority bins; even the lowest bin (bin 6) is stated to be "highly architecture driven." The document itself cautions that this list is "not encompassing of all research and development necessary" and "does not attempt to capture all outstanding risk"; it is a focused demand signal, not a comprehensive R&D roadmap. The prioritization is a yearly snapshot, not a fixed ranking.

Gap #0602 ("In-Situ Resource Identification, Characterization, and Mapping") was **retired in this revision and converted into five architecture-driven data gaps** (DN-006L, DN-007L, DN-008L, DN-010L, DN-013L, in Appendix E) rather than remaining a technology gap; the source states this explicitly. This is why the ISRU-prefix (06xx) block contains only 5 active gaps rather than 6.

#### H. Full state-of-the-art and performance-target text, by sub-architecture group (all 57 gaps)

Each entry: **ID, Title** (rank of 57, bin of 6): current state of the art; performance target; segments.

**C&PNT (01xx)**
- **0101, PNT for Lunar Surface Extreme Environments** (rank 20, bin 3): current relative-only PNT is insufficient for long traverses and charged-regolith conditions; SOTA is VIPER-project sensor development and Mars-rover relative-navigation heritage; target is absolute real-time localization to within 10 m (3-sigma) via 4-or-more-signal orbital-constellation infrastructure (GDOP under 6), with a degraded-mode fallback of 150 m for crew and 100 m for vehicles. Segments: FE, SLE.

#### K. Recurring tenets (Section 3.5), condensed

Nine cross-cutting tenets guide the architecture, each with a current-state assessment and forward-looking gaps: RT-1 (International Collaboration) catalogs extensive partner activity, including the Canadian Space Agency's Canadarm3, a Canadian astronaut on Artemis II, CSA participation in CLPS lunar science payloads and joint technical studies, and NASA's contribution of an infrared imager to CSA's own Lunar Exploration Accelerator Program (LEAP) rover (full Canadian-content detail consolidated in Section N below). RT-2 (Industry Collaboration) explicitly names future-collaboration target areas including power generation and distribution, autonomous robotic operations, and ISRU-derived-consumables provision. RT-3 (Crew Return) documents human-rating standards and flags validated-onboard-autonomy-during-communication-loss as an open gap. RT-4 (Crew Time) and RT-5 (Maintainability and Reuse) both flag automated and robotic maintenance as an unclosed architecture gap. RT-6 (Responsible Use) surveys the Outer Space Treaty, Artemis Accords, and U.S. space-sustainability policy. RT-7 (Interoperability) names a still-open gap in lunar-surface docking standards and notes the International External Robotic Interface Interoperability Standards currently cover microgravity only, not surface operation. RT-8 (Leverage Low-Earth Orbit) discusses the ISS-to-commercial-LEO-destination transition (through 2030) and names in-space food-growth research as a candidate LEO research area. RT-9 (Commerce and Space Development) frames power, communications/navigation/timing, autonomy (including AI), and resource exploration as the architecture's core "basis for economic activity beyond the agency," and calls for NASA to publish future power-level and communications-bandwidth demand estimates to help industry position itself.

#### L. Key architecture decisions relevant to capability areas

Among the completed key definition tasks (Appendix C.2.2), the most consequential for capability-area mapping are: **LD-03-L** (South Pole selected as initial landing region, explicitly for lighting stability and PSR-preserved volatiles relevant to ISRU, plus its analog value for Mars-polar-ice science); **LD-101** (commitment to lunar external power augmentation, without yet down-selecting technology); **LD-102** (Lunar Logistics Strategy); **LD-103** (2025 adoption of Space-to-Space Communications Systems, WiFi 6, and 3GPP/5G as the lunar surface communications technology stack); and **MD-07** (2024 selection of nuclear fission over photovoltaic-plus-storage as the primary Mars surface power generation technology, driven by loss-of-mission-risk mitigation; fission's robustness to landing-site variation, day/night cycle, and global dust storms outweighed solar's lower unit cost). Additional numbered Mars-segment KDTs from the Appendix C.2.3 table include MD-02 (Initial Human Mars Segment Target State, ACR25 down-select), MD-05 (Number of Crew to Mars Surface, ACR25 decision), and MD-12 (Maximum Allowable Crewed Communications Disruption, C&PNT category).

Among still-open definition tasks (Appendix C.2.3), several bear directly on the five capability areas discussed in the Topic mapping section below: **"Food Refrigeration and Growth"** and **"Source of Mars Crew Consumables"** (category 04/05, Human Systems and Habitation / Surface Systems and Infrastructure); **"Primary Mars In-Space Power Generation Technology"** (category 08, distinct from the already-decided Mars surface power technology); and **"Priorities for Crew vs. Robotic Tasks Strategy"** and **"Priorities for Earth Independent Crew Tasks"** (category 12, Robotics and Autonomy).

#### M. Terminology precision relevant to capability-area interpretation

The document's glossary (Appendix F.2) draws careful distinctions worth preserving in downstream analysis: **Automation** (mechanical/electronic devices replacing human labor, with human intervention available but not required) is distinct from **Autonomous System** (elements, potentially including humans, functioning together toward goals independent of external control), **Autonomy** (a system property: goal-achievement independent of external control, not precluding external reprioritization), and **Semi-Autonomous System** (partial autonomy, sharing control with an external operator for complete operation). Separately, **"Robotic Systems"** is explicitly scoped to exclude habitation/transportation systems and "disembodied autonomous systems that lack the capacity for physical interaction"; that is, per the document's own usage, "autonomous" and "robotic" are overlapping but not coextensive categories. This matters for capability-area mapping: not every Autonomous-Systems-tagged gap is robotics-relevant in a narrow sense (for example, gap 1004, Trustworthy Autonomy for Planning and Decision-making, is autonomy software, not embodied robotics).

A consistent **6-metric-ton threshold** distinguishes "large" from "small/moderate" assets and cargo throughout the document, confirmed across the glossary, quantity descriptors, and gap 0808's target. Sample-preservation temperature and depth tiers are also standardized: Cryogenic below -153 C / 120K, Frozen approximately -85 C, Refrigerated +4 C to -20 C; Near-Surface samples 1-2 m depth, Sub-Surface 2-10 m, Deep Sub-Surface 10-100 m.

#### O. Key tensions and cross-source discrepancies flagged for the downstream analyst

This section consolidates every point where the text pass and figure pass either flagged a document-internal inconsistency, or produced a different reading of the same data. Both kinds are collected here so the downstream analyst can resolve them deliberately rather than inherit a silently-picked answer.

**1. ISRU is simultaneously the most unimplemented and the least prioritized capability area.** Per Appendix B.5 (Section I above), every one of the ten defined ISRU use cases is unallocated to any current Foundational Exploration element, making ISRU the single most functionally unimplemented capability area in the architecture. Yet NASA's own criticality/urgency/breadth/depth-weighted prioritization ranks all six ISRU-prefix technology gaps (five active, one retired) in the bottom six of 57 positions, the lowest priority bin. Both facts are stated explicitly in the source; the source itself does not reconcile them, and this summary reports them without reconciliation.

**2. Power/ISRU narrative links are not reflected in formal gap tags.** Gaps 0901 and 0902 (Scalable Lunar/Mars Surface Power Generation) both state in their impact/benefit prose that unclosed power-generation capability constrains ISRU production objectives, and 0902 explicitly names the ISRU objective MI-04. Neither gap carries an ISRU sub-architecture tag in its footer; both are single-tagged to Power only in the consolidated table (Section G). This is a stated-but-untagged cross-capability-area dependency, agreed upon by both prior passes, not a disagreement between them.

**3. The Lunar Nuclear Fission System's formal function mapping is narrower than its own prose.** The element's description (Section E) explicitly links it to enabling higher-power ISRU technology demonstrations, but its Appendix B.3 formal function mapping lists only 3 functions (power generation, distribution, payload power at the South Pole), none tagged to ISRU. Relatedly, Appendix B.5 lists all four Power use cases as unallocated for Foundational Exploration notwithstanding this element's existence (Section I); the text pass flagged this appears to predate, or be inconsistent with, the element's own formal function mapping.

**4. The Lunar Utility Rover's formal function mapping is narrower than its own prose.** The element's description (Section E) discusses science operations and asset maintenance, repair, and servicing, but its formal function mapping lists only 6 functions (cargo repositioning, uncrewed mobility, payload/logistics manipulation, safeguards near crew).

**5. Disagreement on ISRU-gap cross-cutting breadth (0601, 0603, 0604, 0605).** The text pass's narrative (drawing on the per-gap card prose) states that gap 0605 (Lunar Regolith Excavation, Manipulation, and Transportation) is "the only cross-cutting bridge among the four lunar ISRU gaps," implying gaps 0601, 0603, and 0604 carry only the ISRU sub-architecture tag. The figure pass's pixel-verified reading of each gap card's Sub-Architecture footer icon row (Section G table) instead shows **0601, 0603, and 0604 each cross-tagged to ISRU plus Utilization** (a 2-way tag, not ISRU-only), and **0605 cross-tagged to ISRU plus Autonomous Systems and Robotics plus Mobility plus Utilization** (a 4-way tag). Under the figure pass's reading, all four lunar ISRU gaps are cross-tagged, not just 0605; 0605 is simply the broadest of the four, not the only non-ISRU-only one. This discrepancy directly affects the Breadth term of the priority-formula narrative and should be resolved by the downstream analyst against the actual page-204-260 card renders if the distinction matters to the capability-area mapping (marked with a dagger, †, in the Section G table).

**6. Similar Utilization-tag discrepancy on gaps 0502 and 0505.** The text pass's per-gap sub-architecture note for gap 0502 (In-situ Manufacturing of Spares, Repairs, and New Parts) lists "Infrastructure Support, ISRU Systems, Logistics Systems" (3-way); the figure pass's footer reading adds a fourth tag, Utilization. Likewise for gap 0505 (In-Situ Additive/Subtractive Construction): the text pass lists "Infrastructure Support, ISRU Systems" (2-way); the figure pass's footer reading adds Utilization, making it 3-way. Both are marked with a dagger in the Section G table. Given that the figure pass's method (direct pixel reads of the footer icon row) is the more reliable channel for this specific data point (the text pass work notes explicitly that this structure is pixel-only and lost in text extraction), the figure pass's broader tag set should likely be preferred, but this summary presents both readings rather than silently resolving in favor of either.

**7. Revision-date inconsistency.** The document's own revision table gives Revision C's date as both 2025-12-11 and 12/12/2025 (Metadata section above); this is internal to the source, not a disagreement between the two analysis passes, but is flagged since it could cause a downstream citation error if only one form is checked.

### Limitations

This is a self-described non-comprehensive planning artifact, not a requirements or budget document, and it says so repeatedly and explicitly. Its technology-gap catalog is an architecture-driven demand signal only; the document states it does not capture all necessary R&D or all outstanding risk. Its data-gap catalog is explicitly "a representative sample... neither comprehensive nor complete." Section O above consolidates every internal inconsistency and cross-pass discrepancy identified; those should be treated as open interpretive questions for the downstream analyst rather than resolved by this summary.

Many gap performance targets are explicitly marked TBD (value undetermined) or TBR (value stated but provisional) rather than final, notably gap 0604 (Metal Extraction)'s "TBR kg class" target and gap 0605 (Regolith Excavation)'s purely qualitative target, meaning two of the four core lunar ISRU technology gaps carry no committed numeric performance goal as of this revision. Gap 0808's "over 6 metric tons over a TBR distance" target and gap 0306's "TBR mass/volume/performance thresholds" are similarly incomplete.

Appendix B's exhaustive use-case and function tables (over 500 individual entries) were characterized structurally in both prior passes rather than reproduced in full; this synthesis inherits that scoping decision, since full reproduction was judged non-load-bearing for the capability-area mapping task relative to the 57-gap catalog, which is reproduced here in full. The nine non-ISRU, non-Autonomous-Robotics sub-architecture-prefix groups in Appendix B (Transportation, Ground, Habitation, Human Systems, Logistics, Mobility, C&PNT, Data Systems, Utilization) remain characterized only structurally, not itemized entry-by-entry, in the underlying text pass, and this synthesis does not add that missing detail (it was not present in either source file to merge). Figures, photographs, and embedded process diagrams that carry no traceability data (decorative section-divider photos, element renderings) were not analyzed for content beyond their location and caption; this is consistent with both prior passes' scoping and does not affect the gap-catalog completeness this summary is built to preserve.

### Topic mapping

**PROVISIONAL, NOT AUTHORITATIVE.** The mapping below was drafted during the text pass as a first-cut reference. It is included here for downstream convenience only. The authoritative technology-gap-to-capability-area mapping is a separate downstream analytical step, not the output of this intake summary; nothing below should be treated as final. Mapping is stated structurally and topically, not as a relevance or applicability judgment, consistent with this project's summary-best-practices stance. Canonical (a)-(h) definitions are quoted verbatim from `context/reference/CAPABILITY_AREAS_CANONICAL.md` per standing project instruction; they are not paraphrased.

**Autonomous Robots/Rovers.** Corresponds to the document's Autonomous Systems and Robotics sub-architecture and, jointly, the Mobility Systems sub-architecture (Section 2.2). Relevant technology gaps: 0501 (Robotic Inspection, Maintenance, and Repair), 0504 (Autonomous Lunar Surface Structure Assembly), 0804 (Robotic/Mobility Systems in Extreme Cold), 0805 (Autonomous Surface Mobility and Navigation, the 1,050 km/year FE-segment target), 0806 (Payload Offloading/Handling/Manipulation), 0807 (Docking and Berthing), 0808 (Relocation of Large Assets), 1001-1005 (the full Autonomous Systems and Robotics gap cluster: actuators/sensors/interfaces, autonomous monitoring, fault diagnosis, trustworthy autonomy, human-robot teaming). Element-level content: the Lunar Terrain Vehicle, Lunar Utility Rover, Pressurized Rover, and the Canadian-Space-Agency-provided Canadarm3 (Gateway External Robotic System). ISRU-excavation gap 0605 is cross-tagged to this area alongside ISRU Systems and Mobility Systems, making it the clearest single bridge gap between Autonomous Robots/Rovers and Mining and ISRU in the catalog (see the breadth discrepancy noted in Section O.5). The glossary's scoping distinction is relevant here: "Robotic Systems" excludes disembodied autonomous software and habitation/transportation systems, so not every Autonomous-Systems-tagged gap is robotics-relevant in a narrow sense (gap 1004, Trustworthy Autonomy for Planning and Decision-making, is autonomy software, not embodied robotics).

**Mining and ISRU.** Corresponds most directly to the document's In-Situ Resource Utilization Systems sub-architecture, whose own stated production-opportunity list (Section 2.2) is: "water, oxygen, and metals; human consumables and food production; feedstock for construction, manufacturing, and energy; and commodities for transportation vehicles, mobility systems, and propellant depots." All five active ISRU-prefix technology gaps (0601, 0603, 0604, 0605, 0606) plus the related 0502 (In-Situ Manufacturing) gap are directly relevant; see Section H for full catalog entries. See Section O.1 for the unreconciled ISRU-unimplemented-yet-deprioritized tension.

For the eight lettered CSA/SOW capability areas specifically:
- **(a) Resource prospecting and characterization**: *"Volatile detection and ground-truth measurement of ice concentration and stratigraphy before extraction architecture is committed."* Corresponds to gap 0602's replacement data gaps (DN-006L, DN-007L, DN-008L, DN-010L, DN-013L; excluded from this technology-gap-scoped mapping proper per Section J, but structurally the closest match) and, at the technology-gap tier, to the sensor/monitoring child gaps distributed across 0601, 0603, and 0604 (each ISRU gap's "sensors for monitoring ISRU processes" child).
- **(b) Excavation systems**: *"Subsurface access and regolith acquisition in PSR cryogenic conditions without direct solar power."* Corresponds most directly to gap **0605** (Lunar Regolith Excavation, Manipulation, and Transportation) and its child gaps (excavation of granular and hard/icy regolith, robotic regolith manipulation), and to gap **0804** (Robotic and Mobility Systems in Extreme Cold Environments, PSR-focused).
- **(c) Resource processing and extraction**: *"Thermal extraction of water from ice-bearing regolith across the battery-electric to fission power transition."* Corresponds to gaps **0601** (oxygen extraction, carbothermal-reduction-based) and **0603** (water recovery from regolith/ice, including PSR extraction as a child gap); the "battery-electric to fission power transition" phrasing connects structurally to gap 0901 (Scalable Lunar Surface Power Generation), whose child gaps include both battery/energy-storage and nuclear-power-generation options.
- **(d) Feedstock and consumables production**: *"Electrolysis of extracted water to produce O2 and H2 for life support and propellant applications."* No technology gap in the catalog specifically names water electrolysis for the Moon (the closest lunar analog, gap 0601, targets oxygen via carbothermal reduction of regolith, not water electrolysis); the Mars gap **0606**'s child gap for "long-duration water electrolysis for ISRU applications" is the document's one explicit electrolysis-technology child gap, scoped to Mars.
- **(e) Storage and transportation systems**: *"Cryogenic storage and surface transport of water, O2, and LH2 under PSR thermal and vacuum conditions."* Corresponds to gaps **1106** (Cryogenic Fluid Storage) and **1107** (Cryogenic Fluid Transfer), both Transportation-Systems-prefixed but cross-tagged to Infrastructure Support, and both explicitly scoped to Moon/Mars and to microgravity and partial-gravity environments including surface applications; also gap **0503** (In-Space and Surface Transfer of Earth-Storable Propellants) for non-cryogenic commodity transfer.
- **(f) In-situ construction and manufacturing**: *"Regolith-based construction and additive manufacturing for permanent infrastructure; deferred until sustained phase."* Corresponds directly to gaps **0504** (Autonomous Lunar Surface Structure Assembly and Construction) and **0505** (In-Situ Additive/Subtractive Construction on the Lunar Surface, explicitly using feedstock derived from in-situ regolith materials), and to gap **0502** (In-situ Manufacturing of Spares, Repairs, and New Parts, with an explicit child gap for manufacturing materials and components from ISRU-derived feedstock). The "deferred until sustained phase" framing in the canonical CSA definition is structurally consistent with these gaps' segment tags: 0504/0505 trace to FE and SLE; 0502 traces to SLE and HtM only, not FE.
- **(g) Recycling and waste management**: *"Closed-loop resource recovery, tailings disposal, mine waste management, and autonomous maintenance for sustained operations."* Corresponds to gap **0702** (Waste Management, explicitly covering resource recovery and repurposing from trash, including plastics, metals, water, and gases, via trash-to-gas, material sorting, and biological processing) and, for the "autonomous maintenance" component specifically, to gap **0501** (Robotic Inspection, Maintenance, and Repair). Per the project's standing capability-area guidance, (g) is industrial recycling and waste, not human life support; gap 0702's ISS/spacecraft-waste framing should be read through that industrial lens (mine tailings and closed-loop resource recovery) rather than as an ECLSS/life-support match, though the document itself frames 0702 primarily in habitat-waste terms. This is a genuine partial-match caveat, not a clean correspondence.
- **(h) Strategic propellant and consumables architecture**: *"Production of LO2 and LH2 at cislunar-relevant scale with interfaces compatible with NASA and ESA depot standards."* No single gap matches this precisely; the closest structural correspondences are gap **1104** (Mars Transportation Propulsion, whose child gaps include electric-propulsion-propellant transfer) and the combination of gap **0601** (oxygen production) with the document's Power Systems sub-architecture's "lunar power grid" and standardized bi-directional power interface concept (Section 2.2), plus RT-7's discussion of the International Space Power System Interoperability Standards. That is, the interoperability-standards machinery this area calls for exists in the document for power but is not stated for propellant depot interfaces specifically.

**Avionics and Communications.** Corresponds to the document's Communications and Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (C&PNT) sub-architecture and, for onboard computing and data-handling specifically, the Data Systems and Management sub-architecture. Relevant technology gaps: 0101, 0102, 0103 (the #5-overall-priority surface-to-surface communications gap, with its explicit 25-50 Mbps/10 km target), 0104, 0201 (Extreme Environment Avionics, #2 overall priority), 0202 (High-Performance Onboard Computing). Element-level content: Space Communications and Navigation Networks (SCaN, Near Space Network plus Deep Space Network plus LCRNS), the internationally-coordinated LunaNet Interoperability Specification, and the 2025 LD-103 decision naming Space-to-Space Communications Systems, WiFi 6, and 3GPP/5G as the lunar surface communications technology stack.

**Agriculture and Food Production.** No standalone sub-architecture; distributed across the ISRU Systems sub-architecture's "human consumables and food production" production-opportunity line and the Habitation Systems sub-architecture's nutrition function. The single most directly relevant technology gap is **0305** (Food and Nutrition Capabilities for Long-Duration Missions, ranked 9th of 57 overall), whose child gap explicitly names "food production for long-duration deep space missions" and whose current-state-of-the-art discussion cites the ISS's VEGGIE (Vegetable Production System) and Advanced Plant Habitat as food-production technology-demonstration precedent requiring further scale-up development. Related but more narrowly scoped content: Appendix B's bio-regenerative-ECLSS and plant-growth use cases are scoped to cislunar space, not the lunar surface, and are listed as unallocated functions for Foundational Exploration; the still-open "Food Refrigeration and Growth" architecture-definition task (Appendix C.2.3, category 04) and the RT-8 discussion of in-space food-growth research on future commercial LEO destinations are both forward-looking, not-yet-resolved threads rather than closed content.

**Power Generation and Distribution.** Corresponds directly to the document's Power Systems sub-architecture. Relevant technology gaps: **0901** (Scalable Lunar Surface Power Generation, ranked 13th overall, with explicit nuclear, solar, fuel-cell, and storage child gaps and the KRUSTY-2018 fission-ground-test SOTA citation), **0902** (Scalable Mars Surface Power Generation, tracing to the already-decided MD-07 fission-baseline architecture decision), and **0903** (Power Management and Distribution between Surface Elements, with its explicit 20 kW FE-segment inter-element target and its contrast against ISS's non-scalable tens-of-meters power-distribution baseline). Element-level content: the Rev-C-added Lunar Nuclear Fission System element (implemented via NASA Glenn Research Center's Fission Surface Power program, explicitly linked in its own description to enabling higher-power ISRU technology demonstrations), and the Power Systems sub-architecture's stated evolution path toward a standardized, bi-directional-interface "lunar power grid." As detailed in Section O.2, the gap catalog's own impact-statement language links unclosed power-generation gaps to unmet ISRU production objectives twice (gaps 0901 and 0902), even though neither gap is formally cross-tagged to the ISRU sub-architecture; this is a stated-but-untagged cross-capability-area dependency worth carrying into any Power/ISRU joint discussion.
